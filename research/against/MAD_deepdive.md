 What can be pieced together. By region: Australia 79.7% of revenue, North America 18.6%, rest of world 1.7%. By

 How miners treat this labour when prices fall: it is cut first at mines that close and kept elsewhere. The clearest case

 8. Labour and regulation
 Supply of mechanics. Mader has inducted 738 apprentices since 2019 and graduated 524. Apprentice intake fell
 from more than 190 in FY24 to 79 in FY25, which the company called timing [22]. Its internal transfer scheme moved
 more than 240 technicians between countries in FY26, mostly into North America [3]. Diesel mechanic is on the
 Government's core skills list for employer-sponsored visas, and the income threshold rose to A$79,423 on 1 July
 2026, far below what Mader pays, so it does not bind [30]. I did not retrieve sponsor-level visa data.

 Wages. Gross margin has risen in each of the last four half-years, so Mader has passed on wage rises in full and
 then some. I did not retrieve the official wage series.

 'Same Job, Same Pay'. This law lets the Fair Work Commission order a labour supplier to pay at least the host
 mine's agreement rates. There is an exemption for contractors providing a service rather than labour. The courts
 have read it narrowly: pricing by the hour and working under the host's direction pointed to labour supply even for
 BHP's own subsidiary [27]. Mader charges by the hour on customers' sites, so I would not rely on the exemption.
 Two things limit the damage. An order only sets a floor, and Mader's advertised rates look at or above
 mine-agreement rates. And the orders so far are at unionised coal mines in Queensland and New South Wales. I
 searched the Commission's published orders and found none naming a Mader company, but the search was not
 exhaustive. Treat this as a cost and paperwork risk, not a threat to the model.

 Payroll tax on contractors matters little because Mader employs its people directly. I did not research the Canadian
 and United States equivalents.

 Staff views. One job board shows 2.9 out of 5 from 11 ratings, split between five-star and one-star, with 81% rating
 pay as high or average; another shows 3.4 out of 5 from 75 North American reviews [31]. Recurring complaints are
 about communication and promises at hiring, not pay. A third-party write-up puts staff turnover near 30%; I could not
 verify it [35].

 10. The new plan and acquisitions
 What has been said: the aim is a 'globally diversified industrial conglomerate'; earnings per share growth of about
 15% a year for five years; margins held; capital spending of A$30m to A$50m a year before acquisitions; 'measured'
 debt; growth mostly organic, with purchases that are small ('acquire small, then scale hard') and described as
 springboards. Management said it 'probably could' reach 15% without buying anything, and that talks on two targets
 are advanced [3][4]. Bought so far: Wilson Hammond Group, a defence consultancy, on 23 July 2026 for A$2.25m
 plus up to A$3m later [1] p.92.

 The newer lines: infrastructure maintenance grew 45% in FY26 and ancillary services 37%; in FY25 infrastructure
 grew 30%, rail 34% and road transport 65% [4][7]. Their revenue in dollars and their margins are not disclosed.
 Since 93% of revenue is still mobile-equipment work, together they are small.

 Firepower: cash of perhaps A$60m to A$70m by mid 2027 (section 12), undrawn bank lines of A$118.7m, and a
 covenant allowing gross debt of 2.5 times EBITDA [1] note 17. Without new shares Mader could fund A$150m to
 A$250m; 'measured' suggests the lower end.

 The record of others, from general knowledge and not re-checked today: SRG and NRW bought service businesses
 close to what they knew and were rewarded; Perenti (Barminco, DDH1) and Downer (Spotless) bought large and
 earned poor returns for years; Programmed's purchase of Skilled Group in 2015 was followed by falling profit and a
 takeover. What I can show from today's numbers is where the acquirers sit: returns on capital of 8% to 18% against
 Mader's 25% (section 4). Even a successful purchase is likely to pull Mader's return down.

 My odds: 15% on a purchase above A$150m by August 2028, and about one in three that such a deal goes badly,
 so 5% overall. That is the core of my worse case.


 11. Control, the board, pay and dilution
 The board. After the AGM there will be three directors, all executives: Mr Mader, the chief executive Mr Nuich, and
 Mr Conway. Mader's own statement says none of its directors is independent, the chair is not independent, and both
 board committees are the whole board [11]. Mr Conway chairs the audit and risk committee and from 29 October is
 also interim finance chief, so the person preparing the accounts leads the committee that checks them. The
 company told shareholders in 2025 it would recruit an independent director once the new plan was set [22]. None
 has been named.

 Related-party dealings are small: Mader billed A$843,121 to Premium Plant Hire, a company of Mr Mader's; paid
 A$142,170 to another of his entities and A$132,715 to an aviation company linked to Mr Nuich. No loans, and no
 property leased from directors [1] p.50, note 26.

 The founder and a bid. He has said nothing on the record about taking Mader private; in 2025 he told shareholders
 he admires Gerry Harvey, who has kept Harvey Norman listed under family control for decades [22]. His actions
 point the same way: he sold A$61.5m of shares in 2024, started a buy-back, and is asking for a new listed share
 plan. Buying the 107 million shares he does not own at a 30% premium would cost about A$760m (107.2m x
 A$7.10). Debt of 3.5 times EBITDA covers about A$460m, so he would need a partner or Mr Burton to keep his
 shares. In a scheme of arrangement, a court-approved takeover needing 75% of the votes cast by the other holders,
 Mr Burton's stake is 30.7% of those shares, so he alone could block it. Nobody else can bid against a 49% holder.
 The nearest precedent is not encouraging: in 2023 the chairman of Pact Group, who held 50%, bid 68 cents against

 12. Dividends and the cash
 Franking credits (tax already paid that lets a dividend carry a tax credit) were A$13.3m at 30 June 2026, enough to
 frank A$31m of dividends, about 14.7 cents a share [1] note 9, and Australian tax payments add about A$25m a
 year on my estimate. Franking is no obstacle. The board says cash is being held for the five-year plan. Without a
 large purchase, I estimate net cash of about A$65m at June 2027 (35.7 + free cash flow of about 64 - leases 3 -
 buy-back 22 - small deals 7, with a range of A$55m to A$75m) and A$105m to A$125m at June 2028, about 55
 cents a share. A board with that much idle cash, a founder who took about A$9m a year in dividends until 2025, and
 no deal would find it hard to keep paying nothing. To count for you a dividend must be paid by 31 August 2028,
 which means a final for FY27 (paid October 2027) or an interim for FY28 (April 2028). I put 40% on at least one,
 worth 4 to 9 cents in total.

 15. The three inputs that matter most
 The chance that FY28 profit is below FY26's A$65.4m: 13%. FY27 is guided to at least A$72.5m by a company that
 has met or beaten every target for five years and whose profit margin has stayed between 6.3% and 6.8%. A fall
 needs profit about 20% below the path to A$81m. Mader has not had a down year since listing. The 13% covers a
 mining downturn that reaches the Pilbara, the share-plan charge, and a poor acquisition.

 The multiple in August 2028: 17.5 times FY28 profit after tax, the same as today (11.5 times EBIT). The history since
 2022 runs from 17 to 34 times and most peers are at 16 to 24 times forward, so there is room above. I do not
 assume a recovery because the reasons for the discount (board, overhang, conglomerate plan) will mostly still be
 there. Bad case 14 times, good case 21 times.

## The prompt

I'd like you to write the strongest honest case against buying shares in Saunders International Limited (ASX: SND) today, as an independent analyst. Another analyst has written the case for buying. You haven't seen it, and that's deliberate: I want your own view, worked from the evidence. Afterwards the two cases will be put side by side and argued out, so be specific enough that each of your points can be checked.

The setting:
- Today is 9 November 2026. Saunders last traded at A$0.725 on 6 November (no trade since; bid A$0.72, offer A$0.825), with 140.8 million shares on issue, a market value of about A$102m. At 30 June 2026 it had cash of A$29.8m and bank debt of A$9.5m, and owes an earn-out of up to A$11.85m (A$6.85m cash and A$5m in shares) for Aqua Metro in the first half of FY27.
- It's for a simulated portfolio. The position would be small (about A$2,250), bought to hold for up to three years, and judged on what it's worth by 31 August 2028, after the FY28 result. The shares trade about A$11,000 a day.
- The portfolio's rules only allow a buy if all of these hold:
  - the bad case is no more than 30% below today's price;
  - the middling case is above today's price by at least 1.5 times the bad case's fall, and never by less than 15%;
  - the expected gain after costs beats about 12.9% over the period (the cash rate plus 5% a year).

  A "special situation" (for example a holder building a stake toward a bid) skips the first two rules but still needs the third, with its bad case set at the value if nothing happens.

What I need from you:
1. What today's price assumes about Saunders' future. Work it out from the price, the net cash after the earn-out, FY26 (revenue A$305.6m, adjusted EBITDA A$9.8m, NPATA A$3.9m, with a weak first half and a much stronger second half), and the company's outlook for FY27 (revenue growth, and the second-half NPATA margin held through the year). Show the arithmetic.
2. The strongest reasons not to buy at A$0.725. Cover:
   - the record: the FY25 guidance cut and managing director's resignation (June 2025), the October 2025 warning, and the FY26 guidance cut (May 2026); whether the second half of FY26 is a recovery or a good half;
   - how thin the margins are (adjusted EBITDA 3.2% in FY26) and what one bad project does;
   - the Aqua Metro purchase: what it added, the goodwill, and the earn-out's cash and share dilution;
   - liquidity and the cost of getting in and out;
   - the holders: Ahrens Group (16.2%, a private South Australian construction group, buying since December 2024), Desmond Bryant (18.4%), NAOS (11.0%, a seller through 2025 and 2026), the Aqua Metro vendor (6.7%, out of escrow), Anacacia (6.6%, a seller) and Timothy Burnett (5.5%, a seller). Who might sell, and is a takeover by Ahrens likely or unlikely? Why might a strategic holder sit at 16% for years?
   - whether the federal fuel security package (2026-27 Budget) helps before 2028 at all;
   - anything else in the accounts that makes the business look better than it is.

   Weigh the obvious arguments for buying, and say why they're weaker than they look, where they are:
   - record work in hand of A$571.9m and a pipeline of A$5.1bn;
   - 42% of revenue from panels and programmes, and new water panels;
   - its first direct Defence head contract (A$23m, July 2026);
   - FY23 and FY24 net margins of about 4.5%;

3. Your bad, middling and good outcomes for the share price by 31 August 2028, including any dividends paid before then. Give each a chance (they should add to 100%), and say what has to happen in each. Then say plainly whether Saunders passes the rules above at A$0.725, both as an ordinary pick and as a special situation.
4. The price, or the event, that would change your mind.
5. Every fact with its source and link.

## The answer: the report (text extracted from SND_bear_case.pdf)

Saunders: recovery priced in, protection missing
Independent case against buying ASX:SND | 9 November 2026
Assessment to 31 August 2028 | All figures in Australian dollars

I would not buy under your rules at $0.725. Saunders is not obviously expensive if its recovery holds. The
objection is that another ordinary contracting setback could halve the equity value, while the quoted offer makes
entry materially dearer. Ahrens provides credible takeover optionality, but insufficient evidence of a transaction. My
probabilities are judgements, not statistical estimates.
What the price requires. I use your dated share price, share count, bid/offer and turnover as supplied market
inputs. The audited accounts show cash of $29.797m and total borrowings of $9.519m, including asset finance.
That is $20.278m net cash before the earn-out, rather than the presentation’s $22.3m headline. The full $11.85m
earn-out is already accrued, although final assessment is pending. [1,2]

This counts the share earn-out once, through dilution. Its actual issue price is the payment-date 30-day VWAP. At
$0.725, existing holders lose approximately 4.7% of their percentage ownership. The approved cap is 7.69m
shares; a VWAP below $0.65 can require additional cash or further approval. Lease liabilities are included in the
EBITDA multiple to match lease-adjusted accounting. No intervening cash movement is assumed. [1,3]
NPATA means adjusted profit after tax before amortisation. The recovery arithmetic is more encouraging. Published
H1 revenue was $143.557m, adjusted NPAT was minus $2.844m and acquisition-intangible amortisation was
$1.723m. Thus H1 NPATA was approximately minus $1.121m. Subtracting from FY26 gives H2 revenue of
$162.071m and NPATA of $4.985m: a 3.08% margin. Applying that margin to flat FY26 revenue gives $9.40m
NPATA, or $10.15m with illustrative 8% growth. Those imply 11.4× and 10.5× earnings respectively. Management
expects revenue growth and the H2 margin to hold in FY27; it has not promised that 8% growth rate. [1,4,5]
At an assumed 12× earnings, today’s diluted valuation requires $8.92m NPATA, equivalent to a 2.70% margin on
$330m revenue. The price requires a substantial recovery from FY26, but less than management’s indicated
margin. That makes a simple overvaluation argument weak.
A good half is not yet a dependable business. On 13 June 2025, adjusted EBITDA guidance fell from
$14m-$16m to $9m-$10m, alongside Mark Benson’s announced resignation. On 31 October, Saunders said the
same difficulties were continuing into H1 FY26. On 12 May 2026, revenue guidance fell from $315m-$345m to
$300m-$315m and EBITDA-margin guidance from 3.5%-4.5% to 3.0%-3.5%. [6,7,8]
The May explanation was specific: about $30m shifted into FY27 because of cultural-heritage discoveries and
Defence access/user-requirement changes. Saunders said project profitability was unaffected. That is plausible, but
idle capacity and delayed revenue still damaged group earnings. H2 adjusted EBITDA improved to $9.39m from
$0.37m in H1. I credit that recovery; I do not treat one half as proof that forecasting and utilisation risk have
disappeared. [1,4,8]

The accounts are less reassuring than adjusted profit suggests. FY26’s $3.864m NPATA reconciles from a
$0.935m statutory loss by adding $2.260m acquisition/integration expenses and $2.539m amortisation. Excluding
amortisation is defensible when assessing cash earnings, but neither acquisition spending nor integration costs are
free. Adjusted NPAT before that amortisation add-back fell from $3.243m to $1.325m. [1, p30]
More significantly, adjusted EBITDA retains a $2.848m gain on disposal of non-current assets. Removing it reduces
$9.762m to $6.914m, or 2.26% of revenue. The half-year report identifies a $4m property sale in August 2025, so
this concern chiefly weakens the annual headline, rather than explaining away the stronger second half. [1, notes
4,18; 4, p10]
Operating cash flow was minus $1.674m. Plant spending was $5.080m and lease repayments $1.856m: cash use
of $8.610m before asset sales, acquisitions and dividends. The increased cash balance came alongside $5.275m
share proceeds and $9.518m net borrowing. The cash-flow reconciliation shows $6.746m absorbed by receivables
and $5.732m by contract assets, partly offset by liabilities. These movements already adjust for acquired balances.
Revenue also depends on estimates of completion costs, variations and claims. None proves misstatement;
together they demand evidence of cash collection. [1, pp54,65,79]
The reported 3.2% EBITDA margin leaves little room for errors. An extra cost equal to 1% of FY26 revenue is
$3.06m, or 31% of reported adjusted EBITDA. At an illustrative 30% tax rate, that absorbs 55% of NPATA. A $5m
project loss would absorb about 91%. The FY23 and FY24 statutory net margins of 4.72% and 4.33% demonstrate
upside, but not a dependable floor. [1,9; calculations mine]
Aqua Metro improves the business but consumes the apparent cash cushion. It added water capabilities and
utility relationships; the new Water segment delivered $107.537m revenue and $10.177m EBITDA in FY26.
However, changed segment definitions prevent a clean organic-growth comparison. Group revenue growth of
42.5% is acquisition-driven, not evidence of equivalent underlying growth. [1, notes 3,22]
Total acquisition consideration is $32.275m, including a working-capital adjustment, against only $1.636m net
identified assets before goodwill and separately recognised customer intangibles. Goodwill is $22.939m and
customer intangibles initially $7.7m. This is a purchase of future earning power, not asset backing. The $6.85m
cash earn-out reduces post-debt cash to about 9.1 cents per enlarged share, and that cash must fund projects.
Impairment is not inevitable, but cash and goodwill do not establish a $0.725 valuation floor. [1, notes 20-22]
Visibility is real; guaranteed profitability is not. Work in hand of $571.9m equals 1.87 times FY26 revenue. The
claimed 8.1% growth uses a $529m comparator including $360m of acquired Aqua work. The $5.1bn pipeline is
opportunities, not signed revenue. Panels/programmes supplied 42% of revenue, improving repeat business, but
October’s four water arrangements explicitly guarantee no work volume. Their roughly $2bn of accessible
programmes is customers’ spending, not Saunders’ entitlement. [1, note 11; 2,10]
The $23m Butterworth Defence head contract is a genuine advance, contributing in FY27/FY28. AusTender
CN4271325 separately records a Saunders fuel-upgrade contract executed 15 July 2026, worth $26.109m, ending
September 2027. The register does not identify the site, so I neither assert an exact match nor count it as an
additional win. Head-contracting adds responsibility for the complete scope; success still depends on delivery
economics. [11,12]
Fuel security could help before August 2028. It should not be excluded altogether. However, the official proposal
starts reserve procurement/stock build from July 2027, targets full operation in 2030 and envisages government
owning fuel, not storage infrastructure. Storage co-investment includes refurbishment and expansion as well as new
tanks. Consultation closed in September 2026. I give no separate value to unawarded package work: the $3.2bn
reserve budget is not a tank-construction budget. [13,14]
The register supports optionality, not an imminent bid. Ahrens rose from 5.34% in December 2024 to 16.2% in
March 2026, about 16.1% on today’s enlarged denominator. Its water/steel capabilities and acquisition of SBS
Tanks make strategic fit credible. But I found no announced Saunders proposal. At 16%, it can retain exposure,
influence and the option to buy later without funding or integrating the whole company. The takeover rules do not
require it to bid simply because it owns this stake. [15,16,17,23]
Bryant’s 18.4% is another material voting bloc, with no demonstrated obligation to support Ahrens. Anacacia (6.6%)
and Burnett (5.5%) have sold shares. The Aqua vendor’s 6.7% came out of escrow on 30 June, creating sale
capacity, not evidence of selling. NAOS is an important correction: after earlier reductions, it bought 1.502m
shares on 28 August, increasing to 11.04%. Calling it simply a continuing seller is wrong. These holdings can
facilitate a negotiated transaction or create supply; neither outcome is assured. [1,18-22]

148m shares after the earn-out, no further material issuance, and value FY28 NPATA at the multiples below. Cash
is not added again to an equity earnings valuation. Dividends include only payments after purchase and before the

Bad means renewed delays/overruns and weak cash conversion, without insolvency. Middling means the recovery
broadly holds and water growth offsets uneven project timing. Good requires strong panel conversion, successful
Defence delivery and margins approaching historic levels. The 10×/12×/14× multiples reward increasing reliability;
they are valuation assumptions, not claimed peer quotes. The 35% bad-case weighting reflects repeated
disappointments and negative cash conversion. A worse outcome remains possible.
Expected total value is 35% × $0.319 + 50% × $0.891 + 15% × $1.584 = $0.795, a 9.6% gross gain. I allow 5%
against terminal share prices for exit liquidity, plus $10 total brokerage on $2,250. That gives approximately 4.0%
after costs. Even zero trading costs would not clear 12.9%.

Execution makes this worse. Your $2,250 is 20.5% of typical daily turnover. Paying $0.825 is 13.8% above the last
trade; immediate sale at $0.72 loses 12.7% before brokerage. At that offer, the same scenarios return
approximately minus 8.7% after costs. A patient $0.725 limit order might fill, but neither fill nor depth is assured. My
5% exit allowance is an assumption, not a guaranteed cost.
Special situation: also fails my return test. Assume a 10% chance of an executable $1.20 takeover plus $0.02
dividends, and 90% no deal. The no-deal value is the standalone distribution above: central $0.891 including
dividends, probability-weighted $0.795, with $0.319 downside. It does not become zero, or automatically retain the
takeover price, if nothing happens. Applying exit costs only to the no-deal branch gives an expected after-cost gain
of 10.3% at $0.725. Thus it fails even with the first two rules waived.
This conclusion is probability-sensitive: the same takeover payoff needs just over a 14% chance to pass at $0.725,
but over 38% at $0.825. Ten per cent is my judgement given strategic fit without a disclosed process, not a
measurable fact. Someone assigning a higher probability can reasonably reach a different special-situation
conclusion.
What changes my mind. With unchanged scenarios, an executable price around $0.45 passes all three rules; the
bad-case constraint binds at $0.319 / 70% = $0.456. Operationally, two further halves near 3% NPATA margins with
positive cash flow after plant spending and leases, plus earn-out settlement without unexpected dilution, would
justify reducing downside risk. For the special situation, a disclosed credible proposal or sale process would matter
far more than another small stake increase.

## The prompt

I'd like you to write the strongest honest case against buying shares in Mader Group Limited (ASX: MAD) today, as an independent analyst. Another analyst has written the case for buying. You haven't seen it, and that's deliberate: I want your own view, worked from the evidence. Afterwards the two cases will be put side by side and argued out, so be specific enough that each of your points can be checked.

The setting:
- Today is 9 November 2026. Mader last traded at A$5.46, with about 210.8 million shares on issue (211.4 million after an issue of staff shares on 2 October, less about 0.6 million bought back since). That's a market value of about A$1,151m. It reported net cash of A$35.7m at 30 June 2026.
- It's for a simulated portfolio. The position would be small (about A$2,250), bought to hold for up to three years, and judged on what it's worth by 31 August 2028, after the FY28 result.
- The portfolio's rules only allow a buy if all of these hold:
  - the bad case is no more than 30% below today's price;
  - the middling case is above today's price by at least 1.5 times the bad case's fall, and never by less than 15%;
  - the expected gain after costs beats about 12.9% over the period (the cash rate plus 5% a year).

What I need from you:
1. What today's price assumes about Mader's future. Work it out from the price, the cash, the company's own figures and its FY27 guidance (revenue of at least A$1.13bn, profit after tax of at least A$72.5m), and show the arithmetic.
2. The strongest reasons not to buy at A$5.46. Look for reasons growth may stall, may be weaker than it looks, or is already fully in the price. Cover:
   - how exposed Mader is to a slowdown in mining, especially Pilbara iron ore, and how mine operators treat contract maintenance labour when prices fall;
   - the slip in margins in FY26 (EBITDA margin from 12.5% to 12.1%, and 11.6% in the first half) and FY27 guidance for profit to grow more slowly than revenue;
   - the end of the dividend and the plan to become a "globally diversified industrial conglomerate", with an "opportunity warchest" for acquisitions;
   - control and governance: the founder is executive chairman with about half the shares; the finance chief stepped down on 29 October 2026; a long-serving non-executive director with about 15.7% of the shares, who has sold shares every year since 2022, leaves the board at the AGM on 24 November 2026;
   - the 7.965m shares issued to staff in October 2026 under the 2021 bonus scheme, and the new equity plan and performance rights put to the AGM;
   - labour supply and wages for heavy-diesel mechanics, and Mader's reliance on recruiting overseas;
   - North America, where revenue grew but EBITDA barely did;
   - anything in the accounts, such as receivables, capitalised costs or share-based pay, that makes the business look better than it is.

   Weigh the obvious arguments for buying, and say why they're weaker than they look, where they are:
   - net cash and a 25% return on capital;
   - a five-year record of beating profit targets;
   - a multiple below listed mining-services peers;
   - the buyback.
3. Your bad, middling and good outcomes for the share price by 31 August 2028, including any dividends paid before then. Give each a chance (they should add to 100%), and say what has to happen in each. Then say plainly whether Mader passes the three rules above at A$5.46.
4. The price, or the event, that would change your mind.
5. Every fact with its source and link.

Some ground rules:
- Use primary sources wherever you can:
  - Mader's ASX announcements, annual and half-year reports and results presentations (all at asx.com.au and announcements.asx.com.au);
  - the substantial holder notices and directors' interest notices;
  - peers' filings (Monadelphous, SRG Global, NRW, Perenti, Macmahon) and official data on mining activity and labour.

  Use broker notes and press only as pointers, and say when you have.
- Be fair. If the case against turns out to be weak at this price, say so. I'd rather have an honest "the bear case is thin" than a one-sided brief.
- Write in plain English, with figures in Australian dollars. Keep it to about 2,000 words, plus a sources list.

## The answer

**I would not buy Mader at A$5.46 under your portfolio rules.** The strongest case against buying concerns the combination of margin risk, dilution and a change in capital allocation. The case against the underlying operating business is considerably weaker: cash generation is sound, maintenance demand is resilient, and management has delivered growth.

[Download the fully sourced report](https://chatgpt.com/library?file_id=libfile_cd2922dd0fcc81918558fa6daef976a0&account_id=personal).

**What today’s price assumes**

Using your price and share count:

- Equity value: A$5.46 × 210.8m shares = **A$1,150.97m**.
- Enterprise value: A$1,150.97m − A$35.7m net cash = **A$1,115.27m**.
- Including A$5.25m lease liabilities gives **A$1,120.52m**. I use this lease-inclusive figure below. [1,2]

| Measure | Arithmetic, A$m unless stated | Result |
|---|---:|---:|
| FY26 price/earnings | 1,150.97 ÷ 65.373 | **17.6 times** |
| FY27 price/earnings at guidance floor | 1,150.97 ÷ 72.5 | **15.9 times** |
| FY27 earnings per current share | 72.5 ÷ 210.8 | **A$0.344** |
| FY26 enterprise value/revenue | 1,120.52 ÷ 1,001.14 | **1.12 times** |
| FY26 enterprise value/EBIT | 1,120.52 ÷ 95.015 | **11.8 times** |

The revenue multiple looks low, but a business retaining approximately 6.5 cents of each revenue dollar as net profit should not be valued like a high-margin software company. The earnings multiple is the more informative starting point.

At the FY27 guidance floors, revenue grows **12.9%**, profit grows **10.9%**, and net margin slips from **6.53% to 6.42%**. These are minimum guidance figures, not ceilings. [1,2]

A reverse valuation illustrates what investors need. Assume FY28 has 215m diluted shares, A$90m net cash after leases and an enterprise valuation of 11 times EBIT:

**EBIT needed to retain today’s price = (A$5.46 × 215m − A$90m) ÷ 11 = A$98.5m.**

To deliver your 12.9% return hurdle after 0.5% buying and selling costs, the required exit price is A$6.226. That requires **A$113.5m EBIT at 11 times**, but **A$138.7m at nine times**.

These are my assumptions, not market forecasts. Today’s price requires modest growth if the valuation holds, substantially more if it falls.

**Mining maintenance is resilient, but contractor earnings remain cyclical**

Australia produced A$797.7m, almost 80% of FY26 revenue. Mader classifies approximately 93% of group revenue at the pit/extraction stage. Its presentation charts Pilbara exposure, but does not disclose a precise FY26 iron-ore revenue percentage. Diversification remains incomplete. [2,3]

The government’s September outlook forecasts falling iron-ore prices as supply, including Simandou, increases. However, Australian output is expected to rise modestly over the next two years. A collapse in Pilbara production is therefore not a sound central assumption. [4] [www.industry.gov.au](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/resources-and-energy-quarterly-september-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

My concern is the contractor’s share of maintenance expenditure. When budgets tighten, miners can reduce overtime, defer non-essential work, redeploy their own mechanics and negotiate rates while continuing production. Flexible contract labour can absorb those adjustments.

There are offsets: essential maintenance continues, outsourcing avoids permanent headcount, and Mader’s casual workforce provides cost flexibility. The credible downside mechanism is weaker utilisation and pricing, rather than maintenance disappearing. [2,3; analysis]

**The margin warning is real, but needs qualification**

FY26 revenue increased 14.8%, while EBITDA increased 10.2%. EBITDA margin fell from 12.5% to 12.1%. Yet the first-half margin of 11.6% recovered to 12.5% in the second half. Gross margin improved from 18.8% to 19.5%, while administration expenses increased 24.5%. This suggests overhead and expansion costs, rather than demonstrated deterioration in job-level profitability. [1,3]

North America provides the sharper warning. Revenue increased 12.4% to A$186.6m, but EBITDA rose only approximately 3% to A$32.8m. Margin fell from approximately **19.2% to 17.6%**. Growth is not translating proportionately into profit. Constant-currency revenue growth was stronger at 17%, so exchange rates explain part of the reported slowdown. [1,3]

FY27 guidance includes A$3m–4m of profit impact from growth investment. Adding that back implies approximately 16%–17% profit growth. Consequently, slower guided profit growth does not itself establish structural weakness. It does mean investors are funding expansion before its returns are established. [1]

**Labour supply can constrain both growth and profitability**

Heavy-duty diesel technicians represent 52% of Mader’s workforce. Its global mobility programme deployed over 240 technicians to North America during FY26. Recruiting internationally is a competitive capability, but creates dependence on visas, relocation and retention. The deployment figure should not be mistaken for the proportion of employees requiring visas. [3]

Jobs and Skills Australia reports shortages across 48% of technician and trades occupations. ABS mining wages increased 3.5% over the year to June 2026. [5,6] [www.jobsandskills.gov.au](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-10/2026_osl_key_findings_report.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Mader’s employee benefits and superannuation expense totalled A$681.3m. An illustrative **1% increase that could not be passed through** would reduce profit by approximately **A$4.8m after 30% tax**, or 7.3% of FY26 profit. That is a sensitivity, not a forecast. [2; calculation]

**Shareholders are being asked to underwrite a different capital-allocation strategy**

No FY26 dividend was declared. Management’s ambition is a “globally diversified industrial conglomerate”, supported by an “opportunity warchest”. This is not a permanent commitment never to pay dividends, but it does prioritise reinvestment over distributions. [1,3]

The 25% reported return on invested capital deserves credit. It does not prove that acquired businesses, including the premiums paid for them, will produce equivalent returns. A successful organic recruitment and maintenance model is not automatically a successful acquisition model. [3; analysis]

Net cash is also less protective than it appears: A$35.7m represents only **3.1% of market value**, or **A$0.17 per share**. It provides flexibility, not a substantial share-price floor. [1; calculations]

**Control increases the importance of independent scrutiny**

Executive chairman Luke Mader’s approximately 103.8m shares represent about 49.2% of the current share count. That provides strong financial alignment and considerable influence. The company reports **no independent directors**. [2,7]

CFO Paul Hegarty stepped down on 29 October. Interim replacement Patrick Conway previously held both CEO and CFO roles, so there is continuity. The departure is not evidence of accounting trouble. Craig Burton, holding approximately 15.7%, retires at the 24 November AGM. These changes make succession and oversight especially relevant during expansion. [8–10]

One premise requires correction: **“Burton has sold shares every year since 2022” is not supported.** The FY23 and FY24 reports show his 39m holding unchanged. His September 2025 notice confirms a remaining 33.018m shares after further sales. Repeated selling is real; an imminent sale following retirement remains speculation. [10–12]

**Dilution complicates the growth story**

October’s 7.965m staff shares increased the share count by 3.9%, diluting existing ownership by 3.8%. They came from the old scheme and were already reflected in FY26 diluted EPS of A$0.3096, compared with basic EPS of A$0.3218. Treating them as a wholly unexpected new expense would be misleading. [2,13]

The AGM seeks authority for another 10.571m incentive securities over three years, plus separate approval for 1.5m CEO rights. Authority is not an immediate issue.

However, the CEO’s 400,000 FY29 and 800,000 FY31 profit-linked rights have targets to be determined only at the end of FY28 and FY30 respectively. Investors cannot assess those hurdles now. Positively, acquired profits are excluded from the tests. [14]

The remaining 300,000 rights depend on strategic objectives. The notice estimates A$9.32m total compensation expense for the proposed CEO grant, including approximately A$2.2m annually in FY27–FY29, subject to grant-date inputs. Market purchases or cash settlement can avoid issuing shares, but consume cash. [14]

**The buyback is helpful, not decisive**

By 6 November, 609,526 shares had been repurchased for A$3.43m. Even the full discretionary A$30m programme buys only **5.49m shares at A$5.46**, fewer than October’s 7.965m issue. The programme is not a price guarantee or an obligation to complete every purchase. [13,15,16]

**The accounts weaken an aggressive bear case**

Operating cash flow of A$85.0m, less A$27.4m cash capital expenditure and A$2.6m lease principal, leaves approximately **A$54.9m**, a 4.8% equity cash yield. Receivables increased about 12%, slower than revenue, and reported debtor days improved to 67. Those are healthy signs. [2,3]

Items to watch include contract assets, up 23% to A$19.6m, and receivables more than 91 days overdue, increasing from A$3.7m to A$7.0m. These do not establish aggressive recognition. Nor did I find a material software-capitalisation concern: the principal capital asset is the operating fleet. Share-based compensation was expensed at A$1.98m, although eventual dilution still matters. [2]

**The delivery record and peer discount are genuine positives**

Published strategic targets and rounded actual profits, in A$m, were **24/28, 32/39, 40/50, 51/57 and 65/65.4** for FY22–FY26. These are strategic targets, not a reconstruction of every guidance revision. The record supports confidence in the existing business more strongly than in future acquisitions. [1,3]

The new plan targets approximately 15% annual EPS growth, maintaining margins while investing A$30m–50m annually. Execution must reconcile those competing demands. [3]

These differing definitions make the figures cross-checks, not a like-for-like ranking. [17–21] [eoddata.com](https://eoddata.com/stockquote/ASX/MND.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Mader’s discounts to Monadelphous and SRG weaken the overvaluation argument. However, Monadelphous delivered 52% profit growth. Perenti and Macmahon carry substantial fleets and net debt, respectively A$271m and A$111.1m. A sector average mixes different capital needs and risks. [17,19,22]

**Outcomes by 31 August 2028**

These are my subjective scenarios. Each assumes 215m diluted shares, allowing for future awards net of buybacks, and no dividends after purchase.

**Equity value = EBIT × enterprise multiple + net cash after leases.**

| FY28 assumption | Bad: **25%** | Middling: **55%** | Good: **20%** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | A$1.10bn | A$1.29bn | A$1.45bn |
| EBIT | A$80m | A$120m | A$145m |
| EBIT margin | 7.3% | 9.3% | 10.0% |
| Enterprise value/EBIT | 9× | 11× | 13× |
| Net cash after leases | A$25m | A$90m | A$130m |
| Equity value | A$745m | A$1,410m | A$2,015m |
| **Share price** | **A$3.47** | **A$6.56** | **A$9.37** |
| Dividends | Nil | Nil | Nil |
| **Return before costs** | **−36.5%** | **+20.1%** | **+71.7%** |

The bad case combines weaker utilisation, unrecovered wages, disappointing expansion and a lower valuation. The middle case broadly delivers growth with stable margins. The good case requires stronger growth, North American margin recovery and disciplined investment.

The cash assumptions allow cumulative FY27–FY28 free cash flow after capital expenditure and lease payments of A$55m/A$120m/A$150m, the full A$30m buyback, illustrative acquisitions costing A$30m/A$30m/A$20m, and approximately A$6m ending lease debt. These acquisition allowances are assumptions, not announced transactions.

| Portfolio rule | Result | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Bad-case loss no more than 30% | **36.5% loss** | **Fail** |
| Middling upside ≥1.5× downside, minimum 15% | **20.1%**, versus **54.8% required** | **Fail** |
| Expected gain after costs above 12.9% | **15.1%** | **Pass** |

Expected terminal value is:

**25% × A$3.465 + 55% × A$6.558 + 20% × A$9.372 = A$6.348.**

After 0.5% entry and exit costs, expected return is 15.1%. Moving just three percentage points of probability from good to bad reduces it to 11.9%.

**What would change my mind**

I would reconsider around **A$4.60**, provided these fundamentals remained intact. The middle/downside condition binds at approximately A$4.70 before costs, or A$4.66 with the same dealing costs.

At a higher price, I would need better evidence: FY27 EBIT margin near or above 9.5%, recovering North American margins, cash generation after growth expenditure, independently scrutinised acquisitions and meaningful disclosed incentive hurdles.

The operating bear case is limited. The plausible downside and uncertainty over capital allocation are what make Mader fail your rules.

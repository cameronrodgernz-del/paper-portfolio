Corrections to the fact sheet, for the red team:
1. The A$8.3m is not "the original business's EBITDA less leases and sustaining spending". It is the company's group "pro forma free cash flow": pro forma cash from operations of A$40.0m (pro forma EBITDA A$46.1m less A$5.0m of interest, A$0.5m of tax and A$0.6m of working capital), less A$18.1m of sustaining capex and A$13.6m of lease repayments, and it includes Riverside's June quarter [B2, slide 14]. Core EBITDA happens also to be A$40.0m. Worked properly, the original business kept about A$10.4m after leases and sustaining spending in FY26, or A$8.7m after lease interest as well (estimate, because Riverside's quarter of capex and leases isn't split out).
2. Sustaining spending was about 61% of core EBITDA after leases, not 68%: the A$18.1m includes about A$1.9m of Riverside's June quarter (A$7.5m a year over four) [B2, B21].
3. The IPO prospectus forecast FY24, not FY25. It has no FY25 forecast. FY24 pro forma EBITDA came in at A$41.3m against A$38.8m forecast, but the beat was the one-off Thevenard Island decommissioning job (A$6.0m against A$3.2m); the core was A$35.3m against A$35.6m [B14, B15, B16].
4. The 7.21 times is struck on the A$122.6m headline price. Riverside came with A$10.1m of cash, so the net price was A$112.5m, or 6.6 times cash earnings (6.8 times after Riverside's own leases) [B1, note 21]. Riverside has no vessel leases, but it does have A$4.3m of property lease liabilities.
5. "If it goes our way" annualises the best quarter. Core revenue of A$63.7m in 1Q27 was up only 3% on 1Q26 (A$61.6m); the other three quarters of FY26 averaged A$53.1m [B27, B2].
6. The company's A$74.5m net debt counts the A$12.0m BM Fleet loan as cash. The prospectus described the whole loan (then A$10.8m) as a vessel loan bearing 8% "payable monthly in arrears" [B16, section 9.6.4.4]; the accounts show A$6.65m of it came from selling a 50% joint venture stake to BM Fleet at book value, bears no interest, and that the interest on the rest is added to the balance rather than paid [B13, B17, B1 note 22]. See "The BM Fleet loan".
7. Velrosso's 26.8m shares were almost certainly bought on market on 12 May 2026, the day of the trading update, and settled on 14 May (the date on the notice). See "Holders and dealings".
8. A$43.5m of the A$197.4m vessel value, and of the A$119.3m "at cost" figure, is Riverside's vessels at their March 2026 fair value. For the original fleet it is about A$154m at the valuer's replacement cost against about A$76m at cost [B1, note 12].
9. The FY26 restatement made the FY25 balance sheet worse, not better: deferred tax liabilities rose by A$2.9m and equity fell by A$2.9m, though the note's own words say the liabilities had been "overstated" [B1, note 29].

## The company in plain words
Bhagwan Marine runs workboats, tugs, landing craft, barges, multicats, dive support and crew transfer vessels around Australia: more than 70 owned vessels and more leased ones (26 leased at the prospectus), crews and up to 200 divers, working for offshore oil and gas, ports and inshore construction, and defence [B2, B16]. It earns day rates and project fees. About 56% of FY26 revenue was recurring (spot work and contracts over a year) [B2]. The founder, Loui Kannikoski, started it in Geraldton in 2000 with one vessel and is managing director; the chair, Anthony Wooles, has been chair since 2012 [B36, B27]. It listed on 30 July 2024, raising A$80m at A$0.63 a share [B36].

On 31 March 2026 it bought Riverside Marine from the Campbell family: Rivtow harbour towage (21 tugs it manages for miners in the Pilbara and at Mackay), management of two research vessels for the Australian Institute of Marine Science, Riverside Industrial Sands (dredged construction sand in Brisbane), Magnetic Island Ferries and Riverside Oceanic charters. Riverside owns nine vessels and manages about 20 more [B20, B21].

## Why big investors may be overlooking it
- Size and liquidity: a A$105m company trading a median A$29,910 a day (Checks).
- Index: added to the S&P/ASX 300 from 24 March 2025 and removed from 22 September 2025 [B34], so index funds have sold.
- A tight register: four holders own about 62%, and 12% of the shares are in escrow until March 2027 and March 2028 [B1].
- A recent listing that has gone badly: IPO at A$0.63 (July 2024), placement at A$0.41 (February 2026), now A$0.265.
- A sector out of favour: oil and gas was 66% of revenue before Riverside and is about 53% after it [B21].
- Analyst coverage: Euroz Hartleys led the IPO and the placement (with Shaw and Partners) and advised on Riverside [B20]. I found no other coverage in the filings and did not search for broker research.
Some of this is real neglect. Much of it, as below, is the market seeing the cash earnings and the governance clearly.

### Normal sustaining spending for this fleet
- What it has spent: A$12.1m in FY24 (A$13.6m less A$1.6m of asset sales), A$17.9m in FY25 (A$11.9m sustaining, A$6.9m "discretionary", less A$0.9m of sales), about A$16.2m in FY26 for the original fleet [B8, B2]. The FY24 deck warned FY25 capex would rise for a major docking and cost inflation [B15]; the 10-year docking of the Dryden, the largest vessel, cost A$6.7m in 1H25, A$3.9m of it sustaining [B10].
- Against revenue: 4.5% (FY24), 6.3% (FY25) and 7.3% (FY26). Svitzer, the towage group, spent 7.3% of 2024 revenue on dry-docking and other upkeep (DKK 464m of DKK 6,320m) [P4]; Tidewater spent 9.2% of 2025 revenue on dry-docks and capex (US$124.3m of US$1,352.8m) [P7]. Bhagwan's spending per dollar of revenue is normal. The problem is its lower margin: 18% EBITDA, against Svitzer's 30% and Tidewater's 44%, so the same upkeep takes 41% of EBITDA instead of 21% to 25%.
- The fleet's age: at the prospectus about 70% of the owned fleet by book value was built in 2009 or later (47.3% in 2009 to 2013, 22.9% from 2014) and the rest earlier [B16, figure 2.8]. Most of the fleet is now 13 to 17 years old. No later filing gives a fleet age. Australian domestic commercial vessels need a renewal survey, in and out of the water, every five years, an extra out-of-water survey at year 3 for high-category vessels, and 10, 20 and 30-year survey items [R1]. Bhagwan capitalises surveys and refits and writes them off over one to five years [B1, note 12]. The bulk of the fleet is coming up to its 15-year renewal surveys and then its 20-year survey items, which do not get cheaper.
- Carrying value against cost: vessels are carried at A$197.4m, the valuer's depreciated replacement cost, against A$119.3m on a cost basis [B1, note 12]. A$43.5m of each is Riverside's nine vessels at their March 2026 fair value, so the original fleet is carried at about A$154m against about A$76m at cost. Depreciation on those revalued amounts was A$20.6m in FY26 (vessels A$16.0m). The company itself says "current rates are not at levels to encourage further investment" and "industry rates must rise substantially to encourage further investment" [B5, B2]: in plain words, today's day rates don't pay for new vessels, so replacement cost is above what the earnings support.
- My figure: about A$16m a year keeps the original fleet in survey. That excludes replacing vessels as they pass 15 to 20 years, which Bhagwan books as "growth" capex (Coral Knight A$13.7m in FY25, Seawind 1, the Bhagwan Ocean for about A$6m in FY26 [B8, B2, B26]). Including an allowance for replacement, the long-run cost is nearer the A$19m to A$20m of depreciation. I use A$16m in the valuation, because the peers' multiples below are struck before fleet renewal too, and treat replacement as a reason not to pay more than peers. The fact sheet's A$18.1m included Riverside's quarter.

### Riverside: the price, the debt and the earnings
- The price [B1, note 21; B20]: A$100.0m cash, 48,780,488 shares at A$0.41 (A$20.0m) and a A$2.6m working capital and net debt adjustment, A$122.6m in all. The earn-out of up to A$10m was not triggered: it started at A$25.2m of FY26 EBITDA and Riverside made A$24.5m [B26]. A$10.1m of cash came with the business, so the net price was A$112.5m, and A$4.3m of lease liabilities came too. Goodwill was A$44.2m and customer contracts A$26.4m (being written off at about A$4.7m a year: A$1.176m in the June quarter [B1, note 3]). The vessels were booked at A$43.5m, above the A$35.7m of the November 2024 and May 2025 independent valuations cited in the acquisition deck [B21, slide 20].
- The funding: A$30m of new shares at A$0.41 (40.0m on 17 February 2026, then 23.9m to institutions and 9.3m to directors and related parties after the 24 March meeting) and a new A$70m bank facility [B1, note 21; B22].
- The A$70m debt [B1, note 17; B22]: a cash advance facility added to the existing Commonwealth Bank facility agreement, fully drawn, maturing 25 March 2029. The report lists its rate as "2.40%" without saying whether that is the margin over the bank bill rate; I read it as the margin. It is secured with everything else: first-ranking security over all present and future property of every group company, Riverside's companies included (shareholders approved that as financial assistance on 24 March 2026), interlocking guarantees and a negative pledge. The covenants are a debt service cover ratio and a gross leverage ratio, plus a minimum equity ratio reported since the first quarter of FY26. Their levels are not disclosed. The covenants "were reviewed and remained unchanged" when the facility was renegotiated, are tested quarterly, and were met at 30 June 2026. Beside it: a A$30m working capital facility (A$20m drawn, bank bill rate plus 0.80%, due 30 June 2027), a A$10m market rate loan (A$5m drawn, plus 1.50%, due 20 December 2027) and A$15.3m of asset finance at 6.01% to 6.25% to 2029 and 2030. Bank debt was A$110.3m and cash A$23.8m at 30 June 2026, with A$29.7m undrawn [B1, notes 1 and 17].
- Riverside's earnings: revenue A$52.0m, A$55.0m and A$60.0m and EBITDA A$17.9m, A$21.6m and A$24.5m in FY24, FY25 and FY26 (34.4%, 39.3% and 40.8% margins) [B2, slide 9]. These are management figures, not audited. Riverside's own audited accounts should be lodged with ASIC as a large proprietary company, but they sit behind a paid search and I have not read them. The FY26 annual report gives one audited check: had Bhagwan owned Riverside all year, its revenue would have been A$60.0m and its profit before tax A$14.3m [B1, note 21]. A$24.5m less A$14.3m leaves A$10.2m of depreciation and amortisation; less about A$4.7m of contract amortisation, that is about A$5.5m of depreciation. So the acquisition deck's A$7m to A$8m a year of maintenance spending (30% to 35% of EBITDA) [B20, B21] is above Riverside's depreciation: a cautious figure, not a flattering one. Riverside's cash earnings are about A$17.0m (A$24.5m less A$7.5m), or about A$16.5m after an estimated A$0.5m a year of property lease payments.
- What it is made of [B21, slide 21]: Riverside Industrial Sands about 39% of EBITDA, Rivtow towage 28%, Magnetic Island Ferries 22%, Oceanic charters 7%, research vessel management 3%. Two points the deck doesn't settle: the sand business is described as "1.6MT available for dredging" [B21, slide 15] and later as a "large long-life resource base" [B27], with no annual volumes, so the life of the biggest piece can't be checked from the filings; and the towage contracts (11 and 9 years with the two miners) are managed-vessel contracts whose renewal dates aren't given.
- Was 7.21 times fair? Net of the cash that came with it Bhagwan paid 6.6 times cash earnings (6.8 times after leases) and 4.6 times EBITDA; on the headline, 7.2 times and 5.0 times; on the A$26.2m vendor forecast the deal was priced at 6.4 times (A$120m over A$18.7m). Against the control deals in the peer table (Svitzer about 9 times cash earnings, MMA Offshore about 12 times) it is a fair price for a business with about 90% recurring revenue, not a bargain and not an overpayment. Two cautions: Bhagwan bought after margins rose from 34% to 41%, and the business missed the vendors' forecast by 6.5% within three months (cut on 3 August 2026, blamed on Oceanic) [B26]. The scrip has fallen with Bhagwan's shares: 48.8m shares at A$0.265 are worth A$12.9m, so the vendors have borne A$7.1m of the fall.

### The BM Fleet loan: a governance test
The facts, from the filings:
- What it is: BM Fleet Pty Ltd is "an entity associated with" the managing director, Loui Kannikoski, and the chair, Anthony Wooles [B1, note 22]. It was set up when Bhagwan's bank would not fund some vessels; BM Fleet bought or built them with a NAB loan, guaranteed personally by the two directors, and leases them to Bhagwan [B1, note 22; B16, section 6.7.1].
- Two loans from Bhagwan to BM Fleet [B1 note 22; B7 note 19; B13; B17 note 26; B18 note 12]:
  - A vessel loan made "on or around February 2022" to acquire or build the Phoenix (now the Bhagwan Micah), the Bhagwan Mover and the AMS Henderson. A$3.0m at 30 June 2022, A$3.1m at 30 June 2023, A$4.4m at 30 June 2024 (A$5.7m at its highest that year), A$5.8m at 30 June 2025, A$6.1m at 30 June 2026. Interest at 8% from 1 July 2023 (A$0.25m in FY24, A$0.43m in FY25, A$0.48m in FY26) is added to the balance: the cash flow statements take it out of operating cash flow as non-cash income [B1, B7].
  - A A$6.65m loan that arose in 2023 when Bhagwan transferred its 50% interest in a joint venture, Bhagwan Seaworks Marine Pty Ltd (carried at A$4.96m plus a A$1.56m receivable at 30 June 2022), to BM Fleet "at the original investment amount", with no gain or loss [B17, notes 17 and 26]. It bears no interest. The company says arm's-length interest would have been 8%, about A$0.5m a year [B13, B7, B1]. A$5.9m was outstanding at 30 June 2025 and 2026.
- Together: A$9.78m (June 2023), A$10.56m (December 2023), A$11.01m (June 2024), A$11.73m (June 2025), A$12.01m (December 2025), A$11.98m (June 2026) [B17, B18, B13, B7, B4, B1]. In FY25, after listing, the balance rose A$0.72m: A$0.43m of interest and about A$0.29m more. In FY26 it rose A$0.25m: A$0.48m of interest less about A$0.23m repaid. Four years on, nothing of substance has been repaid.
- Terms: unsecured; repayable only "from any available operating cash (after BM Fleet Pty Ltd has first repaid any amounts owing under the NAB facility), or on sale of an asset where release of the NAB security has occurred"; no repayment date; "not expected to be settled within the next 12 months"; no provision for loss [B1, note 22]. BM Fleet's NAB lender is secured over the vessels and is paid first.
- How it was approved: both loans were made while Bhagwan was a proprietary company. Its directors in FY23, the year of the joint venture transfer, were Anthony Wooles, Loui Kannikoski and Kerren Kannikoski (Loui's wife) as alternate [B17]; in FY21 the same three plus a second alternate [B19]. So the counterparties and a spouse were the whole board. The first independent director, Tracey Horton, joined on 5 June 2024 [B27]. The company says no shareholder approval was sought because the arrangements were entered into while it was proprietary and so did not attract Chapter 2E of the Corporations Act [B1, note 22; B16, section 6.7].
- The listing rules: Listing Rule 10.1 requires shareholder approval for buying or selling a substantial asset (5% or more of equity interests) from or to a related party, and Rule 10.3(e) exempts agreements made before listing whose existence and material terms were disclosed in the prospectus [R2]. The prospectus used that route for the Phoenix lease (A$3,000 a day, A$5.475m over five years) [B16, section 6.7.1, note 3], and disclosed the loan in section 9.6.4.4. Chapter 10 has no rule on loans as such [R2]. So, as far as the filings show, no listing rule approval was triggered: everything was done before listing and disclosed. Any new advance or change of terms now would have to be tested against Rule 10.1 and Chapter 2E. No shareholder vote on the loan has been held.
- Where the disclosures don't line up:
  1. The prospectus described the whole A$10.8m as a loan to buy or build the three vessels, with interest "payable monthly in arrears at a rate of 8% per annum" [B16, section 9.6.4.4]. The accounts show A$6.65m of it came from the joint venture transfer and bears no interest, and that the interest on the rest is capitalised, not paid monthly [B17, B13, B1].
  2. The FY23 and 1H24 accounts called the joint venture loan "priced on an arm's length basis" [B17, B18]; from FY24 the accounts say it bears no interest and that arm's-length interest would have been 8% [B13, B7, B1].
  3. The FY26 note says the directors who are not receiving the benefit "consider that the terms and conditions of the related party transaction described in this note were no more favourable than those available... on an arm's length basis", and the same note says A$5.9m of the loan bears no interest where arm's-length interest would be 8% [B1, note 22].
- How the company presents it: its net debt counts the loan as cash. A$74.5m at 30 June 2026 against A$86.5m of bank debt less cash; A$1.0m at 31 December 2025 against A$13.0m; A$5.3m at 30 June 2025 against A$17.0m [B2, B24, B8]. The 1.2 times leverage in the FY26 deck uses the lower figure [B2, slide 15].

What it means for minority holders. The sums are not large against the business: A$12.0m is about 3 cents a share and about 6% of my middling enterprise value. The governance signal is larger. A company that lent A$12m of its own money, unsecured and half of it free, to the two men who chair and run it, and transferred a joint venture stake to them at book value, with no independent director in the room, has shown what its controllers will do when no rule stops them. Since listing the board has one independent director of four, a chair who is not independent and an executive director of finance, so "the Board (excluding conflicted Directors)", which approves the related-party lease renewals [B29], is two people, one of them an executive of the company. I value the loan at nil to A$8m, not at face, and treat governance as one reason the market won't pay full peer multiples.

### Other related-party dealings
- Vessel leases from BM Fleet (A$0.63m expensed in FY26) and from KFAMS Pty Ltd, the company of the managing director's son Tom (A$0.76m), A$1.4m paid in all including GST [B1, note 22]. KFAMS leases expire on 21 November 2026, 11 December 2026 and 15 May 2027; the BM Fleet barge was off-hired in November 2025; the Bhagwan Micah lease runs five years from delivery. Bhagwan also billed BM Fleet A$0.63m over two years, at cost plus 10%, for finishing and commissioning the Micah it will lease from BM Fleet [B1, B7]. The Bhagwan Mover, leased from BM Fleet to 6 October 2026 at the prospectus [B16], no longer appears in the FY25 or FY26 lists; a A$5.6m vessel moved from leased to owned in FY26 is not explained [B1, notes 12 and 13]. Whether they are linked isn't stated.
- The Dampier base is leased from the Kannikoski Property Trust, renewed in June 2026 for one year to 30 June 2027 (about A$0.3m a year) [B1].
- Three close family members of directors are employees; Kerren and Tom Kannikoski receive performance rights approved by shareholders [B1, B27].
- On the other side: the prospectus disclosed all of these, shareholders voted on the director placement and the performance rights, and the founders bought shares in the placement at A$0.41 and on market at A$0.28 to A$0.33 [B22, B31].

### Method 2: discounted cash flow
After-tax cash earnings (30% tax) growing at a steady rate, discounted at a cost of capital. Inputs: tax 30%; cost of capital 11% in the middling case (Australian ten-year rate about 4.5%, plus a share risk premium for a small, cyclical, illiquid company of about 8% on the equity, with about 40% of the capital in debt at about 6% before tax), 12% in the bad case and 10% in the good; growth 1.5%, 0% and 2.5%. Python: middling 28.6 x 0.7 x 1.015 / (0.11 - 0.015) = A$213.9m, 7.5 times pre-tax cash earnings, equity A$137.9m, A$0.35 a share; bad 19.3 x 0.7 / 0.12 = A$112.8m, A$0.07; good 35.5 x 0.7 x 1.025 / 0.075 = A$339.4m, A$0.68. The single input that moves it most is the gap between the cost of capital and growth: a point either way moves the middling value by about 4 cents. It supports Method 1's middling value (A$0.31 to A$0.35) and shows how fragile the ends are. Growth of 1.5% on a fleet that will need replacing is generous.

### Method 3 (cross-check): net assets
Net tangible assets are A$147.1m, A$0.37 a share, with vessels at the valuer's replacement cost, or A$92.5m, A$0.23, with the vessels at cost (A$78.1m of uplift less 30% tax) [B1]. The company's own words on day rates say replacement cost is above what earnings support. MMA's independent valuer put MMA's fleet at A$578m on the market, below the A$842m its cash flows supported [P3]; Bhagwan is the other way round. Net assets would only be realised through a sale of the company or the fleet, so they are not the middling case.

### Range
A$0.13 to A$0.50 a share, middling A$0.31 (Method 1) to A$0.35 (Method 2). It depends above all on the original business's cash earnings: each A$1m a year of core EBITDA, or of sustaining spending saved, is worth about A$7m, 1.8 cents a share. The second input is whether the market credits the BM Fleet loan (3 cents a share at face). A control buyer paying Svitzer's 9 times on the middling cash earnings would pay about A$0.45; that is the good case's territory and needs the founders' consent.

## Why the market is wrong
I can't say that it is, today. At A$0.265 the market values Bhagwan at A$192m including bank debt (A$105.5m of shares and A$86.5m of net bank debt), which is 3.0 times FY26's annualised EBITDA (A$64.5m: core A$40.0m and a full year of Riverside's A$24.5m) but 7.6 times FY26's annualised cash earnings (about A$25.2m) and 6.7 times my FY27 middling A$28.6m. That is about what Bhagwan paid for Riverside and a little under Tidewater. The low EBITDA multiple is explained by the A$15m of leases and A$16m of upkeep that come out of the original business's A$40m to A$47m of EBITDA. The market looks to be pricing the cash, the debt and the governance about right. It would be wrong if the 1H27 result shows the original business back at its FY25 rate with upkeep held near A$8m a half and debt falling; that is the event to wait for.

# Grange Resources (ASX:GRR): deep dive. Not a value pick: the bad case (A$0.05) is 52% below the price and the middling case (A$0.12) only 14% above it

Written 2026-11-08 by the research desk. Price at writing: A$0.105 (close on 30 October 2026, Norgate, data/norgate/universe/prices_2026.csv.gz). Market value: A$121.5m (shares on issue 1,157,338,698, half-year report note 15 [G3]).
Status: finished. This is a simulation and not financial advice.
Fact sheet: research/facts/GRR.md, grade B. The question it set: will the A$287m sub-level cave return its money at today's pellet prices, or use up the cash and leave the A$148.7m closure bill?

Order of work. The brief and the fact sheet both state the A$0.105 close, so the price was known before the work began. The filings and the valuation below were done without using it: no input to either comes from Grange's own share price (the peer section uses the peers' prices). Grange's price series was opened only after the valuation arithmetic was run, for the liquidity, event and outcome sections.

In short: the fact sheet was too generous on both ends. Its A$0.44 "our way" figure adds the plan's A$357m project value to today's cash, but the company's own year-by-year cash chart is worth about A$244m at the plan's 8.3% rate, not A$357m, and the plan prices pellets at A$181/t when the September quarter got A$164.41/t and today's iron ore price points to about A$157/t. Its A$0.13 "against us" figure (cash less the closure provision) assumes the cash stays in the bank, but the board has committed it to the underground mine. Built on plan at the September quarter's price, the company runs short of cash in 2028 by about A$40m (about A$84m once a working cash buffer is kept); at today's price it is about A$100m (about A$140m with the buffer), close to or above the whole market value. Worked through, the business is worth about A$0.10 to A$0.12 a share at the September quarter's price once the gap is paid for, A$0.04 to A$0.06 at today's, A$0.12 if it were wound down now, and A$0.23 to A$0.33 only if pellets get back to the plan's A$175 to A$181. The expected price by 31 October 2028 is A$0.118, about 12% above A$0.105 before costs and about 6% after them, over two years. It doesn't meet the value pick test on either leg. No card; watch for the December quarter and the FY2026 result.

## The company in plain words
Grange owns and runs the Savage River magnetite mine in north-west Tasmania, an 85km slurry pipeline, and a pellet plant and port at Port Latta. It mines magnetite ore, upgrades it to a concentrate above 65% iron, and turns that into iron ore pellets for blast furnaces, selling about 2.3 to 2.5 million tonnes a year plus some lower-value "chips" [G5, G7]. Pellets are priced off iron ore indexes with a premium for quality, less freight [G3 note 17]. It also owns the undeveloped Southdown magnetite project near Albany in Western Australia (1,257Mt resource), for which it has been looking for an equity partner since at least 2025; Southdown costs are expensed, so it has no book value [G4, G2, G7].

Who runs it. Weidong Wang is CEO [G1]. The board has five directors: three independents (chair Dr Michelle Li, Fong Hoon, Ajanth Saverimutto) and two from Shagang: Guo Wei, CFO of Jiangsu Shagang Group, appointed 9 March 2026, and Jie Yu, President of Jiangsu Shagang Group Investment Holding, appointed 2 June 2026 [G3, G17, G18]. They replaced two more junior Shagang directors who resigned in December 2025 and April 2026 [G19, G20].

Who owns it. Shagang International Holdings (Hong Kong), part of Jiangsu Shagang Group, China's largest private steelmaker, holds 554,762,656 shares (47.9%; the related-party note says 48.02% at 28 February 2026) [G5, G4]. The next holders are funds and private client accounts: RQI 3.9%, Pacific International 1.7%, DFA 1.6%, UBS private banking 1.2%, First Sentier 1.1%, Interactive Brokers clients 1.1%, Dimensional 1.1%; the top 20 hold 65.7% [G5]. About 8,450 holders at 30 September 2026 [G2]. There are 7,385,995 performance rights on issue (0.6%) [G5]. At the May 2026 AGM a candidate not endorsed by the board got 0.81% of the vote; Shagang's shares made up about 86% of the votes cast [G8].

## Why big investors may be overlooking it
- Size and index: A$121.5m market value. Removed from the S&P/ASX All Ordinaries before the open on 21 September 2026 [G23]; not in the S&P/ASX 300.
- Trading: the median day over the 20 sessions 5 to 30 October 2026 traded A$71,738 (lowest A$31,685); the quoted spread is one tick, 0.105 to 0.11, about 4.7% (D1, ASX company data).
- Register: Shagang's 47.9% leaves a free float of about half the company, and Shagang is also the biggest customer.
- History and mood: a write-down above the top of the company's own range, a shelved block cave, a mine life cut from 2040 to 2035 and a refused funding request, all in 2026 [G6, G10, G3, G1].
- Sector: iron ore is weak. 62% fines were about US$90.3/t on 27 October 2026, down 6.1% in a month [M1]. Champion Iron, the closest listed seller of high-grade product, had a negative cash operating margin in the September quarter and its net realised price was down 30% on a year earlier [P1].
- Who sold: First Sentier and MUFG went from 5.05% (their December 2025 notices) to 6.11% (notice of 3 March 2026) and then below 5% on 8 April 2026 [G21, G22]. The heaviest trading of the year was March to May 2026 (35.9m, 43.3m and 61.1m shares a month, against 14m to 19m a month since July), while the price fell from 22c to 15c around the 13 May funding announcement (D1). That is a holder who knows the company leaving, not an overlooked share.
- Coverage: I didn't check broker databases, so "little coverage" isn't claimed.

So some of the "overlooked" signs apply (size, index exit, thin trading, a controlled register), but the price fall also lines up with real news: the funding refusal, the write-down and a weaker iron ore market. It looks cheap for a reason as much as overlooked.

### The closure bill
The decommissioning provision rose from A$119.5m at December 2025 to A$148.7m at June 2026. Most of the rise (A$38.3m) is a "change in estimate" that the company puts down to a change in discount rate [G3 note 14]; shortening the mine life also brings the spending forward. It covers Savage River and Port Latta, and a repayment owed to the Tasmanian government through remediation spending under the Goldamere Act [G4]. It is a discounted figure; the filings don't give the undiscounted cost or its timing. The accounts also say that if the mine plan dropped the rehabilitation now done during mining, the provision would go up [G4]. Bank guarantees and an environmental bond of about A$9.8m back part of it [G4 note 30].

### The underground plan, checked against its own numbers
The board approved the North Pit Underground sub-level cave on 14 October 2026 [G1]:
- A$287m of underground capital and A$234m of sustaining capital to 2035; first SLC ore in 2028, about 4Mt a year by 2030; open pit finished in 2028.
- Based on US$90/t 62% fines and A$1 = US$0.68, giving an average product price of A$181/t, a C1 of A$132/t (A$126/t once the SLC is at full rate from 2030) and an all-in cost of A$164/t.
- NPV A$357m at 8.3% real after tax, IRR 43%, payback 5.8 years; "projected to be fully funded from existing cash reserves and cashflows"; the cash forecast "maintains funds above A$24M through the life of mine" and ends 2035 at A$830m; no distributions to shareholders are included.
- "The SLC has been tested across a range of price scenarios and maintains a positive cash position at prices as low as US$80/t (62% Fe fines)." No pellet price or exchange rate is given for that test.

What I found when checking it:
1. The A$357m doesn't match the company's own cash chart. Reading Figure 2 of the release year by year gives free cash flow of about -93, -150, +50, +21, +74, +138, +167, +160 and +160 (A$m, 2027 to 2035), and an opening balance of about A$285m, which adds to about A$812m by 2035 against the stated A$830m, so the reading is close. Discounted at 8.3% real, those flows are worth about A$244m, with an IRR of about 26%, not A$357m and 43% (arithmetic below). The release doesn't say what the A$357m is measured against. If it is measured against shutting the mine after the open pit, it already includes the gain from not paying the closure bill early, and adding it to cash and then taking off the closure provision, as the fact sheet did, counts value that isn't there. I use the cash chart, because that is what a shareholder actually gets.
2. The A$287m isn't the whole cost of going underground. Figure 9 books about A$48m of "project development and readiness" in 2027, and A$80m of lateral development in 2028, as operating cost, not capital [G1]. The 2027 cash call is about A$120m on top of running the open pit.
3. The plan's operating cost is the same as the June 2026 impairment test's: A$355.9m in 2026, A$1,846.1m in 2027 to 2031 and A$1,116.0m in 2032 to 2035, which adds to exactly the A$3,318m in the release [G3 note 17, G1]. So the release and the write-down rest on one model. The difference is price: the impairment test used pellets at US$119.23 to US$125.52 in 2027 to 2031 and US$128.72 to US$134.07 in 2032 to 2035, at AUD/USD 0.71 to 0.73 (about A$170/t and A$180/t).
4. The plan's price is above today's market. In the September quarter, Grange's FOB price (US$116.13) was about 1.03 times the 65% index average for the same quarter (US$112.8, from Champion Iron's filing [P1]); 62% fines traded at about US$95 to US$101 in August and September (US$98.70 on 8 September, US$96.60 on 25 September) [M2, M3]; I found no July figures and take the quarter at about US$97. Fines were US$90.3 on 27 October [M1]. On the September quarter's relationship (my estimate, using either index), US$90 fines give about US$107 to US$111/t FOB, which is A$154 to A$161/t at AUD/USD 0.69 to 0.70 (0.6900 on 6 November 2026, data/fx/fx_daily.csv). The plan's A$181/t needs fines back near US$100, a bigger pellet premium, or a much weaker Australian dollar. Iron ore prices here come from trade press, not company filings.
5. Costs are close to plan in the open pit years. The plan's average C1 of A$132/t, with A$126/t from 2030, implies about A$141/t before 2030 (my arithmetic: 25.1Mt at A$132 is A$3,313m; 2030 to 2035 at A$126 on about 15.3Mt is about A$1,928m; the rest, about 9.8Mt, averages about A$141/t). The last four quarters averaged A$144.90/t [G13, G12, G11, G2]. The gap between plan and today is price, not cost.

## Valuation
Every figure is in A$, on 1,157.34m shares. The arithmetic was run with python3 -I (the scripts sit in the scratchpad, not the repo); results are rounded.

### Method 1: the company's own cash flows, at the plan's price and at today's
I take the yearly free cash flow from the release's Figure 2, change only the realised price, and keep everything else (tonnes, costs, capital, timing) as the company planned. A A$1/t change in realised price moves cash by about A$2.6m a year before tax (2.6Mt a year, 2.4Mt in 2035). I give no tax relief in 2027 and 2028, when the plan is loss-making, and 30% from 2029. Value = opening cash of A$285m (the model's January 2027 balance) + the present value of the 2027 to 2035 flows at 8.3% real (10% real as a check) - the A$148.7m closure provision.

Checking the plan first: the flows add to A$527m, ending at A$812m (company: A$830m); present value A$244m at 8.3% (A$234m with end-year discounting), A$205m at 10%; IRR about 26%.

Paying for the gap at the September quarter's price (equity A$163m before funding, gap about A$81m including the buffer, measured at mid-2028; A$84m if the end-2028 balance is lower):
- Debt for three years at about 9% plus 2% fees costs about A$23m: equity A$140m, 12.1c.
- An equity raise of A$81m at 7.5c would issue about 1,076m shares (93% of today's); value per share afterwards about 10.8c. At 6.0c, 1,345m shares and about 9.6c.

So the real "nothing happens" value (the mine built on plan, pellet prices where they are) is:
- about 10c to 12c at the September quarter's A$164/t, once the gap is paid for, which is about the price (within 10%);
- about 4c to 6c at today's iron ore price (A$154 to A$161/t), with a gap of about A$140m, more than the whole market value, to fund first. That is 40% to 60% below the price.

Because iron ore has fallen since the quarter closed, the second is the better reading of "where prices are". Neither is the fact sheet's 13c, which assumed the cash stays put.

The main thing this method depends on is the realised pellet price: each A$1/t over the life is worth about A$13m today, about 1.1c a share. The directors' own test gives the same scale (US$1/t is A$19.4m, about A$13.9m per A$1/t at AUD 0.72) [G3].

### Method 3: named peers
- Fenix Resources (ASX:FEX), a small WA iron ore producer: enterprise value A$197.9m (ASX company data, 7 November 2026 [P3]) against FY2026 EBITDA of A$80.7m [P2], about 2.5 times. It pays a dividend.
- Grange's enterprise value, counting the closure provision and leases as debt, is about -A$27m (121.5 - 300.4 + 148.7 + 3.4), against adjusted EBITDA of about A$103m over the year to June 2026 (H1 2026 A$32.7m [G3]; H2 2025 about A$70.6m, my arithmetic from the 2025 accounts [G4] less H1 2025's A$37.4m [G24]). At Fenix's 2.5 times, Grange would be worth about 35c, and about 21c on a forward EBITDA of A$40m.
- That multiple doesn't carry across. Grange's EBITDA is struck after capitalising about A$100m a year of open pit stripping, and before A$521m of underground and sustaining capital over nine years (about A$58m a year). Free cash flow is the binding measure, and it was -A$21.9m in 2025 and +A$3.3m in H1 2026. At today's price (A$157/t, C1 about A$143/t) forward EBITDA on a cash basis is nearer A$26m a year.
- Champion Iron (ASX and TSX:CIA), the nearest listed seller of high-grade iron ore: in the September 2026 quarter its gross realised price was US$105.5/dmt against a 65% index average of US$112.8, its net realised price fell 30% on a year earlier, and its cash operating margin was -C$8.0/dmt [P1]. The high-grade squeeze is industry-wide; Grange isn't the only one priced for it.

I give the peer method little weight: it brackets the answer (21c to 35c) only by ignoring the capital the mine needs.

### Range
- Worth about 4c to 6c a share if the mine is built and today's iron ore price holds (Method 1 at A$154 to A$161/t, after funding).
- About 10c to 12c at the September quarter's price, or if the board stopped the underground now (Methods 1 and 2).
- About 23c to 33c only if realised prices go back to the plan's A$175 to A$181/t (Method 1).
The main thing each depends on: the realised pellet price over 2027 to 2030, and whether the cash gap is filled with debt or with new shares sold cheaply.

## Why the market is wrong
On these numbers it mostly isn't. The fact sheet's case was that cash less closure (13c) is above the price, so the market values the mine at less than nothing. But that cash is committed: at today's iron ore price the plan needs about A$140m more than the company has, and the mine's value is close to zero until pellet prices recover. The price of 10.5c sits between the value at today's prices (4c to 6c) and at the September quarter's (10c to 12c), with the plan's 33c as the upside if iron ore recovers. That is a fair price for a two-way bet on iron ore with a funding gap, not a clear mispricing.

What could still make the market wrong, and should be watched: the Australian dollar (each one cent fall is about A$2.3/t on the realised price), a recovery in the pellet premium, a funding route that doesn't need new shares (debt sized for the smaller SLC, or a prepayment from a pellet buyer), or Shagang bidding for the rest of the company.

- Stake and history: 554,762,656 shares, 47.9%, with no substantial holder notice in the ASX announcement lists for 2023 to 2026 [G5, G26]. It is also the biggest customer (30.5% of 2025 revenue) [G4].
- What it has said: support was "anticipated" in February 2026 and refused "at this stage" in May 2026 [G4, G6]. Since then it has put its group CFO and the president of its investment arm on the board [G17, G18].
- What the rules let it do: as a holder above 19% for more than six months it can buy 3% every six months without a bid (Corporations Act s611 item 9), which would take it past 50% within six months; it can bid for the rest; it can underwrite a rights issue and take up the shortfall (items 10 and 13), the usual way a controlling holder gains control in a funding squeeze; or holders not associated with it can approve an issue of shares to it (item 7). As a foreign holder it would probably need FIRB clearance to raise its stake; I haven't confirmed this.
- What it can block: with 47.9% and about 86% of the votes cast at the 2026 AGM [G8], it carries any ordinary resolution and blocks any special resolution. No outside bidder can get control or reach 90% without it, so there is no rival bid unless Shagang sells, and a buyer of its stake would have to bid for everyone.
- What it can afford: the rest of the company costs about A$63m at 10.5c, or about A$80m to A$95m at a 30% to 50% premium, small for a group of Shagang's size. Whether it wants to is unknown.
- Its interest isn't the same as minority holders'. As a customer it wants pellets to 2035; as a 48% owner it may prefer to fund a shortfall through a discounted, underwritten issue rather than a loan. The board's statement that the SLC "secures our ability to continue supplying high-quality pellets to customers" fits both [G1].

## What would make this wrong
The pick's case would break on:
1. Pellet prices staying where they are now. At A$154 to A$161/t the plan needs about A$110m to A$170m more than the company holds by 2028, a sum near or above the market value. Whether that becomes debt or cheap new shares decides whether holders get 10c or 4c.
2. Shagang using the gap. The 48% holder, which is also the customer that wants pellets to 2035, refused to fund the project in May 2026 and now has two senior executives on the board. An underwritten discounted issue would move value to whoever takes up the shares. The board may also keep spending into a loss to keep the supply going.
3. The cave itself. Ground "from fair to very poor", inrush risk under the pit and scarce caving staff [G1]. The independent review left out geotechnical conditions and ore recovery. A delay to first ore in 2028 lands just when the cash is lowest.

What would make "no card" wrong: iron ore recovering to about US$100 fines, the Australian dollar falling toward 0.65, a funding route that needs no new shares, or a Shagang bid. Any of these lifts the expected price above 15c.

Exit rule, if a card is ever written: sell the whole position at the next open after an announced equity raise not offered pro rata to all holders, a quarterly realised price below A$150/t, or a delay to first SLC ore beyond 2028; otherwise sell on or after 31 October 2028 unless a fresh deep dive renews it. A takeover proposal is a fresh decision.

Reopen if any of these happens:
- The price falls to about A$0.055 or below with no worse news. Both legs of the value pick test would then pass on these outcomes, subject to a fresh deep dive.
- The December 2026 quarter or the FY2026 result shows a realised price of A$165/t or more, together with a debt or prepayment facility that covers the gap without new shares.
- Shagang moves: a substantial holder change, a proposal, or an announced underwritten issue. That makes it a special situation deep dive.

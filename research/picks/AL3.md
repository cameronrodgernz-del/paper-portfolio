## The company in plain words
AML3D makes large metal parts by robotic arc welding, laying wire down layer by layer (wire-arc additive manufacturing, which it calls WAM), and sells the machines that do it (ARCEMY systems) [A1]. Money comes from three places [A1, note 6; A6]:
- Selling systems: A$8.78m of FY26's A$11.76m of sales (75%). The flagship ARCEMY X sells for about A$1.7m to A$2.5m; custom heavy versions for shipyards about A$2.2m to A$2.5m each. The company says gross margins on systems are 50% to 60% [A6].
- Printing parts and qualifying alloys for customers: A$2.70m (23%).
- Licences, service and support: A$0.28m, plus A$0.71m of equipment lease income shown outside revenue from contracts.

Where: US customers were A$9.44m of the A$11.76m (80%), the UK A$1.24m (BAE Systems), Australia A$1.08m [A1, note 28]. The US business runs from Stow, Ohio (opened December 2024, officially June 2025); Australia from North Plympton, Adelaide [A1; A6].

Who runs it: Sean Ebert, managing director since September 2023 (ex Worley M&A); chair Noel Cornish (ex BlueScope Australia and New Zealand chief executive); founder Andrew Sales, chief technology officer until August 2026, now a non-executive director; Peter Siebels, ex KPMG partner [A1].

Who owns it: the only substantial holder is Netwealth Investments (a wrap platform holding for its clients) with 35,716,514 shares (6.29%). The top 20 hold 31.41%; there are 6,500 holders and 1,605 hold less than a marketable parcel [A1, shareholder information, 24 August 2026]. The four directors hold about 6.75m shares between them (1.2%): Ebert about 3.29m (2.29m of it from rights that vested on 27 October 2026), Siebels 2.2m, Cornish 0.70m, Sales 0.55m [D1, D2, D3, D4].

Delivery record: of the seven announced systems commissioned since mid-2024, five were on time (Cogitic, TVA, NNS's first two, FasTech) and two ran late by four and nine months (both Austal). The first half of FY26 also slipped: of A$16.5m in hand then, A$3.25m was booked, "attributable to delays in the receipt of raw materials and extensions to project timelines" [A4].

When revenue is booked. The policy is "a point in time when the performance obligation is satisfied which is typically upon delivery to the customer" [A1, note 2(j)], and KPMG's key audit matter is the risk of revenue landing in the wrong period at year end [A1, auditor's report]. In practice systems have been booked at final commissioning:
- Cogitic was installed and tested in June 2024 (FY24) and commissioned in August 2024; the FY25 annual report lists it among "key ARCEMY installations in FY25" [A14], and H1 FY25's two largest customers were 74% of that half's revenue [A4, note on major customers], which fits Cogitic (A$2.5m) plus the LWS purchase (A$1.1m).
- TVA's system was commissioned in August 2025 and FY25's A$3.52m of equipment sales fits Austal plus the LWS purchase without it; H1 FY26's three largest customers were 88% of A$3.25m [A4], which fits TVA (A$1.97m) plus BAE's UK work (A$0.41m) [A4, note on geography].
- FY26's A$8.78m of equipment sales is close to the four systems commissioned in the year: TVA A$1.97m, FasTech A$1.69m, Austal portable A$1.2m, NNS's first two about A$4.1m (A$8.96m) [A1, A7].
So the four NNS systems are most likely booked when each is commissioned at Newport News. The company's own timing for that has been January to March 2027 in three of the four statements.

What is contracted for FY27. A$16.8m rolled into FY27 [A1, A3]. Identified within it: NNS A$9.9m and BlueForge A$2.61m; the other about A$4.3m isn't itemised (the rest of the BAE programme, TVA's qualification work of about A$0.30m, other qualification prints, licences and service). Added since 1 July: Austal A$0.5m and Northrop Grumman A$2.2m. That is about A$19.5m, of which Northrop Grumman's A$2.2m is due for installation in April to June 2027 and could slip into FY28. Plus recurring lease and licence income of about A$1m a year. FY27 revenue of about A$17m to A$19m is in sight if nothing slips; For scale, FY26 worked through A$29m of orders (A$9m carried in, A$20m new), booked about A$12m and carried A$16.8m out [A5, A8].

Half by half: if NNS is commissioned by 31 December, H1 FY27 revenue is about A$11m to A$13m; if in January to March, about A$2m to A$4m, with H2 then carrying NNS, BlueForge, Austal's parts and Northrop Grumman.

Customer concentration. Four customers were 77% of FY26 revenue from contracts (35%, 19%, 12%, 11%); FY25's two largest (32% and 31%) fell to 8% and 5% [A1, note 28]. Customer 1, at 35% (about A$4.1m), matches NNS's first order. In FY27 NNS's A$9.9m would be about half of revenue. The customers are a handful of US Navy primes and their suppliers: NNS (HII), Austal USA, BlueForge, Cogitic, LWS, Northrop Grumman; FasTech and TVA are the non-Navy exceptions.

### 2. The US Navy demand
The programmes the orders come from, from US government sources:
- Money already spent on the submarine industrial base: "more than $10 billion", and "DOD does not know how much funding it needs" beyond that; GAO recommended that DoD assess the full scope and improve oversight, and found DoD "has not taken key steps to oversee some of its costliest submarine industrial base investments" [G3]. The Congressional Research Service puts the amount appropriated through FY2024, requested for FY2025 and programmed for FY2026 to FY2028 for the construction industrial base at about US$9.8bn, cites the Navy's estimate that a further US$17.5bn would be needed from FY2024 to FY2029, and notes Australia's US$3bn AUKUS contribution to the US industrial base, about US$1bn of it paid by October 2025 [G2].
- The 2025 reconciliation act, section 20002, about US$29.2bn for shipbuilding available to 30 September 2029, including "$450,000,000 for United States additive manufacturing for wire production and machining capacity for shipbuilding industrial base", US$500m for "adoption of advanced manufacturing techniques in the shipbuilding industrial base", US$250m for "additional advanced manufacturing processes", US$750m for supplier development and US$4.6bn for a second Virginia-class boat in FY2026 [G5, the Senate Armed Services Committee's text; G2 confirms the US$4.6bn line in the enacted act].
- BlueForge Alliance, the non-profit through which several AML3D orders were placed: a US$950,744,520 cost-plus-fixed-fee contract (options to US$980.7m), sole source, from Naval Sea Systems Command on 10 September 2024, US$503.1m obligated at award from shipbuilding and foreign partner funds [G1]. AML3D raised A$30m on the expectation that "a significant proportion" would go to additive manufacturing [R8]; no AML3D document says how much has.
- The FY2027 budget request (21 April 2026): US$65.8bn for shipbuilding, two Virginia-class and one Columbia-class submarine; the press release gives no figure for the industrial base [G6].
- The rate the money is meant to fix: Virginia-class output has run at about 1.1 to 1.2 boats a year since 2022 (1.13 at the end of 2024) against a goal of 2.0 and later 2.33 [G2].
- The Navy's Maritime Industrial Base programme (Matt Sermon): additive manufacturing is "central", nearly 1,200 supplier development and advanced manufacturing projects since 2018 in 40 states, and it is "targeting nine material and build process combinations" to cover more than 75% of long-lead submarine components [G7].
- The letter AML3D received (27 June 2025, from the Navy's Maritime Industrial Base office) [O14]: an "intent to collaborate"; the Navy is "interested in providing AML3D with a representative list of potential parts"; "These parts, however, still need to follow the traditional procurement process by the Shipyards and Shipbuilders"; "a minimum of ~400 parts will need to be produced additively" in FY26 rising to about 1,600 by FY30; and "the need for up to 100 new systems to be installed across the industrial base", for which "AML3D's innovative solutions can play a pivotal role". AML3D's headline that it was "identified as key to the overall supply of around 400 components" goes further than the letter. ASX queried the timing of the announcement [O15].
- Competition: on 30 September 2025 the Navy's MIB programme, General Dynamics Electric Boat and Lincoln Electric announced a large-scale metal AM capability in Cleveland to make "critical submarine components" [S1, a secondary report of the joint announcement].

How dependent AML3D is: 80% of FY26 sales were to US customers, and the company says 15 of its 16 US systems "directly support the US Navy MIB" [A5]. Of the about A$19.5m identified for FY27, about A$15.2m (78%) is Navy industrial base work. The budget lines are large relative to AML3D (US$450m for wire AM alone is about 50 times AML3D's FY26 revenue), but the money reaches it only through shipyards' and primes' purchase orders, one competitive tender at a time (TVA and Northrop Grumman were both tenders [O11, O27]).

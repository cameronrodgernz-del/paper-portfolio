# Helloworld Travel (ASX:HLO): deep dive. Not a value pick: the bad case (A$1.10) is 19% below the price, and the middling business value (A$1.80) needs a re-rating the market hasn't given Helloworld this year

Written 2026-11-08 by the research desk. Price at writing: A$1.355 (close on 6 November 2026, ASX company data in data/research_data/asx_company.json, fetched 7 November 2026 UTC; bid A$1.345, ask A$1.355). Norgate's last close is A$1.34 on 30 October 2026. Market value: A$253.5m (187,109,776 shares: 163,680,166 plus 23,429,610 issued to Crown Currency Exchange's vendors on 30 October 2026, Appendix 2A [H14]).
Status: finished. Verdict: wait for a named event, Helloworld's H1 FY27 result (expected about 25 February 2027); the conditions that would reopen it are set out under "What would make this wrong". Not a value pick, and not an ordinary pick at today's price. This is a simulation and not financial advice.
Fact sheet: research/facts/HLO.md, grade A as a value pick ("our way" A$2.77, "against" A$1.24, price about A$1.34). The question it set: does underlying EBITDA turn into cash (A$6.6m of operating cash over two years against A$115.8m of EBITDA), and does the A$135m, mostly borrowed, Crown purchase earn its cost?

Value pick test (rulebook section 5a): it fails. On my valuation the bad case is A$1.10, 19% below A$1.355 (the limit is 10%). The middling business value is A$1.80, 33% above, which passes the 25% leg; but the market has priced Helloworld's core business at about 5 to 5.6 times EBITDA after leases since at least January 2026, and at that multiple the middling outcome is about A$1.50, 11% above. Counting about 15c of dividends over 24 months, the bad case would be 8% below the price; the desk tests the prices themselves (as in NZ_ARG), so that doesn't change the answer, but it shows the call is closer than the headline.

Order of work. The brief and the fact sheet both quote the price, so it was in view before the work began. "What the filings show" and "Valuation" were worked from Helloworld's filings, the peers' filings and the peers' prices; no input to either comes from Helloworld's own share price. The Webjet stake was valued at the Webjet market price and at the desk's Webjet deep dive figure, which I read only after the valuation below was done. Helloworld's price is used only in the peer comparison's last line, "Why the market is wrong", "Outcomes and chances" and the checks.

In short:
1. Cash conversion is fine. FY25's 15% was mostly an accident of the calendar: FY25 carried 53 weekly airline settlement (BSP) payments instead of 52, an extra A$40.0m that the company itself quantifies [H5]. Put back, and with the cash costs Helloworld leaves out of "underlying" profit added back, pre-tax operating cash was 108% of underlying EBITDA in FY25 and 107% in FY26. FY26 is the normal year.
2. But almost none of the A$84.8m of cash at 30 June 2026 was Helloworld's own. After the money owed to airlines, suppliers and clients (net of what agents owe it), its own cash was about minus A$4m, so with Crown it has about A$141m of net debt on my measure, not the A$51m that cash less bank debt suggests.
3. "Underlying" profit leaves out cash costs that come back every year (start-up, acquisition, legal and restructuring costs): A$4.3m, A$6.7m, A$4.2m and A$9.7m in FY23 to FY26, about A$6.2m a year.
4. Crown's A$22m of EBITDA is, on Helloworld's lease accounting, before the rent on its 68 stores (many in shopping centres), and the rent isn't disclosed. At my central A$7m a year of rent, Crown earns about A$9.4m of free cash a year, worth about A$90m at Helloworld's own 11.5% discount rate, against A$135m paid; the debt terms aren't public either.
5. The value is about A$1.80 a share in the middling case (range A$1.10 to A$2.75). The fact sheet's A$2.77 is my good case, not my middling one: it double counts the Webjet dividend, uses A$1.9m of recurring costs instead of about A$6m, a 25% tax rate and A$2.2m for Crown's stores.
6. Expected price on 5 March 2027: A$1.44, plus about 4.7c of interim dividend, +9.7% before costs and about +7% after them, against a guide bar of about 9%. The H1 FY27 result will show Crown's costs and debt; if they come in at or better than my central figures, the bad case rises to about A$1.33 and this becomes a value pick.

## The company in plain words

- Retail networks: about 2,600 member agencies (Helloworld, Travelworld and others) and home-based brokers, more than 10,000 agents and brokers in all; MTA (Mobile Travel Agents, about 450 home-based advisors) has been fully owned since 21 October 2025 [H1, H16, H29].

## What the filings show

### Cash conversion, year by year (question 1)
Operating cash flow from continuing operations was A$62.8m in FY24, minus A$12.5m in FY25 and A$23.3m in FY26 [H5, H1]. Three things sit between underlying EBITDA and that line every year, and two more distorted FY25 and FY24:
- Tax. Helloworld paid A$3.2m in FY24 (tax payable rose from A$0.2m to A$16.0m), then A$22.3m in FY25 and A$23.6m in FY26 as it caught up [H7, H4, H1]. Current tax expense was A$15.1m in FY25 and A$12.0m in FY26 [H1, note 2.4.1].
- Income counted in EBITDA but received as investing cash: dividends from Webjet (A$3.1m in FY26) and from associates such as MTA and Phil Hoffmann Travel (A$2.8m in FY26, A$4.6m in FY25) [H1, cash flow statement].
- The cash costs Helloworld calls significant items and leaves out of underlying EBITDA (A$9.7m in FY26, A$4.2m in FY25), which are inside operating cash flow.
- The airline settlement calendar. Agents pay Helloworld for the week's tickets on Wednesday; Helloworld pays the airlines through IATA's BSP on the following Monday, so year-end cash "varies considerably depending on the day of the week" [H1, p. 29]. FY25 ran from a Monday payment on 24 June 2024 to one on 30 June 2025, 53 payments; the company puts the extra at A$40.0m [H5]. FY26 (payments 7 July 2025 to 29 June 2026) had the usual 52.
- Interest paid on the Citibank loan (A$1.6m in FY26) sits in operating cash flow but not in EBITDA.

### Whose cash it is (question 1, continued)
The cash at bank is mostly money Helloworld holds for others: payments from agents and clients that it owes on to airlines, cruise lines and hotels. "Cash at bank and on hand" includes client cash, and "restricted cash" is held in entities with IATA requirements [H1, note 5.1]. The company's contracts let it delay paying suppliers until it has been paid [H1, note 3.3]. I measure Helloworld's own cash as cash and deposits less the net amount it holds for others: trade and other payables and deferred revenue, less trade and other receivables, accrued override commission and prepayments, then less any tax owed.

The day of the week moves cash and payables together (a Sunday year end holds a week of agents' money that is still owed to the airlines), so this measure doesn't swing with the calendar the way cash does. Over three years Helloworld used about A$118m of its own resources: Webjet shares (A$61.4m), acquisitions (Express Travel Group A$53.7m, MTA A$35.9m, Barlow A$10.7m, Gilpin A$7.5m, Phil Hoffmann A$4.4m, Brighton A$1.3m) and dividends (A$57.9m), against free cash flow (A$54.0m reported), sales of Corporate Travel Management shares (A$29.3m) and the A$35.0m Citibank loan [H7, H4, H1]. MTA shows the pattern: it came with A$25.3m of cash and A$23.9m of payables, so almost none of its cash was spare [H1, note 6.4.3].

After Crown: the A$101.25m of Crown's price not paid in shares is borrowed [H10, H13], so net debt on my measure is about A$140.6m before anything paid or earned since 30 June 2026. Crown will also bring its stock of foreign banknotes, which will show as cash but is the stock it trades, not spare money. From 31 December 2026 (a Thursday, after agents' Wednesday payments and before the Monday BSP payment) reported cash will look high again; the measure above is the one to watch.

### Crown Currency Exchange (question 2)
What is disclosed:
- Price A$135m for 100% (through KCE HoldCo Pty Ltd), 6.1 times Crown's FY26 underlying EBITDA of A$22m; 68 stores, about 200 staff; acquired by the vendor in 2019; Emily Palermo (CEO) and Greg Woolley (chairman) stay on [H10, H21].
- Funding: "a loan facility from NAB, a vendor share placement and a vendor loan facility" [H10]. The shares: 23,429,610 at an AUD equivalent of A$33.75m (A$1.44 a share), issued on 30 October 2026 under the 15% placement capacity [H13, H14]. ASX confirmed shareholder approval wasn't needed [H11]. Completion was 30 October 2026 [H12].
- FY27 guidance includes A$14m of underlying EBITDA from Crown for eight months [H15].

What isn't disclosed in any filing I read: the NAB facility's size, rate, term, security and covenants; the vendor loan's size, rate and repayment; whether NAB refinanced or sits alongside the A$35m Citibank revolver; Crown's revenue, store rents, capital spending and banknote stock; and whether A$22m is before or after store rents.
- The one source for the split is a secondary one: Quartr's AI-written summary of the 21 September 2026 investor call says 50% from a NAB facility, 25% in vendor shares and 25% through a vendor facility "repayable over three years" [S1]. The 25% in shares matches the filing (A$33.75m), so I use about A$67.5m from NAB and about A$33.75m from the vendor, repaid at about A$11.25m a year. Treat this as unverified.
- The Citibank facility: a secured A$35m revolver and a A$10m guarantee facility signed 30 June 2025, fully drawn, expiring 8 October 2028, with a general security agreement over all of Helloworld Travel Limited's property and two covenants tested each 31 December and 30 June: net debt no more than 2.5 times adjusted 12-month EBITDA, and EBITDA at least 2.5 times interest [H1, note 5.2.3]. Nothing filed says it has been repaid, so I assume total borrowings of about A$136.25m. A lender with security over everything would normally have to consent to another secured lender; how that was handled will show in the half-year accounts.
- Interest: Citibank cost about 6.3% in FY26 (A$1.59m on A$35m drawn from about 8 October 2025). At about 6.3% on the bank debt and 7% on the vendor loan (my assumption), interest is about A$8.8m a year, A$6.2m after tax.
- Covenants look comfortable as the bank is likely to measure them (cash including client money deducted): about (136 - 85) / 82, or 0.6 times. On my own-cash measure, net debt is about 2.0 to 2.2 times EBITDA after leases.

Crown's earnings after store rent. Helloworld reports under the lease standard (AASB 16), which puts rent below EBITDA, and its guidance of A$14m for eight months (A$21m a year) is on that basis [H15, H1 note 1]. So Crown's rent is not in the A$22m. Search results show Crown's stores listed in Westfield shopping centres [S3]. I assume A$100,000 a store a year including outgoings (A$60,000 to A$150,000), or A$7m (A$4m to A$10m). That is my assumption, not a disclosed figure, and it is the input that most changes what Crown is worth.

Does it earn its cost? On central figures, no: about 7% after tax on the A$135m paid, against the 11.5% post-tax rate Helloworld uses to test its own goodwill [H1, note 4.4.1]. On underlying earnings per share the deal is roughly neutral: about A$5.2m of profit after rent, about A$1m of depreciation, A$6.6m of interest and tax on central figures, before any amortisation of Crown's acquired intangibles, against 14.3% more shares (FY26 underlying EPS of 18.5c becomes about 18.9c; at A$10m of rent, about 17.8c).

### What else a quick reader would miss
- Dividends paid in FY26 (A$17.9m) used all of FY26's free cash (A$17.9m); FY25's (A$22.5m) were paid while free cash was minus A$21.0m as reported, or A$19.0m with the extra BSP payment put back [H1, H4]. At 10c a share on 187.1m shares the dividend now costs A$18.7m a year, before the vendor loan repayments.
- Book value is all intangibles: intangible assets A$336.1m against equity of A$318.6m at 30 June 2026, before Crown [H1].
- MTA's full year adds about A$3.1m of profit before tax to FY26 on a full-year basis (the company's pro forma A$13.3m against A$10.2m) [H1, note 6.4.4].
- 1,699,002 shares from the 2024 franchisee loyalty programme came out of escrow on 31 October 2026 [H33a].

## Valuation
Done from the filings and the peers' prices. Arithmetic in python3 -I (scratch scripts, not in the repo). Shares: 187,109,776 after the Crown issue.

  - Sell: into a BGH or other bid, or on market. A 20% stake in a A$117m company is hard to sell except to a bidder.
  - Nothing: the stake blocks anyone else from reaching 80% and compulsory acquisition (90%), so it gives Helloworld a say in any outcome.
- What it means for Helloworld's price: at market the stake is 12.8c a share, at the deep dive's value 9.2c. ## Why the market is wrong
On my numbers it is only partly wrong. At A$1.355, Helloworld's enterprise value on my measure (A$253.5m of shares plus A$140.6m of net debt, less A$19.9m of associates and A$23.9m of Webjet) is A$350m: 5.5 times FY26 pro forma EBITDA after leases (A$63.5m) and 5.1 times my middling FY27 figure (A$69.2m), against 8.7 to 9.2 times for Flight Centre and Web Travel. After capital spending the gap is wider (5.5 to 6.0 times against about 12 times).

Two plain reasons the price could be too low:
1. The reported cash flow looks poor (minus A$14.6m of operating cash in FY25) because of a calendar effect the company explained, and anyone screening on cash conversion sees a problem that isn't there.
2. Crown's earnings are reported before rent, the debt terms aren't public and the deal was done without a shareholder vote, so the market can't yet tell whether it pays; the price fell from A$1.40 to A$1.37 on the announcement and didn't move on the FY27 guidance (A$1.38 to A$1.37 on 16 October, Norgate). The half-year accounts will show both.

But the discount has been steady: at A$1.89 in early January 2026 (Norgate), before Crown, the core business was priced at about 5.6 times EBITDA after leases on my measure. The likely reasons are durable: three holders with 46%, a chief executive who has spent A$61m on a Webjet stake now worth about A$24m and borrowed A$101m for Crown, recurring "one-offs", and guidance delivered below the original range two years running. I don't expect that discount to close in four months, and over 24 months it needs a run of clean results.

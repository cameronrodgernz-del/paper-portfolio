## The company in plain words
Adairs sells homewares and furniture in Australia and New Zealand under three brands, each designing and sourcing its own range and selling through its own stores and websites [A1]:
- Adairs: bed linen, bedding, towels, cushions and homewares; 160 stores at June 2026 after leaving New Zealand, and 28.8% of sales online. More than 80% of its sales go to about one million paying "Linen Lovers" members [A3, A2].
- Mocka: online furniture and homewares, 97.5% online, with a first trial of three stores (Maroochydore, Tower Junction in Christchurch, and Mornington by Q3 FY27) [A3, A1]. About a third of Mocka's sales are in New Zealand (my estimate from the growth rates: Australia up 38.0%, New Zealand up 0.4%, total up 22.9%) [A1].
- Focus on Furniture: larger-ticket furniture and bedding, 27 showrooms mostly in Victoria, 6.7% online [A3].

Over 93% of products are made outside Australia, mainly in China, India and Bangladesh, and paid for largely in US dollars [A2, risk section].

Who runs it. Group CEO and managing director Elle Roseby (appointed executive director 22 January 2025) has resigned; her notice is expected to end in March 2027 [A5, A2]. Matt Edmonds became Group CFO on 9 February 2026 and from 29 September also "Operations Director" [A2, A5]. Rachel Taylor became Executive General Manager of the Adairs brand on 5 October 2026 [A5]. Focus has had a new CEO, Candice Deale, since November 2025, after its previous executive team left [A6, A1]. Trent Peterson chairs the board [A18].

Who owns it [A2, shareholder information at 5 August 2026]. The top 20 holders had 49.12%, mostly custodians. Substantial holders: Santa Lucia Asset Management 12,103,000 (6.78%, notice 10 April 2026), Paradice Investment Management 11,761,551 (6.73%, notice 5 September 2024), entities associated with BBFIT Investments (Brett Blundy) 9,107,996 (5.10%, 7 May 2026) and Dimensional 8,938,440 (5.00%, 7 July 2026). ## What the filings show

### The question: what "underlying" leaves out
"Underlying" EBIT is the company's own measure. It is struck after rent (it removes AASB 16 lease accounting) and leaves out what the company calls significant items [A3, Appendix 1 and 6]. Leaving aside lease accounting and the Focus write-down, the items left out have been:

| Year | Left out (pre-tax) | What it was for | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FY24 | A$4.9m | SaaS cloud software projects A$3.1m; move into the National Distribution Centre A$1.9m | [A9] |
| FY25 | A$9.0m | SaaS projects A$4.4m (including the start of the Adairs ERP); leadership change A$1.6m (former CEO's notice period and executive redundancies); head office and Focus office and warehouse moves A$2.1m; warehouse system change A$0.8m | [A9, A10] |
| FY26 | A$21.2m | ERP and other SaaS projects A$18.6m (A$15.7m of development spending plus A$2.8m of old software written off); exit from Adairs New Zealand A$2.5m | [A3, A2] |
| FY27 (company) | about A$5m | "Adairs technology upgrade ~$5m remaining" | [A3] |

The exact totals are A$4.932m, A$8.974m and A$21.154m; the fact sheet's A$8.9m and A$21.1m are the same figures rounded down.

What the software money is. The Adairs "data and technology upgrade" replaces the old ERP with Microsoft Dynamics 365: a A$25m to A$30m program "rolling out through FY25–FY28" [A8]. Because the new system is rented software (SaaS), the build costs can't be capitalised and are expensed; the company calls them costs "that would have been classified as software development capital expenditure under previous accounting guidance" [A8]. The online channel moved to the new platform in July 2026 and the main go-live ("Project Compass") was achieved on 16 September 2026 [A1, A5].

Has the company said when they stop? Yes, for the ERP: about A$5m remains in FY27 [A3], and the July update said total program costs are "in line with overall guidance" [A4]. Two things are less clear. First, the FY26 results say the phases after go-live "are enhancements that enable new capability including RFID and Deliver from Store" [A1], and the original plan ran to FY28 [A8]; the filings don't say whether those phases sit inside the A$5m. Second, Focus is replatforming its website and CRM [A3], and the filings don't say how that will be accounted for.

Are any of them really running costs? The company has said the new system's running cost, about A$1.1m a year, "will add to CODB" inside underlying profit [A3], and in August 2025 that "annual operating costs post-implementation will be expensed as normal in underlying operating costs" [A8]. That is the right treatment. But three things count against reading underlying profit as the real profit:
- The "SaaS project" line has been used every year, including FY24 before the ERP began (A$3.1m) [A9]. A retailer this size will keep spending on software, and under these rules some of it will always be expensed. Spreading the ERP's A$25m to A$30m over about eight years is A$3m to A$4m a year.
- The non-software items (moves, leadership changes, the New Zealand exit) are different each year but average A$3.0m a year over FY24 to FY26 [A9, A2]. FY27 will bring CEO search and transition costs and the Focus turnaround.
- The items left out have tended to come in above the company's own guide. On 23 June 2025, a week before year-end, FY25's were guided at about A$6.2m to A$7.2m (SaaS about A$3m to A$4m, warehouse system A$0.6m, moves about A$1.3m, leadership about A$1.3m); all four came in higher, A$9.0m in total [A12, A9]. FY26 development spending was guided at A$13m to A$15m and came in at A$15.7m, plus A$2.8m of write-offs [A8, A3]. The New Zealand exit came in below its guide (A$2.5m against A$3.5m to A$4.0m) [A4, A3].

One more point: executives' short-term bonuses are set on underlying EBIT [A2, remuneration report], so leaving costs out of it raises the measure the bonus is paid on. No FY26 bonus was paid: the board's minimum was A$58.0m and the result was A$55.0m [A2].

My answer to the question: once the ERP is finished, underlying profit overstates the real run rate by about A$5m a year before tax (A$3m of software and A$2m of restructuring that keeps coming back), not by the A$15m to A$21m of FY25 and FY26. On that basis FY26 EBIT after rent was about A$49.7m: A$55.0m, plus the A$0.8m New Zealand loss that has gone, less the A$1.1m ERP running cost and the A$5m allowance.

### Leases, debt and cash [A2, A3]
- EBIT after rent: underlying EBIT of A$55.0m is already struck after rent. The company's lease adjustment adds A$59.3m of rent back to other costs; cash paid for leases was A$64.7m (A$49.0m of principal and A$15.7m of interest). The gap of about A$5.4m most likely sits in warehouse costs (the statutory cost of sales carries A$4.5m of depreciation and A$1.6m of interest), so underlying EBIT appears to carry the full rent, but the filings don't show the split.
- EBITDA: A$68.7m after rent; about A$128m before rent (A$68.7m plus the A$59.3m add-back).
- Leases: liabilities A$230.9m (A$51.7m current), against right-of-use assets of A$199.8m. Undiscounted payments are A$278.8m, of which A$64.8m fall due within a year.
- Bank debt: A$59.0m drawn less A$11.4m of cash is net debt of A$47.6m (FY25 A$67.6m), 0.7 times EBITDA after rent. Facilities are A$135m: A$90m maturing July 2028 and A$45m July 2029. Facility A, the drawn one, costs BBSW plus 2.15% and matures 14 July 2028; Facility D (BBSW plus 2.30%) matures 14 July 2029 and is undrawn. Another A$12m multi-option line had A$5.8m used for guarantees. Unused facilities total A$82.2m. Interest rates aren't hedged. The company expects to meet its covenants for at least 12 months. No debt falls due before July 2028.
- Cash flow: statutory operating cash flow was A$98.2m, less A$49.0m of lease principal and A$15.6m of capital spending, leaving A$33.6m. That is already after the A$15.7m of ERP spending, which sits in operating cash flow, and it includes a A$14.0m working capital release. FY25 on the same basis was A$16.9m, after an A$8.7m working capital build. Dividends paid were A$13.4m.
- FY27: capital spending "up to $25m" (about half not yet committed), the last A$5m of ERP, about 75% of US dollar purchases hedged at 67.4 US cents (FY26 66.4c). The A$10.7m fall in ERP spending is mostly offset by up to A$9.4m more capital spending, so FY27 cash flow won't rise much.
- Balance sheet: equity A$171.4m, of which goodwill and brands are A$208.7m, so tangible equity is negative. Current liabilities exceed current assets by A$34.7m once current lease liabilities are counted, which is normal for a retailer. The auditor, EY, gave a clean opinion; its key audit matters were inventory and the intangibles. Since the reporting date the directors report no significant matters other than the final dividend.

### Focus on Furniture after the write-down [A2 note 10, A4]
- The A$63.5m impairment wrote off all A$41.0m of Focus goodwill and cut its brand from A$37.0m to A$14.4m.
- The company's own estimate of what the whole Focus business is worth, excluding leases (brand plus net tangible operating assets), was about A$25m to A$31m in July 2026 [A4]. Its test assumes five-year revenue growth of 2.2% a year and EBITDA growth of 0.6% a year, discounted at 18.0% before tax with 2.25% long-term growth. Read backwards, A$25m to A$31m at 18.0% less 2.25% is a cash flow of about A$3.9m to A$4.9m a year after rent, close to FY26's A$3.8m EBIT plus A$1.8m of depreciation less a little capital spending.
- On current trading Focus is losing money (about A$2.0m of EBIT in H2 FY26, and a weaker Q1 FY27). The carrying value now equals the recoverable amount, so "any adverse change in a key assumption would result in a further impairment" [A2]. A 0.5-point lower revenue growth rate alone cuts the value by A$4.6m.
- So Focus is worth something only if the turnaround works. The company's own figure is the good case (about A$28m). Half of it, about A$15m, is the middle case, and nothing is the bad case. It could be worth less than nothing if the showrooms had to be closed, given their leases; the filings don't give the Focus lease liability, though the segment's total liabilities are A$105.7m [A2 note 3].

### Method 2: owner cash flow, valued as a growing stream
Owner cash = EBIT after rent x (1 - 30%), less net investment of A$4m (capital spending of about A$17.6m a year against A$13.6m of depreciation for Adairs and Mocka; FY26 group capital spending was A$15.6m and FY27's is up to A$25m), less A$1m of working capital. Middle case: 46.8 x 0.7 - 4 - 1 = A$27.8m. Value = owner cash x (1 + growth) / (discount rate - growth), plus Focus, less A$52.6m:

| Earnings case (owner cash) | 12% and 1.5% growth | 11% and 2% | 10% and 2.5% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low (A$24.3m) | A$1.01 | A$1.23 | A$1.55 |
| Middle (A$27.8m) | A$1.28 | A$1.54 | A$1.90 |
| High (A$31.3m) | A$1.54 | A$1.83 | A$2.23 |

Worked example, middle at 11% and 2%: 27.76 x 1.02 / 0.09 = A$314.6m, plus A$15m, less A$52.6m = A$277.0m, or A$1.54 a share. The discount rate is for the business after rent, so it carries the lease leverage; 11% is a fair rate for a small, lease-heavy retailer, and 12% is just as defensible.

Cross-check: the company's own discount rates. Adairs' impairment test uses 13.6% before tax and Mocka's 15.8%, both with 2.25% growth [A2]. On the middle earnings that gives 35.8 / 0.1135 + 11.0 / 0.1355 = A$396.6m, plus A$15m, less A$52.6m: A$1.99 a share. The company's rates are more generous than mine.

Range: A$1.10 to A$2.15 a share, central about A$1.58 (the middle of Method 1 at 7.0x, A$1.61, and of Method 2 at 11%, A$1.54).
- The low end (A$1.10) needs Adairs' earnings to slip (a bad ERP cutover over Christmas or weaker demand), Focus to be worth nothing, and the market to keep paying a third less than peers.
- The high end (about A$2.13 to A$2.23) needs either the middle earnings at peer multiples or the high earnings in the cash flow method, which means Adairs holding its second-half FY26 margins and Focus recovering to the company's own forecast.
- The input that moves it most is the multiple, or in Method 2 the discount rate: each 1.0x on middle earnings is worth 26 cents a share.

## Why the market is wrong
1. The price assumes the "underlying" exclusions never end and Focus is worth nothing. At A$1.33 the market pays A$292.4m for the business including net debt and the last ERP spend. Take off A$15m for Focus and that is 5.9 times my all-in middle EBIT for Adairs and Mocka, which already allows A$5m a year for the costs that keep being left out. Listed peers trade at about 9.5 times. Put the other way, the price is my low-earnings case at 7 times, or the middle case at under 6 times. The ERP's main spending is finished (go-live on 16 September 2026, about A$5m left), and statutory and underlying profit should come much closer together from FY27.
2. The forced seller has gone and informed buyers have stepped in. The index exit in March 2026 moved 5.2% of the company in one day. Since then Brett Blundy's companies have bought A$11.3m of shares at an average of A$1.28, Santa Lucia Asset Management has built to 6.78%, and the chair bought A$377k at A$1.25 a week after the CEO resigned.

Why this probably won't show by March 2027:
- The first-half result will be compared with H1 FY26's A$30.0m, which included A$5.8m from Focus. On my middle case, Focus earns about nil in the half, Adairs about A$20.5m and Mocka about A$6m, so the headline will be a fall of about 12% (A$26.5m against A$30.0m) even if Adairs and Mocka do well.
- The CEO search won't be finished when the result comes out, and a new CEO often resets expectations.
- Home furnishing demand is falling across the sector [P1, P5], and the ERP is going through its first peak season.

So the market is probably wrong about the value but may well be right about the next four months. The case is better over ten months, through the FY27 result in August 2027 with the interim dividend in the window, than over four.

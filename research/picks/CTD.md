# Corporate Travel Management Limited (ASX:CTD): deep dive, the case for buying

Written 2026-11-09 by Claude for the one-company-at-a-time process (rulebook section 5a, docs/RESEARCH_PROCESS.md). This is the case for buying.
Price at writing: A$3.61 (last sale, ASX company data, read 9 November 2026) [S16]. Market value: about A$501.6m (138,936,922 shares on issue [S17]). No bank debt drawn at 30 June 2026 and A$106.9m of cash, but about A$172m of refunds still to pay to UK customers [S1, S3].
Status: finished. Not a value pick at A$3.61 (see "Where it lands"). This is a simulation and not financial advice.

Order of work: chosen on 9 November 2026 as a company sold for reasons other than a weak balance sheet (it left the S&P/ASX 200 and 300 when it came back from suspension). The price was in view from the start. Under the 9 November process change, the bad case was roughed out from the filings first. It came out inside the 30% limit, so the work went on to the evidence outside the filings.

## Where it lands
Wait. The business has come through the scandal in better shape than the share price said in September, and the evidence outside the filings backs that up: the UK government, the customer it overcharged, has kept giving it new contracts. But the market caught up in October. The shares have risen 55% since they came back on 3 September, and at A$3.61 the business is valued at about 5 times EBITDA, in line with Flight Centre. That's roughly what the evidence supports, not less.

On my numbers the middling case is about +20% to August 2028 and the expected gain after costs about +10%, below the 12.9% minimum. The bad case is 29% below the price, just inside the limit, but the middling gain falls well short of the 44% the value test then asks for. It qualifies at about A$3.25. The cheap moment was September, between A$2.00 and A$2.60, while index funds and big holders sold; the weekly sweep found it two months late.

No case against has been written, because the case for doesn't pass (process step 4, as changed on 9 November).

## What the price assumes
The business is valued at about A$550m: A$501.6m of shares, about A$30m of net debt once the refunds are paid (the company's own forecast, below), and about A$20m for the fee owed to its new lender when the loan ends (4% of the market value) [S5].

FY26 underlying EBITDA was A$113.6m [S1]. On that the business is valued at about 4.8 times. Flight Centre, the closest listed peer, is valued at about the same (A$2.49bn of shares, about A$240m of net cash at December 2025, A$466m of FY26 underlying EBITDA) [S20, S21]. Flight Centre's leisure arm had a poor year, which holds its multiple down.

Profit tells the same story. FY26 underlying profit after tax, worked from the company's figures, was about A$47m, or 34c a share [S3]. The workings: A$113.6m of EBITDA less A$9.7m and A$2.1m of depreciation and A$32.6m of software amortisation, plus A$2.2m of interest received and less A$3.6m of finance costs; tax at about 28%; and about A$1.75m for minority holders. The price is 10.7 times that. But for FY27 and FY28 the new loan costs about A$20m a year in cash interest [S5], which takes profit to about A$33m (24c) and the multiple to about 15 times.

So the price assumes FY26's earnings hold, with no growth, and no reward yet for the governance clean-up. It doesn't assume the business shrinks.

## The one thing the market is missing
Stated as the rulebook asks: the market priced CTM in September as if its customers would leave after the overcharging scandal. The public contract records show the opposite: the UK government kept awarding it work through 2026, and the Australian Government's audit found no overcharging. But the price has since risen to reflect that, so the gap that was there in September has mostly closed.

## The test outside the filings
Done on 9 November 2026, under docs/RESEARCH_PROCESS.md step 2. The questions the filings can't answer:
1. Are customers leaving after the scandal?
2. How much of FY26's recovery in Europe is temporary government work?
3. How big is the class action risk?
4. Who sold, and is the forced selling over?

What the evidence showed:

1. Customers aren't leaving; the public contract records show the opposite.
   - In the UK, the Ministry of Justice made a direct award to CTM for travel and accommodation, worth up to £170m, in February 2026, at the height of the scandal [S22]. Its own spend control records approved the deal on 20 October 2025 [S22].
   - Other UK public bodies did the same during the scandal: ACAS (£2.1m to December 2029), Ordnance Survey (£3.46m to January 2030), VisitBritain (£5m, August 2026) and others, all under the government's own travel frameworks [S22].
   - The Home Office, the Scottish Government and the Cabinet Office extended their contracts to August and September 2027, about £105m of transactions [S9, S23].
   - In Australia, the Department of Finance's independent audit of CTM's whole-of-government contract found "no evidence of widespread or systemic overcharging", with a rating of "No Major Concern" [S24].
   - On AusTender, federal agencies are still recording their CTM contracts to 30 June 2027, and several added to them in August and September 2026 [S25]. The contract began on 1 July 2023 for four years, with three one-year options to 2030 [S24]. The first option is the decision to watch.

   Verdict: the customer base held. The company's own figure, 97% of transaction value retained into FY26 [S2], is consistent with the public records.
2. Part of Europe's recovery is temporary.
   - Europe's EBITDA went from minus A$1.2m in FY25 to A$24.7m in FY26 [S1], mostly from "special projects" for the UK government that began in the second half of FY25 [S2]:
     - asylum accommodation and travel for the Home Office, about £65m of transactions to August 2027 [S9];
     - accommodation for the Afghan Resettlement Programme for the Ministry of Defence, about £28m in the first six months, under a contract capped at £170.3m to March 2029 [S8, S23].
   - Both are shrinking. The Home Office says it is determined to close asylum hotels [S23]. Afghan resettlement arrivals fell 48% to 3,840 in the year to June 2026, fewer than 9,000 people are left to come, and hotels are closing [S26].
   - In FY24, which also had some special project work, Europe earned A$16.6m [S2].

   Verdict: about A$8m to A$12m of Europe's EBITDA is likely to fade by FY28. That's in the bad and middling cases.
3. The class action risk is real but shared.
   - Phi Finney McDonald says it is "well advanced" in investigating a shareholder class action against CTM and its former auditor, PwC, over the accounts up to FY24 [S27]. None has been filed, and the company says no material claims have been brought [S3].
   - Shareholders lost most of about A$2.3bn when the shares fell from A$16.07 before the suspension to A$2.32 on return [S18].
   - Any settlement would likely take years, would be shared with PwC, and may be partly insured. I've allowed A$10m to A$60m of value for it across the cases, as the market would price it once filed.
4. The forced selling is over, and informed money bought.
   - AustralianSuper, State Street and ECP sold out in the first days after the return [S19].
   - The founder, Jamie Pherous, who left as managing director in February 2026, bought 2.4m shares on 4 September, taking his stake from 11.34% to 13.68% [S19, S3].
   - Pinnacle's managers (including Antipodes) built 11.2% by 4 November, DNR Capital crossed 5% on 16 October, and First Sentier took 6.3% [S19].
   - Short positions fell from 7.3% during the suspension to 1.6% by mid-September and are about 2.4% now (ASIC daily reports) [S17].
   - Rejoining the S&P/ASX 300 isn't close. CTD's A$495m at 30 October was larger than only 11 current members (my count from Norgate prices and ASIC share counts), and new members need to sit well up the list.

What this changes:
- It confirms what the price now assumes. It backs the franchise, which makes the worst cases less likely, and shows Europe's government work shrinking, which keeps the middling case modest.
- There's no remaining gap between the evidence and the price at A$3.61. There was one at A$2.00 to A$2.60 in September.

## The company in plain words
CTM books and manages business travel for companies and governments in Australia and New Zealand, North America, Asia and Europe [S3]. It earns fees and commissions on about A$9.8bn of travel a year (FY26) [S1].

FY26 underlying EBITDA by region [S1]:
- North America A$62.3m, about flat;
- Australia and New Zealand A$39.2m, up 53% (it is the sole travel manager for the Australian Government);
- Europe A$24.7m, from a loss;
- Asia A$15.8m.

The scandal, in short:
- August 2025: shares suspended at A$16.07 before the FY25 result, after problems were found in the UK business's accounts and its charging of customers [S18, S4].
- FY25 was restated, and goodwill was written down: a loss of A$348.5m [S1]. FY24's EBITDA was restated from the originally reported figure to A$116.4m [S2].
- February 2026: the founder stepped down as managing director. Ana Pedersen became acting chief executive, and was appointed permanently in July [S10].
- August 2026: refund agreements were reached with UK customers covering 86% of the expected liability [S4]. A A$175m loan from Pacific Equity Partners' credit arm was arranged [S5], and the FY25 and FY26 accounts were lodged.
- 3 September 2026: the shares returned and closed at A$2.32 [S18].

The FY26 accounts carry a qualified audit opinion, but only on the opening balances of the European business, which the auditor couldn't verify. There is no going-concern uncertainty [S3].

## What the filings show
FY26 [S1, S3]:
- revenue and other income A$669.9m (up 4%);
- underlying EBITDA A$113.6m (FY25 A$83.6m, FY24 restated A$116.4m);
- net profit A$17.7m after A$29.7m of one-off costs, mostly forensic and restatement work;
- operating cash flow only A$29.6m, after A$23m of refunds, about A$40m tied up when IATA moved CTM to daily settlement, and the one-off costs [S2, S3].

Balance sheet at 30 June 2026 [S3]:
- cash A$106.9m, including A$15.8m of client money;
- no borrowings;
- A$211.2m of customer liabilities;
- a A$49.2m tax refund owed to it;
- negative net tangible assets.

Refunds to settled customers are scheduled at A$141.7m in FY27 and A$30.3m in FY28 [S3].

The company's own cash plan, Q1 FY27 to Q2 FY28 [S2]:
- money in: A$107m of cash, A$62m of tax refunds, A$36m of other net cash flow, and up to A$175m of new debt;
- money out: A$246m of customer refunds;
- debt drawn peaks at about A$140m, with about A$99m of cash at December 2027.

Since then, IATA has returned the A$65m guarantee and the old settlement terms, which frees about A$30m [S7]. So own net debt at the end of 2027 should be around A$30m.

The new loan [S5, S3]:
- A$175m from PEP Credit, secured, at the bank bill rate plus a margin;
- about A$20m a year of cash interest in FY27 and FY28;
- matures 1 July 2028 and is expected to run to 25 August 2029;
- a make-whole payment if repaid in the first two years, and a fee of 4% of the market value when it ends;
- leverage and interest cover covenants. EBITDA would have to fall about A$40m before one is breached [S3].

Trading since: July 2026 transactions rose to 1.6m from 1.5m, but revenue fell to A$53.3m from A$58.3m [S1]. New business won in FY27 to 1 September was A$178m [S1]. FY27 guidance comes at the AGM on 12 November [S1, S11].

## Valuation
Worked at August 2028, after the FY28 result, about 22 months out. The method is a multiple of underlying EBITDA (as the company reports it, after leases), cross-checked against Flight Centre at about 4.8 times.

From each value I deduct:
- about A$30m of net debt;
- about A$20m for the lender's exit fee;
- about A$15m for the minority holders' share;
- a case-by-case amount for the class action.

| Case | FY28 EBITDA | Multiple | Business value | Less debt, fee, minorities, class action | A$ a share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Worse | A$85m | 4.5x | A$383m | A$125m | 1.85 |
| Bad | A$100m | 5.0x | A$500m | A$105m | 2.84 |
| Middling | A$115m | 6.0x | A$690m | A$90m | 4.32 |
| Good | A$132m | 7.0x | A$924m | A$75m | 6.11 |

Arithmetic for the middling case:
- EBITDA: FY26's A$113.6m, with about A$10m of fading government work replaced by growth elsewhere (new wins, the Amadeus partnership's savings [S6]), gives about A$115m.
- Value: 6 times is A$690m. Less A$90m is A$600m, or A$4.32 a share.

The middling multiple is above Flight Centre's because CTM is a pure business travel company and the governance discount should narrow once the refunds are paid. At Flight Centre's 4.8 times, the middling case would be about A$3.32, below today's price.

What each depends on most: the multiple the market will pay once the scandal is over. Each turn of EBITDA is worth about A$0.83 a share in the middling case.

## Why the market was wrong, and isn't now
In September the market priced CTM as a business losing customers, while funds that had to sell (index funds and holders who'd waited out a 13-month suspension) sold. The public contract records showed customers staying, the founder bought, and short sellers covered. The re-rating that followed IATA's release on 20 October (the shares rose from A$2.48 to A$3.82 in three days [S18]) priced most of that in.

## Outcomes and chances
Resolve date: 31 August 2028, after the FY28 result (661 days away). Dividends are suspended, and none are assumed.

| Outcome | What happens | Chance | Price by resolve date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Worse | Government work ends, the Australian contract isn't extended, a class action is filed for a large sum, and the market keeps a deep discount | 10% | A$1.85 |
| Bad | The UK special projects fade and nothing replaces them; the class action is filed | 25% | A$2.84 |
| Middling | Earnings hold at about FY26's level as growth replaces government work; refunds are paid and the loan is refinanced | 45% | A$4.32 |
| Good | Growth and the Amadeus savings lift EBITDA to about A$132m, the scandal fades, and dividends restart | 20% | A$6.11 |

These chances were set after the evidence from outside the filings. The UK and Australian government records are why the worse case is only 10%. The shrinking asylum and Afghan programmes are why the middling case is no better than flat.

For the card, which takes three outcomes, the first two combine into one bad case: 35% at A$2.56 (the chance-weighted average of A$1.85 and A$2.84). The card would read 35% at A$2.56, 45% at A$4.32 and 20% at A$6.11.

Expected price: 0.35 × 2.557 + 0.45 × 4.32 + 0.20 × 6.11 = A$4.06, or +12.5% before costs. After about 2% of costs it's about +10.5%. The costs are A$15 brokerage each way on about A$2,250, half the 0.3% spread each way, and the 0.5% currency fee. The rulebook minimum over 661 days is 12.9%, so that test fails.

The value test (rulebook section 5a):
- The bad case of A$2.56 is 29.2% below A$3.61, inside the 30% limit.
- Against that fall the middling case would need to be +43.8%, and it's +19.7%.
- So the test fails, on the middling case.

It passes at about A$3.25. There the bad case is 21% below, the middling case is +33% (it needs +32%), and the expected gain after costs is about +23%.

## What would make this wrong (in either direction)
For the better:
- FY27 guidance at the AGM above about A$120m of EBITDA, with the government work holding up.
- The whole-of-government contract extended early.
- The market going back to valuing CTM at a premium to Flight Centre, as it did for a decade before the scandal.

For the worse:
- A class action filed for a large sum.
- The Home Office work ending in August 2027 with nothing to replace it.
- July's 9% fall in revenue turning out to be a trend, not seasonal.

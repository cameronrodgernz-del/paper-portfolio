# Dexus Convenience Retail REIT (ASX:DXC): deep dive. Not a value pick: on cash earnings after maintenance, incentives and the Dexus fee (AFFO) it yields less than Waypoint REIT and about the same as other externally managed REITs; the bad case (A$2.25) is 18% below the price and the middling case (A$2.73) about 1% below it

Fact sheet: research/facts/DXC.md, grade A as a value pick, only just. The question it set: are the valuers right that the 91 sites are worth A$779m (a 6.18% cap rate), and does an external manager taking about A$4.8m a year mean holders never see that value?

Value pick (rulebook section 5a, from 8 Nov 2026): no. The bad case (A$2.25) is 18.5% below the price (the limit is 10%) and the middling case (A$2.73) is 1.1% below it (it needs +25%). Counting the seven quarterly distributions to 30 June 2028 (36.6c), the middling case is 12.2% above the price, still well short. As an ordinary research pick the events inside six months (December valuations, the 8 February 2027 half-year) can't by themselves move the price to NTA. No card.

> In short: DXC earned 19.33c of AFFO a security in FY26 (FFO 20.9c less A$1.76m of maintenance capital spending and A$0.40m of leasing incentives; FFO is already after the A$4.8m Dexus base fee). At A$2.73 that is a 7.1% cash yield, and about 6.85% on FY27, when management says FFO will fall a little short of the 20.9c distribution. So DXC is about 10% dearer than Waypoint on cash earnings, not cheaper. Against other externally managed ASX REITs its FFO yield (7.5% on FY27) is the peer median (7.4%) and its price to NTA (0.71 times) is in line (0.70 to 0.74). The 6.18% cap rate is roughly supported by sales (market deals at 6.26% in the first half of 2026, DXC's own 25 sales since 2022 at about 1.5% below book on average), but the direction is up, not down: the market yield rose about 20 basis points in that half while DXC's valuers held 6.18%. The distribution has exceeded AFFO for two years (108% in FY26, 111% in FY25). MA Financial is not building a stake: it bought nothing since March and crossed 5% only because the buy-back cancelled securities. Nothing on the calendar turns the A$3.88 NTA into cash; that needs a bid, a sale of the portfolio or internalisation, each of which needs Dexus's agreement. As a listed trust DXC is worth about A$2.45 to A$2.90, central A$2.75. No card.

## The company in plain words
DXC is three stapled trusts owning 91 service station and convenience sites valued at A$779m at 30 June 2026 (metro 64 sites A$491m, highway 9 A$180m, regional 18 A$108m), 77% on the east coast [D2]. Rent is A$49.3m a year; property income after property costs was A$44.7m in FY26 [D1, D2]. It has no staff: Dexus Asset Management, a Dexus subsidiary, is the responsible entity and another Dexus company is the investment manager [D1]. Pat De Maria has been fund manager since September 2025, when Jason Weate moved to run Dexus Industria [D13]. Holders: 4,603; top 20 hold 51.94%, mostly custodians; Dexus's APD Trust is fourth at 7.38% [D1]. Five-year record from the filings read: FFO 20.7c (FY25) and 20.9c (FY26); NTA A$3.64 then A$3.86; no equity raised; 25 assets sold since 2022 [D1, D2, D5].

## Why big investors may be overlooking it
Small (A$369m), not in the S&P/ASX 300 at 30 October 2026 (data/norgate/index_members.csv), externally managed, and trading a median A$380,000 a day against Waypoint's A$2.9m (Norgate, 20 sessions to 30 October). ### The Dexus fee, the holders, the buy-back and what holders can do
- Fees to Dexus in FY26 [D1 note 16]: base management fee A$4.80m (0.65% a year of gross assets up to A$0.5bn, 0.60% from A$0.5bn to A$1.0bn), property management about 2% of gross income (A$0.97m payable), custody A$0.15m: about A$5.9m, 4.4c a security. Because the base fee is charged on gross assets at book, it is 1.3% of DXC's market value a year, and a buy-back funded with debt doesn't reduce it. DXC's total running cost is 0.75% of assets against Waypoint's 0.31% [W2]. Run at Waypoint's ratio DXC would save about A$3.3m a year (2.5c, about 35c a security at a 7% yield) before paying Dexus anything to give up the contract; the filings I read don't state what that would cost.
- Holders who could move the price: Dexus 9.3% on its own account plus 2.9% in client funds [D1, D10]; MA Financial's funds 5.05% now, passive since March (correction 1) [D9]; Charter Hall's funds held 5.29% in March 2025 and fell below 5% by May 2025 [D11]. Directors bought on market only once in the year: Jonathan Sweeney, 35,500 in February 2026 (fact sheet source 14; his holding rose from 20,000 to 55,500 [D1 note 16]). No proposal, review or approach has been announced.
- Buy-back: since 9 March 2026, 4,266,404 securities (3.1%) for about A$11.55m, about 62% of the 5% target; 1.10m in September, 0.61m in October and 90,598 on 2 to 5 November [D8]. Over the same period DXC returned +4.9% and Waypoint -4.8% (above), so it may be holding the price up relative to its peer; it has not closed the gap to NTA.
- Removing the responsible entity: in a listed scheme an ordinary resolution (more than half the votes cast) is enough; the extraordinary resolution in s601FM applies only to unlisted schemes, and in a listed scheme the responsible entity and its associates may vote their units on their own removal (City Pacific v Bacon (No 2) [2009] FCA 772, quoting s601FM and s253E) [L1]. Members with 5% of the votes can require a meeting (ss252B and 252D, as summarised in Wellington Capital v Premium Income Fund Action Group [2011] FCA 781) [L2]; MA alone has that. Dexus would vote its 12.2% against.
- A bid: a trust scheme needs a special resolution (75% of votes cast) to amend the constitution and, for a transfer scheme, an ordinary resolution under item 7 of s611, with the bidder and its associates unable to vote in favour, and the responsible entity has to put it forward (Takeovers Panel Guidance Note 15) [L3]. A takeover bid is the other route; Dexus's 12.2% sits above the 10% that would block compulsory acquisition at 90% (s661A; a standard rule I didn't re-read in the statute). Either way a deal needs Dexus's agreement in practice.

## Valuation
Shares 133.49m after the November buy-backs; NTA A$3.88 after buy-backs since June (A$3.86 reported). Worked in python3 -I.

### Method 1: cash earnings at what the market pays peers
- FY27 FFO of about 20.5c at the externally managed peers' median FFO yield of 7.42%: A$2.76 (A$2.62 to A$2.90 across the 7.08% to 7.82% range).
- FY27 AFFO of about 18.7c at Waypoint's 7.77%: A$2.41; at DXI's AFFO proxy of 6.2%: A$3.00.
- Central A$2.70. Each 0.1c of FFO moves it about 1.3c.

### Method 2: NTA, marked to sales and priced like peers
- Book A$3.88. Marked to the sales evidence (sites sell about 1.5% below book) A$3.80; if the December valuations follow the market's 20 basis points, A$3.62. If the whole portfolio were sold, take off about 1.5% for selling costs: about A$3.55 to A$3.70. That value needs a sale or a bid.
- As a listed trust, at the peers' 0.70 to 0.75 times NTA: A$2.72 to A$2.91; at the median 0.72: A$2.79.

### Range
As a listed trust DXC is worth about A$2.45 to A$2.90, central about A$2.75: its cash earnings priced like peers' and its NTA at the peers' multiple. The low end is DXC priced like Waypoint on AFFO; the high end is DXI's AFFO yield or the top of the NTA multiples. A$3.45 to A$3.70 (0.9 to 0.95 times NTA) is the value only if someone buys the portfolio or the trust, which needs Dexus's agreement.

## Why the market is wrong
On the measure it uses, it isn't. DXC is priced at the same cash yield as externally managed peers and at a lower one than Waypoint, which owns the same kind of sites without an external fee. The discount to NTA is the sector's (peers 0.70 to 0.75 times), and DXC's version has two extra reasons: a fee equal to 1.3% of its market value every year, and a distribution that has run above cash earnings for two years and is guided to do so again in FY27. The buy-back is accretive at 0.71 times NTA, but it is small (about A$7m left), it is funded with debt that the fund-through commitments also need, and so far it seems to have held DXC up against Waypoint rather than closed the gap. A +25% middling case needs a mechanism this file can't date: a bid, a sale of the portfolio with cash returned, or internalisation, all of which run through Dexus.

## What would make this wrong
"No case" is wrong if the price rises well above the A$2.73 middling value. The likeliest ways:
1. Dexus decides to exit. A sale of the trust, the portfolio or the management rights (or a merger with Waypoint) at 0.9 times NTA would be worth about A$3.49. Dexus's 9.3% and the buy-back broker's parent re-crossing 5% are not signs of this on their own (correction 1), and nothing in the filings points to it, but Dexus controls the route, so it could come without warning.
2. Rates fall and listed property re-rates. DXC traded at A$2.95 in November 2025 (Norgate), 0.81 times its NTA then; a return to 0.80 times A$3.90 is A$3.12, +13%, before distributions.

And for anyone who did buy, it gets worse if the December valuations soften, if FY27 FFO falls further below the distribution than "modestly", or if the fund-throughs and the buy-back push gearing towards 35% as the FY28 hedges reset higher.

Exit rule: no position is taken. If one were, it should be sold at the next open after FY27 or FY28 distribution guidance below 20.9c, NTA below A$3.60 at a reported balance date, or gearing above 37%, and otherwise on 31 August 2028.

Reopen with a fresh deep dive if: Dexus or the board announces a strategic review, a sale, an internalisation or a merger, or any party announces an approach; a holder other than Dexus goes above 10% with a stated intention, or a holder requisitions a meeting; or the price falls to about A$2.18 or below while FY27 and FY28 distribution guidance stays at 20.9c and NTA stays at A$3.75 or more (at that price the middling case is 25% above and the bad case within 10%).

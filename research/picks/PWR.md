## The company in plain words
Peter Warren runs new and used car dealerships on Australia's east coast: 80+ franchise operations for more than 30 car makers, at 25 locations, mostly in Greater Sydney (Warwick Farm, Macarthur, the North Shore) with others in regional NSW, Queensland and Victoria [P1, P12]. It has two segments. Vehicle Retailing sells new and used cars, servicing, parts, finance and insurance commissions and accessories. Property owns the Warwick Farm automall in south-west Sydney and the Southport sites on the Gold Coast, and charges the dealerships rent for them [P1, note 4]. All revenue is earned in Australia [P1, note 5].

Where the money comes from in FY26 (to 30 June 2026) [P1, note 5]: new and demonstrator vehicles A$1,559.5m (63% of revenue), used vehicles A$417.7m, parts A$286.6m, service A$155.4m, finance and insurance A$37.0m and accessories A$32.1m. Most of the profit comes from the back end: parts, service, finance and other "mixed business" made 60.4% of gross profit [P3, p. 7].

Who runs it: Paul Warren became Executive Chair on 1 July 2026 when the independent chair, John Ingram, retired; Niran Peiris is Deputy Chair; Andrew Doyle is CEO; Anna Bail became CFO on 1 March 2026 [P1]. The board is four directors: Paul Warren, Niran Peiris, Catherine West and John Eastham [P1, corporate directory].

Who owns it, at 27 July 2026 [P1, shareholder information]: The Warren Family Pty Ltd 20.98% and WF Property Holdings Pty Ltd 19.22% (together the Warren family, 69,284,167 shares, 40.22%), S M A Motors Pty Limited 9.12%, Citicorp Nominees 9.03%, Bond Street Custodians 3.61%, UBS Nominees 3.42%, Warbont Nominees 1.85%, HSBC Custody 1.76%, Ja & Ra Eastham Pty Ltd (director John Eastham) 1.21%. The top 20 hold 78.97%. Substantial holders listed: the Warren family 40.22%, S.M.A Motors 9.12%, Regal Funds Management 8.29% (since cut to 7.08% [P17]).

## Why big investors may be overlooking it
- Index: it was not in the S&P/ASX 300, and S&P removed it from the All Ordinaries before the open on 21 September 2026 [P20]. 866,380 shares traded on 21 September, more than five times September's median day of about 155,000 shares (Norgate).
- Size and trading: A$112.8m market value; the median day over the 20 sessions to 30 October traded A$137,729 (Norgate). A fund wanting A$10m would need months.
- Register: the Warren family holds 40.2%, S M A Motors 9.1% and Regal 7.1%, so the free float is about 40% [P1, P17].
- Sector and history: the shares have fallen 63% this year (A$1.77 at the end of January, A$0.655 now), after profit fell five years running (Norgate; table above).
- Coverage: I did not check broker databases. The company holds results webcasts for analysts [P2], so it is covered to some degree.

So it is small, thin and out of favour. But the main reason it is cheap is visible: profit has fallen from A$88m to A$14.5m in four years, and the latest half earned almost nothing.

### The earnings base: brands, new against used, the order bank
- New against used [P1, note 5; P3, p. 16]: new and demonstrator revenue fell 3.8% to A$1,559.5m on 0.5% more units (31,390), so the average new car sold for about A$49,700, 4.3% less than in FY25. Used revenue rose 12.8% to A$417.7m on 9.0% more units (10,578, a record). Service rose 6.6% and parts 3.7%, both records. In H2 the company sold 15,917 new cars against 16,000 a year earlier, so volume held; the problem was price and cost, not units [P3, P6].
- Brand mix: the legacy makers are Toyota, Mazda, Ford, Kia, Hyundai, Honda, Isuzu, Volkswagen, Land Rover, Audi and Mercedes-Benz [P19]. Chinese brands (partnerships with the Geely, Chery, SAIC, GWM, GAC, Leapmotor and DFAC groups, including Geely, Zeekr, MG, GWM and Omoda Jaecoo dealerships) went from none in June 2023 to 2, 9 and then 17 dealerships by June 2026, with about 10 more approved, and the company aims for over 30% of its brand portfolio by FY27 [P3, pp. 11 to 14]. Peter Warren says it is Geely's largest partner nationally [P3, p. 13]. The presentation also says new car margins suffer from "legacy brand discounting against new entrants" and that "pressure on new car margin and contribution will remain" [P3, p. 20].
- The market: national new car sales in September 2026 were 102,924, up 0.9% on a year earlier; battery electric cars were 24.2% of sales against 11.3% a year before, and China supplied 35.5% [F1]. Volume is flat and the mix is moving fast towards the Chinese brands.
- Order bank: 6,349 units at 30 June 2026, up 1,319 (26%) on a year earlier [P3, p. 7], about 2.4 months of new car sales. The June update said demand had shifted so fast that year-end deliveries of some high-demand models were delayed and "order banks have increased substantially as a result" [P7]. Some H2 profit was deferred into FY27, but the company has not said how much.
- Inventory: new and demonstrator stock rose from A$332.3m at December 2025 to A$409.8m at June 2026, "mainly due to new brands" [P3, p. 6; P6, p. 4]. Ageing improved (stock over 120 days down 3.4 points to 26.1%) [P3, p. 7].

### Floorplan and corporate debt, kept apart
- Floorplan (stock finance): A$498.6m drawn at 30 June 2026 against A$549.4m of facilities, so only A$50.8m unused (A$92.5m a year earlier) [P1, note 17]. It is repaid as each car is sold, is classed as current, and is secured over the cars and by general security agreements over the assets of group companies [P1, notes 2 and 17]. Weighted rate 6.07% at June (6.47% a year earlier) [P1, note 23]. Floorplan interest was A$24.6m in FY26 (A$12.0m in H1, A$12.6m in H2) [P1, note 7; P4].
- At today's rates: the cash rate rose 0.25 points on 30 September 2026, to 4.60% [R1]. If the floorplan rate moved with it, to about 6.32%, the June balance would cost about A$31.5m a year, A$6.9m more than FY26; a balance of A$420m would cost A$26.5m. The company's own figure is A$2.9m of pre-tax profit for each 0.5 points on its A$589m of borrowings [P1, note 23]. The cash rate averaged about 3.84% in FY26 and will average about 4.54% in FY27 if it stays at 4.60% [R1]: 0.70 points more, which on the A$498.6m June floorplan is about A$3.5m a year (and about A$0.6m more on the A$90.4m of corporate debt). For the half year alone, the cash rate will average about 4.48% against 3.66% in H1 FY26, about A$2.0m more floorplan interest on the June balance, and more again if stock stays near June's level. The realised rate has lagged the cash rate (it fell from 6.47% to 6.07% over FY26 while the cash rate rose 0.5 points [P1, note 23; R1]), but I use a A$3.5m drag on the dealerships in every case below.
- Corporate debt: a A$77.4m capital loan from Australian Alliance Automotive Finance at its wholesale rate plus 0.5%, repaid in fixed monthly instalments, with tranches maturing between 29 November 2031 and 28 February 2034; and a A$20m working capital facility with A$13.0m drawn. Both are secured on Warwick Farm [P1, note 17]. Of the A$90.4m, A$24.7m is current: the A$13.0m working capital line and about A$11.7m of loan instalments. The working capital line's term is not disclosed.
- Cash A$43.0m (plus A$11.1m of term deposits held as security for bank guarantees, which I don't count) [P1, notes 11 and 27]. Corporate net debt A$47.4m at June; about A$76.4m after the A$28.0m Wakeling purchase (paid from existing facilities, completed 1 October 2026) and the A$1.0m final dividend [P2, P10, P11].
- Leases: A$218.2m of lease liabilities at 5.7%; cash rent (principal and interest) was A$37.6m in FY26 [P1, notes 16 and 32; P3, p. 22].
- Debt against the right earnings line: EBITDA after rent and after floorplan interest, the money that is actually left to service corporate debt, was A$35.4m on an underlying basis in FY26 (A$22.7m in H1, A$12.7m in H2) [P3, pp. 22 and 29; P4]. Corporate net debt is 1.34 times that at June, 2.16 times pro forma for Wakeling, and about 3.0 times the H2 rate (A$12.7m doubled). The company's "0.83 times" uses EBITDA before floorplan interest, and its "0.7 times" divides by operating cash flow before A$37.6m of rent [P3, pp. 6 and 22]. Both understate the load.
- Covenants: none are disclosed in the annual report, so covenant headroom can't be checked. The company's own measure of loan to value (net debt over property) was 19.5% [P3, p. 6]. The A$90.4m secured on Warwick Farm is 57% of its book value of A$159.9m [P1, note 17].
- Goodwill headroom: the recoverable amount exceeded book by A$94.1m at June; a 35 basis point fall in average gross margin, a 1.21 point fall in revenue growth or a 1.82 point rise in the discount rate would use it up [P1, note 14]. A write-down at the half year is possible if H1 FY27 is weak. It would not touch cash or net tangible assets, but it would be a large statutory loss.

### The property: what it is worth, on what basis, and how recently
- Book: land and buildings at fair value A$243.7m (land A$165.0m, buildings A$78.7m) against A$193.2m on a cost basis [P1, note 12]. Warwick Farm is A$159.9m, so Southport is about A$83.8m [P1, note 17]. Both were bought at the 2021 listing for A$202.3m [P21].
- Valuations: Warwick Farm was independently valued in April 2026, adding A$17.7m (A$15.0m to land, A$2.7m to buildings; A$12.4m after tax, through other comprehensive income, not profit); before that, in May 2023. Southport had a desktop valuation in June 2024 and a valuation in April 2025; the directors see "no material movement" since [P1, notes 12 and 24; P9, note 12].
- Method: "summation, income capitalisation and direct comparison", with highest and best use "as a single automall / precinct"; capitalisation rates 5.75% to 6.5%, weighted 6.3%; market rent A$196 to A$505 a square metre, weighted A$390 (A$365 a year earlier) [P1, notes 3 and 24].
- The rent actually paid: the property segment's income was A$11.0m in FY26 (A$10.8m of it from the dealerships), and its EBITDA, after the owner's costs, A$9.4m [P1, note 4]. The segment note says the internal rent is "at market rates" [P1, note 4]. On book value that is a 3.85% net yield (4.5% gross), while the property's own loan costs 6.32% [P1, note 23]. The segment made A$0.5m before tax after A$6.1m of interest [P1, note 4].
- What a buyer would pay: a property investor buys a rent. At the valuer's own 6.3%, A$9.4m of net rent is worth A$149m (A$145m at 6.5%, A$163m at 5.75%; A$174m if the A$11.0m gross rent were all net). To be worth A$243.7m at 6.3%, the sites would have to pay about A$15.4m a year, about 40% more than the dealerships pay now. Either the valuer's market rent is well above the rent charged, or the book value leans on land comparisons rather than income.
- Evidence from dealership property in filings: Eagers values its dealership property at capitalisation rates of 5.0% to 8.3% (6.0% to 6.5% for sites with a better alternative use), so Peter Warren's 5.75% to 6.5% is in the normal band [E2, note 12]. In the six months to June 2026 Eagers received A$14.8m from sales of property, plant and equipment and booked a A$0.7m gain on disposal of properties [E2, cash flow statement and segment note], a small sign that dealership property does sell at or a little above its book value. None of this tells us the rent at which Peter Warren's sites would sell.
- Encumbrances and leasebacks: Warwick Farm is mortgaged for the A$90.4m of corporate debt [P1, note 17]. No mortgage over Southport is disclosed, though the floorplan financiers hold general security agreements over the assets of various group companies, and I can't tell from the filings whether those reach Southport [P1, note 2]. The only leaseback disclosed is office space at Warwick Farm let to the Warren Family Office for A$88,736 a year, with an option exercised to 29 June 2031 [P1, related parties]. No sale and leaseback of the owned property is disclosed. Management's stated plan is the opposite: to "sweat own property" and reduce outside leases [P3, p. 16].
- Tax: a sale at book value would trigger tax on about A$50.5m of gain over cost, roughly A$15.2m at 30%.
- Related-party rent the other way: the Warren family owns some of the sites Peter Warren leases. Lease liabilities to Warren-related entities were A$39.7m at June, with A$7.5m of lease interest and depreciation in FY26 [P1, related party leases]. The family earns that rent whatever the dealerships make; the FY26 dividends of 3.6 cents come to about A$2.5m on its 69.3m shares.

### Wakeling
- Bought for about A$28m (A$21.7m of goodwill plus net assets at completion), funded from existing facilities, completed 1 October 2026: 30 dealerships, 16 brands, about A$500m of turnover and about 370 staff, said to be "immediately EPS accretive after funding costs" [P10, P11]. No profit figure has been published.
- The deal nearly failed. The ACCC opened a Phase 2 review on 2 June 2026; Peter Warren withdrew and refiled with a remedy; the vendors at first declined to extend the sunset date (2 July) and then agreed to extensions to 8 and 21 September [P11, P12].
- The ACCC approved it on 3 September 2026 on condition that eight dealership sites in Campbelltown and Smeaton Grange are sold (brands Kia, GMSV, RAM, Isuzu UTE, GAC, Volkswagen and Suzuki), with an s87B undertaking accepted on 9 September [P12, P13]. The buyer must be approved by the ACCC; an independent manager runs the businesses until they are sold; the sale period is confidential; if they are not sold in time, a divestiture agent sells them "at no minimum price" [P13]. The leases go with them; no freehold is mentioned [P13]. Without the remedy Peter Warren would have run 25 of 34 dealerships in the Macarthur region [P12].
- What Wakeling earns after the sale is not public. My estimate, clearly an assumption: if the eight sites carry about a quarter of turnover, about A$375m remains; at a profit margin of 0.5% to 1.5% before funding (Peter Warren made 0.9% in FY25 and 0.6% in FY26, Autosports 1.7%, Eagers 3.0% in Australia and New Zealand [A1, E1]), that is A$1.9m to A$5.6m a year, less about A$1.8m of interest on the A$28m. The sale proceeds would cut debt, but with a no-minimum-price fallback I count nothing for them.

### Who owns it and who is moving
- The Warren family's buying is long-running, not new. Since its June 2022 notice (36.36%) it bought 978,143 shares at A$2.35 in June 2022, about 86,500 at A$2.90 to A$3.04 in September 2022, about 122,000 at A$2.30 to A$2.44 in February 2024 and 541,000 at A$1.80 to A$1.90 in May 2024 (plus 38,985 from converted rights in August 2023), reaching 37.32% [P14]. In June 2026 it bought 5,002,151 more, to 40.22%: about 806,700 at A$0.76 to A$0.91 between 3 and 11 June, and 4,195,455 at about A$1.03 on 15 June [P14, P15]. Each earlier wave of buying was followed by lower prices.
- The A$1.03 was a block, not a market bid. On 15 June 2026 Regal sold 4,110,795 shares for A$4,234,118.85, exactly A$1.03 each [P17], and the family bought 4,195,455 at about A$1.03 the same day [P15]. The market closed at A$0.975 that day, on 265,719 shares (Norgate). The family paid about 6% above the market to take Regal's block, which says it wanted those shares kept off the market, more than it says what the business is worth.
- Regal: rose from 8.10% to 10.36% (notice of 4 November 2025), kept buying through November and December 2025 at about A$1.78 to A$1.92, sold 1.9m at A$1.88 on 30 December 2025, bought 1.8m at A$1.335 on 9 March 2026, then sold down to 8.29% by 15 June and 7.08% by 21 August 2026 [P17]. It still holds 12.2m shares, a likely seller.
- John Eastham bought 852,602 shares in June 2026 [P16]. On each day from 3 to 11 June his parcels match Paul Warren's to within one share, at the same prices (for example 282,000 at A$0.76 on 3 June for both) [P15, P16]. That looks like one broker filling a joint order and splitting it. Nothing in the filings says the two are associates, and the Warren family's notice does not list Eastham as one [P14]. If they were associates, their combined rise over six months would be about 3.4 points, above the 3-point limit discussed next. I flag it only as something I could not settle.
- The creep rule: a holder with at least 19% throughout the past six months may add up to 3 percentage points above its holding six months earlier without a bid (Corporations Act section 611, item 9; my reading). The family held 37.32% until 3 June and 40.22% from 15 June 2026. So until about 15 December 2026 it can add well under one point (about 0.1 point now, rising to about 0.6 point by mid-December as the June buys pass six months), and from about 16 December 2026 up to a further 3 points, about 5.17m shares (about A$3.4m at A$0.655). The fact sheet's "from about 3 December" is too early for the full 3%.
- Others: Spheria became substantial at 6.19% on 21 November 2025 and ceased on 31 March 2026; JPMorgan was substantial from 29 June to 20 July 2026 through stock lending [fact sheet section 4; P22].
- No capital raisings in two years: issued capital was A$493.9m at both June 2025 and June 2026 [P1].

### Cash and dividends
- Statutory operating cash flow A$42.0m, but A$54.4m of it was new floorplan borrowing against a A$67.1m rise in stock [P1, note 32]. Operating cash flow less lease principal (A$24.5m) and capital spending (A$5.5m) was about A$12.0m, the same as the A$12.0m of dividends paid [P1; P3, p. 22].
- Dividends: 3.0 cents interim and 0.6 cents final for FY26, fully franked; the final was paid on 2 October 2026 [P2]. ### What a quick reader would miss
1. The second-half collapse was mostly costs, not gross profit.
2. The property earns 3.85% on its book value; book value needs a rent about 40% above what is paid.
3. The A$1.03 was Regal's block, bought above the market.
4. The family's buying runs back to 2022, at prices up to A$3.04.
5. The company's leverage figures leave out floorplan interest or rent.
6. Unused floorplan was only A$50.8m at June, before Wakeling's stock.
7. A 35 basis point fall in gross margin would wipe out the goodwill headroom.
8. The Warren family is also a landlord to the company, at about A$7.5m a year.

## Valuation
Shares 172,256,068. Pro forma corporate net debt A$76.4m (A$47.4m at June, plus A$28.0m for Wakeling and A$1.0m for the final dividend). Floorplan is not deducted, because the dealership earnings used are already after floorplan interest and the floorplan is matched by stock. Leases are not deducted, because the earnings are after rent. Tax 30%. Minority interests (20% of Warwick Farm Automotive and 5% of the regional dealerships) take about A$1.1m to A$1.2m of profit a year [P1, note 21]. The arithmetic was run in python3 -I.

Dealership earnings in three cases (A$m a year, before tax, after floorplan interest, rent and corporate costs):

| | Bad: H2 FY26 is the level | Middle: FY26 is the level | Good: back to FY25 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starting point | 3.0 (H2 FY26's A$1.5m doubled) | 14.0 (FY26) | 23.2 (FY25) |
| Higher floorplan rates | minus 3.5 | minus 3.5 | minus 3.5 |
| Cost savings | 0 | plus 3.0 | plus 6.0 |
| Recurring acquisition and restructure costs | minus 3.0 | minus 3.0 | minus 3.0 |
| Dealership profit before tax | minus 3.5 | 10.5 | 22.8 |
| After tax and minorities | nil | 6.2 | 14.7 |

The savings follow management's own words: the A$6.4m of cost inflation is to be "neutralised" in FY27 [P3, p. 21]. The good case takes all of it, the middle case half, the bad case none. I don't use FY24 (A$57.0m) as a normal year: margins were still well above today's after the shortage, and every year since has been lower.

### Method 1: sum of the parts
Property at its income value on the rent the dealerships pay now (A$9.4m net at 6.3%, A$149.1m). Dealerships at a multiple of after-tax profit: 6 times in the bad case, 7 in the middle, 8 in the good. Autosports, the closest listed peer by size, register and disclosure, trades at about 7.6 times trailing earnings and about 5.7 times its normalised profit after tax; Eagers, the large and better-run leader, at about 22 times [A3, E5]. Wakeling at cost (A$28.0m) in the middle and good cases, and at its net assets only (A$6.3m, the goodwill worth nothing) in the bad case.

- Bad: A$149.1m + nil + A$6.3m - A$76.4m = A$78.9m, A$0.46 a share. (The dealerships lose about A$3.5m a year here, so nil is generous.)
- Middle: A$149.1m + 7 x A$6.2m (A$43.3m) + A$28.0m - A$76.4m = A$144.0m, A$0.84 a share.
- Good: A$149.1m + 8 x A$14.7m (A$117.8m) + A$28.0m - A$76.4m = A$218.4m, A$1.27 a share.

The fact sheet's anchor, in this frame: the property at the directors' A$243.7m less A$76.4m is A$167.3m (A$0.97 a share). On income value it is A$72.6m (A$0.42 a share); at cost, A$116.7m (A$0.68).

Alternative version, for completeness: property at the directors' value less tax on the gain (A$228.6m), with the dealerships charged the extra A$6.0m a year that a buyer at 6.3% would need. Bad A$0.92, middle A$1.13, good A$1.54 a share. I don't use it for the outcomes: nothing in the filings points to a buyer for the property at that rent, management plans to keep it, and the dealerships earned A$1.5m in H2 before any rent increase.

### Method 2: the whole group on earnings
Group profit before tax = dealerships (as above) + the property segment (A$0.5m after its own interest in FY26, less about A$0.6m of extra interest at today's rates, so about minus A$0.1m) + Wakeling after funding (A$0.1m, A$2.0m and A$3.9m a year at margins of 0.5%, 1.0% and 1.5% on about A$375m, less A$1.8m of interest).

- Bad: minus A$3.5m; no earnings value.
- Middle: A$12.3m before tax, A$7.5m after tax and minorities; at 7 times, A$52.5m, A$0.30 a share (A$0.44 at 10 times).
- Good: A$26.5m before tax, A$17.4m after; at 8 times, A$138.8m, A$0.81 a share (A$1.01 at 10 times).

### Why the two methods differ, and the cross-check
On earnings, the property is worth almost nothing to an owner of the shares: it yields 3.85% and its loan costs 6.32%, so it adds no profit at today's rates. In the sum of the parts it counts for A$149m. The gap closes only if someone buys the property or the company. Cross-check: net tangible assets are 148 cents a share on the company's basis and 116 cents on owners' equity less intangibles [P1]. The price is 0.44 and 0.57 times those; at the end of 2025 the shares traded at about 1.3 times the company's December figure of 143.81 cents [P4; Norgate].

### Range
A$0.46 to A$1.27 a share on the sum of the parts; nil to A$0.81 on earnings. For the outcomes I take the midpoint of the two methods in each case (where earnings are nil, the assets set the value): A$0.46 bad, A$0.57 middle, A$1.04 good, before dividends.
- The low end depends on the dealerships earning nothing (the H2 FY26 level, with higher floorplan costs and the recurring deal costs) and on the property being worth only its income at today's rent.
- The high end depends on the dealerships earning their FY25 profit again (about A$23m before tax), which needs costs to come back down, and the market paying 8 times for it.
- The single input that moves the answer most is the dealerships' cost base: every A$5m of operating cost saved or added is about A$3.5m after tax, worth 14 to 16 cents a share at 7 to 8 times.

## Why the market is wrong
I can't say it is. At A$0.655 the market values the business at A$189.3m including the A$76.4m of corporate debt. With the property at its income value and Wakeling at cost, that leaves about A$12m for the dealerships, which is what they would be worth on about A$4m a year of profit before tax: between my bad and middle cases. With the property at the directors' value, the dealerships and Wakeling together are priced at about minus A$54m, which is the fact sheet's case. But that value needs a buyer at a rent the dealerships can't yet afford, and nothing dated in the window brings one. On earnings alone the price is on the high side. There is no trade.

## What would make this wrong
1. The costs come back faster than I allow. Gross profit held level in H2, so if the A$15m second-half cost increase was mostly new-dealership start-up costs that fall away, H1 FY27 could come close to H1 FY26's A$12.5m despite higher interest. Then the good case is more likely than 20%.
2. Someone pays for the property.

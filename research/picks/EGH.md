# Eureka Group Holdings (ASX:EGH): deep dive, the case for buying

Written 2026-11-10 by Claude for the one-company-at-a-time process (rulebook section 5a, docs/RESEARCH_PROCESS.md). This is the case for buying.
Price at writing: A$0.62 (last sale on 9 November 2026, ASX company data read 10 November 2026) [S14]. Shares on issue 562,261,051 [S14], so the market value is about A$348.6m. Pro forma net debt about A$213.6m after the NSW purchase [S5], so the business is valued at about A$562m.
Status: wait. It fails at A$0.62 and qualifies at about A$0.59, as an ordinary pick or as a special situation (see "Where it lands"). The case-against prompt is filed (research/against/EGH_prompt.md), to run at about A$0.59 or on Cam's call. This is a simulation and not financial advice.

Order of work: chosen on 9 November 2026 from the holder-notice screen (tools/holder_notices.py), which flagged Filetron building from 21.0% to 35.8% over eight notices. The first job was to find out who Filetron is, after TOT showed that the builder can be the controller. The bad case was roughed out from the filings first at about 20% to 30% below the price, inside the 30% limit, so the work went on.

## Where it lands
It fails at today's price. On my numbers:
- the bad case is 21.3% below the price, inside the 30% limit;
- the middling case is +21.5%, against the +32.0% the value test then asks for;
- the expected gain after costs is about +7.4%, against the 12.9% minimum.

It qualifies at about A$0.59, where both tests pass: the middling case is +27.7% against +26.0% needed, and the expected gain after costs is +13.0%. It closed at A$0.595 on 11 and 12 June 2026, just before its trading update [S15]. As a special situation (the Cottle build-up) the answer is the same, because the expected-gain test is the one that binds.

What the evidence outside the filings showed:
- **Filetron is Ben Cottle**, the founder and chair of FDC, the Sydney builder that floated in July 2026 at A$969m [S9, S10]. A second buyer, Development Management & Constructions (DMC), which crossed 5% on 30 September, belongs to his brother Blake Cottle, also an FDC director [S4, S9, S11]. Between them they hold about 39.7% of Eureka [S3, S4].
- The two groups report separately in Eureka and say they aren't associates [S3, S4]. In FDC's own notices the brothers are declared associates [S11]. They share an office and a chief financial officer, three companies appear in both groups' lists [S3, S4], and five of the same trades in March 2026 appear in both groups' notices [S3, S4]. On its own each group's buying fits the takeover rules. If they were associates in Eureka, their combined buying in the first half of 2026 would be about 5% in six months, against the 3% allowed (my arithmetic). That's a legal question I can't settle.
- The family also appears to own Broadlands, a land lease operator in regional NSW, the same kind of community Eureka has just started buying [S12, S3, S4].
- A fund manager, Copia Investment Partners, holds 23.6% and has built from 9.7% since early 2025 [S2]. With the Cottles that's about 63% of the company in three hands.
- Eureka isn't cheap against its peers or against what private capital offered for Ingenia (see question 3).

So the builder is a family that can reach control without a bid, and probably wouldn't need to pay a premium to do it. That's the same lesson as TOT, in a business that's growing faster.

## What the price assumes
At A$0.62 [S14, S5, S6]:
- 1.10 times pro forma net tangible assets of 56.3 cents a share;
- 14.8 times the FY27 underlying earnings guidance of at least 4.2 cents a share, reaffirmed at the AGM on 30 October [S6]. Eureka's "underlying EPS" is before tax (underlying profit before tax divided by shares) [S5]. Its revenue tax losses cover about A$16.6m of profit, probably about a year's worth (my arithmetic from the A$5.0m deferred tax asset [S1]). After a 30% tax charge the multiple would be about 21 times;
- a 2.4% dividend yield (1.46 cents a year, unfranked) [S1].

The price assumes the growth plan works: earnings up at least 22% in FY27 from acquisitions, then steady growth from rent rises and new homes. Brokers' targets (Morgans A$0.85, Moelis A$0.75) [S16] are higher, but both firms underwrote the September raising [S5].

## The one thing the market is missing
The two biggest buyers are one family. They have just been paid out of FDC's float, run a land lease business in Eureka's new segment, and can take control without making a bid.

That cuts both ways. A Cottle-led deal (a bid, or Broadlands sold into Eureka) could add value. Their record, though, is to buy steadily on market and to refuse bids: Filetron arrived in March 2024, during Aspen's takeover bid, and said it wouldn't accept [S7]. A creeping path to control pays minority holders no premium. On its own it doesn't make Eureka pass.

## The test outside the filings
Done on 10 November 2026, under docs/RESEARCH_PROCESS.md step 2. The questions the filings can't answer:
1. Who are the builders, and what do they want?
2. Did Eureka pay a fair price for the NSW portfolio?
3. Is Eureka cheap against its peers and against recent bids in the sector?
4. How good are the earnings, and how exposed are they to rates?

What the evidence showed:

1. The builders are the Cottle brothers, and they're buying steadily.

   - DMC Pty Ltd as trustee for the Three Sisters Trust is Blake Andrew Cottle and Justine Cottle [S4]. Blake Cottle is an FDC director [S9].

Three companies appear in both groups' lists, with the same company numbers: FDC Building Services, Mount Gambier Hospital Limited, and a Caloola company [S3, S4].
   - Five trades in March 2026 appear in both groups' notices with the same share numbers and amounts (530,790, 480,383, 10,449, 20,789 and 16,015 shares, A$560,702 in all), dated a day or so apart [S3, S4]. Either a company in both groups made them, or one notice is wrong.
   - FDC floated in July 2026 at A$969m. The existing owners sold about A$350m of shares, and FDC paid a A$140m dividend before listing [S10]. Both Cottle groups bought Eureka shares on market at about A$0.65 within three weeks of the float [S3, S4].
   - Filetron's record [S3, S7]:

   - In the six months from 19 January 2026, Filetron bought about 12.4m shares on market, about 2.9% of the shares then on issue: just inside the 3% allowance (my arithmetic). DMC, below 20%, can buy without limit; it bought about 11.2m between March and June [S4]. Together, net of the five duplicated trades, that's about 5.3% (my arithmetic).
   - Corio Developments owns Broadlands, which runs over-50s land lease communities at Green Point, Tamworth and Muswellbrook and has more planned at Harrington, Narooma and South West Rocks [S12]. Filetron's list includes Corio Projects Pty Ltd, and DMC's includes Green Point, Harrington, Muswellbrook and Armidale "Lifestyle Living" companies [S3, S4]. I haven't confirmed the ownership from the company register; the names line up.
   - Eureka's board: John Whiteman, a Eureka director, is also an FDC director [S1, S13].
   - Copia Investment Partners holds 132.4m shares (23.6%). It took up its entitlement and more in September, at about A$0.62 [S2].
   - Reading: the Cottles look like long-term builders rather than bidders. They could pass 50% without a bid: Filetron by creeping 3% every six months, DMC by buying freely up to 20%. A bid with a premium is possible, but I put it at about 10% by August 2028. The more likely event is a deal between Eureka and Broadlands, which would need a holder vote with the Cottles excluded, and an independent expert.
2. The NSW portfolio was bought at the seller's book value.
   - Ingenia sold the six communities "at 30 June book value at a yield of circa 6.5%" [S17]. Eureka describes the same A$123.8m price as an 8.1% ingoing yield [S5]. The gap is the difference between Ingenia's income figure and Eureka's own forecast. Ingenia called them "mature" and "lower growth" assets [S17].
   - Simon Owen ran Ingenia for 14 years before joining Eureka [S1]. Eureka's team managed some of these properties before [S18].
   - Reading: a fair price, not a bargain. The 8.1% depends on Eureka running them better than Ingenia did.
3. Eureka trades at about the price private capital offered for Ingenia.
   - Warburg Pincus proposed A$4.75 a security for Ingenia in September 2026, 1.11 times its June NTA of A$4.28; Ingenia rejected it [S19]. Ingenia trades at A$5.20, 1.21 times NTA [S14].
   - Eureka trades at 1.10 times pro forma NTA. On price to assets it sits at about the level of a recent private bid for its best-known peer, and below where Ingenia now trades.
   - Reading: no discount to close against the sector.
4. The earnings are growing, but before tax, with some sales gains, and partly floating.
   - FY26 underlying EBITDA included about A$1.5m of gains on home and unit sales and other income [S5]. The plan is to sell homes at Bundamba and Eagleby for A$22m to A$24m over two to three years [S8], so more of these gains are coming.
   - Debt: A$216m pro forma; A$40m hedged with collars at July 2026 [S5]. Each extra half-point on the rest costs about A$0.88m a year, about 0.16 cents a share (my arithmetic). The guidance was set on 3 September, when the cash rate was 4.35%; it's 4.85% now [S6, S20].
   - The loan-to-value ratio is 39.8% pro forma, just under the 40% internal ceiling [S5]. More growth needs equity or capital partners. Two raisings in two years have brought in A$150.6m: A$70.4m in November 2024 and A$80.2m in September 2026 [S6].
   - Rents are tied to the pension and to CPI. Seniors' occupancy was 97% at June [S5].
   - Reading: the 22% FY27 growth is mostly bought. After that, growth comes from rents, new homes and management fees, less higher interest. I take FY29 underlying EPS at 4.9 cents in the middling case.

Summary: the market isn't missing an upside here either. The holder build-up is real and large, but it's a family that can take control without paying for it, and the company is priced in line with its sector.

## The company in plain words
Eureka owns and runs affordable rental communities [S1, S5]:
- seniors' rental villages, mostly in Queensland, where residents pay rent from the age pension;
- all-age rental and mixed-use parks: permanent homes alongside tourist cabins and sites;
- since September 2026, land lease communities, where residents own their homes and rent the land.

After the NSW purchase it has 70 communities and 5,492 homes and sites, and about A$666m of assets under management [S5]. Simon Owen has been chief executive since September 2024 [S1].

FY26 [S1, S5]:
- revenue A$56.7m (+24%);
- underlying EBITDA A$21.7m;
- underlying profit before tax A$14.7m, or 3.45 cents a share;
- statutory profit A$16.7m;
- NTA 57.7 cents;
- a dividend of 1.46 cents.

## The players and what the rules allow
- The Cottles: Filetron 34.6% and DMC 5.1% [S3, S4]. Filetron can add 3% every six months; DMC can buy freely to 20%. A bid by either would need to be for all shares, at a price that gets past Copia. Buying a substantial asset from a 30% holder needs holder approval with that holder's votes excluded, and an independent expert's report (ASX Listing Rule 10.1).
- Copia Investment Partners, 23.6% [S2]. A fund manager; any outside bid needs Copia and the Cottles.
- Australian Retirement Trust, about 8.4% [S22].
- The board: Russell Banham (chair), Simon Owen (managing director), Greg Paramor (7.4m shares), John Whiteman (also on FDC's board), Carolyn Tregarthen and Sue Renkin [S1].
- At the 2026 AGM, 11.8% of votes went against the remuneration report [S13].
- About 74% of the shares are in the hands of the four big holders and the directors, so the free float is about A$90m (my arithmetic).

## Why the market is wrong
Mostly it isn't. The market prices Eureka in line with its sector, and the growth to FY27 is in the guidance. What it may underweight:
- For: two cashed-up holders from the same family, with a land lease business of their own, own 40% and keep buying. A deal of some kind before 2028 is likely.
- Against: they can reach control without a bid. The earnings measure is before tax, and the tax losses run out soon. Debt is near the internal ceiling, so more equity raisings are likely.

For the card, the first two combine into one bad case: 35% at A$0.488. The card would read 35% at A$0.488, 45% at A$0.753 and 20% at A$0.864.

Expected value: 0.35 × 0.488 + 0.45 × 0.753 + 0.20 × 0.864 = A$0.683, or +10.1% before costs. After about 2.6% of costs it's about +7.4%. The costs are A$15 brokerage each way on about A$2,250 (1.3%), an assumed half-cent spread (0.8%), and the 0.5% currency fee. The minimum over 661 days is 12.9%, so that test fails.

The value test (an ordinary pick):
- The bad case of A$0.488 is 21.3% below A$0.62, inside the 30% limit.
- Against that fall the middling case needs +32.0%, and it's +21.5%. It fails.
- It qualifies at about A$0.59: the bad case is 17.3% below, the middling case is +27.7% against +26.0% needed, and the expected gain after costs is +13.0%.

As a special situation (the Cottle build-up), the value test doesn't apply, and the expected-gain test still sets the price at about A$0.59.

# Australian Agricultural Company (ASX:AAC): deep dive. Not a value pick: the middling case (A$1.50) is 13% above the price, short of 25%, and no sale, return or bid is under way. No case

Written 2026-11-08 by the research desk. Price at writing: A$1.325 (last trade on Friday 6 November 2026, bid A$1.31, ask A$1.35, ASX company header fetched 8 November 2026 NZ time, data/research_data/asx_company.json; Norgate's last close in the repo is A$1.33 on 30 October). Market value: A$798.7m (602,766,747 shares on issue, 2026 Annual Report p152 [A4]).
Status: finished. This is a simulation and not financial advice.
Verdict: no case. No card and no red team. One dated check: the first-half result for the half to 30 September 2026 (about 19 to 20 November 2026). It reopens this note only on the conditions under "What would make this wrong".
Fact sheet: research/facts/AAC.md, grade B. The question it set: the market has never paid AACo's net tangible assets (NTA) in the last decade and the discount is the widest it has been. Is that because the land and herd values can't be turned into cash for minority holders, or will the company or its holders act on it? Its "our way" anchor was A$1.94 (NTA of A$2.92 at the ten-year average of 0.665 times).

Value pick (rulebook section 5a): it doesn't qualify. Over 24 months the bad case, which here is "nothing happens" (A$1.25), is 5.7% below the price and inside the 10% limit, but the middling case (A$1.50, a capital return or buyback without a bid) is only 13.2% above it. The only outcome that clears 25% is a control transaction, and that is the good case, not the middling one.

Order of work: the price was in the brief and the fact sheet, so it was in view from the start. "What the filings show" and "Valuation" were worked from AACo's filings, the holders' notices and listed peers' filings; no value there was set by reference to AACo's price except where a method is explicitly about what the market pays (the peer ratios and the implied realisation rate), and those are marked.

In short. The land values hold up: AACo's own valuer's benchmarks and 2025 to 2026 station sales are consistent with the book, and a listed landlord (Rural Funds Group) sold four cattle properties in 2026 at 25% above its book values, including Rewan, which AACo leases. The problem is the mechanism. In ten years AACo has paid no dividend, bought back no shares, returned no capital and sold no pastoral property; the July 2026 AGM sentence on "dividends and share buy-backs" is a general statement after years of "we will not be paying a dividend". The land and herd earned 0.7% a year before tax on NTA in cash over the last five years (A$11.7m a year), and free cash flow after all capital spending and lease payments was negative in seven of the last ten years (minus A$160m in total). Listed farmland owners that pay a 6% cash yield trade at 0.57 to 0.61 times NTA, and one with no cash earnings trades at 0.35 times; on a like-for-like basis (before AACo's A$0.71 a share of deferred tax) AACo is at 0.37 times. So on the measure the market uses for this kind of business, AACo is priced in line with its peers. The value is reachable only through a control transaction, and the two holders (the AA Trust, 53.1%, controlled by Joe Lewis's Tavistock; Tattarang, 22.4%) each block the other. Expected price in 24 months A$1.38, +4.3% before costs and about +0.8% after a 3.5% round trip, well below cash.

## The company in plain words
AACo breeds, grows and finishes cattle on its own land in Queensland and the Northern Territory and sells them as boxed Wagyu beef under its own brands (Westholme, Darling Downs, 1824) or as live cattle [A1, A4]. It runs 19 owned cattle stations, three leased stations (Rewan, Comanche and Gordon Downs; the first two leased from Rural Funds Group), two owned feedlots (Goonoo and Aronui), two owned farms and one leased farm, about 6.5 million hectares in all [A4 p15 and OFR; A1 note F3; A31, A32]. FY26 (year to 31 March 2026) revenue was A$422.1m: beef A$314.4m (South Korea A$81.7m, USA A$78.3m, China A$38.1m, Australia A$37.5m) and live cattle A$107.7m [A1 note A5].

Who runs it: Donald McGauchie has chaired the board since 2010; David Harris is managing director. Two of the eight non-executive directors, Sarah Gentry (a Tavistock vice president) and Josh Levy (Tavistock's co-chief executive), are not independent "as they are officers of Tavistock Group which controls the AA Trust, a major 53.09% shareholder" [A4, remuneration report KMP table; A5].

Who owns it, at 29 May 2026 [A4 p152]: Bryan Glinton as trustee of The AA Trust 319,999,220 shares (53.09%); Tattarang Pty Ltd as trustee of the Peepingee Trust, Nicola and Andrew Forrest, 134,891,347 (22.38%). ## What the filings show

### The land: how it is valued, and whether sales support it
- Book value: pastoral property and improvements A$1,741.5m at 31 March 2026, up from A$1,588.5m (additions A$16.7m, revaluation increment A$143.3m to reserve, depreciation A$7.0m) [A1 note A4]. It is mostly Crown leasehold: rolling-term pastoral leases in Queensland and perpetual pastoral leases in the Northern Territory [A1 note A4].
- Method and frequency: the directors adopt an independent valuation by LAWD Pty Ltd each year at 31 March, using direct comparison with sales. 17 properties (A$1,356.3m) are valued per adult equivalent of carrying capacity, average A$3,624 (range A$1,890 to A$7,576), up from A$3,207; 2 properties (A$158.6m) per hectare, average A$3,443; 2 feedlot properties (A$226.6m) per hectare plus a rate per standard cattle unit [A1 note A4]. Carrying capacity is reassessed by an outside expert "periodically"; in other years management reviews it internally [A1 note A4]. The average carrying capacity of the 17 properties fell from 26,947 to 25,634 adult equivalents while the rate rose 13%. Property is not revalued at the half year: the 1H26 report shows NTA moved only with the herd [A8].
- History: book value rose from A$698.2m (FY18) to A$1,741.5m (FY26), and the average rate per adult equivalent from A$1,384 (FY17) to A$3,624 (FY26) [A1, A10 to A18]. Revaluations net of tax added A$376.5m (A$0.62 a share) to equity from FY23 to FY26, while retained profit in those four years was A$16.2m. Nearly all the NTA growth since March 2022 (A$2.27 to A$2.92, about A$392m) is land revaluation [A1, A10 to A12].
- AACo's own sales: none. Proceeds from selling property, plant and equipment were between A$0.2m and A$2.5m in every year from FY16 to FY26, and the pastoral property note shows disposals of nil (FY26) and A$8,000 (FY25) [A1, A10 to A19]. The chairman told the 2017 AGM, as a point of pride, that "there were no land sales" in his time [A24].
- Sales by others, 2025 to 2026:
  - Rural Funds Group (ASX:RFF), primary source: on 10 July 2026 it contracted to sell four cattle properties for A$234.5m net against December 2025 book values of A$187.0m, 25% above book: Rewan A$106.9m against A$72.8m (+47%; Rewan is leased to AACo until 2029), Wyseby A$43.2m against A$35.1m (+23%), Cerberus A$34.1m against A$26.2m (+30%), Cobungra A$50.3m against A$52.9m (minus 5%) [P1].   - Northern stations, secondary source (Beef Central, 25 March 2026): Aroona sold for A$44m "bare", which a Herron Todd White valuer called in line with the last 18 months of sales; Murranji (447,500 hectares) for about A$44m; LAWD (AACo's valuer) put Northern Territory values at about A$2,500 to A$3,000 per adult equivalent and lower-rainfall Queensland at A$9,000 to A$10,000; the valuers described prices as holding, not rising [S1]. Beetaloo, Mungaroom and OT Downs (about one million hectares) sold in December 2025 for a reported A$300m-plus including about 80,000 cattle and plant [S2].
  - What this means: AACo's average A$3,624 per adult equivalent, a mix of Territory and Queensland country, sits inside these ranges, and listed holders of Queensland cattle country have recently sold above their books. I found no evidence that the land is carried above what it would fetch. The book value is not the problem.

## Valuation
Four views, because the answer depends on who you are: a buyer of the whole company, or a holder of 0.0003% of it.

1. Net assets, to a buyer of the whole. NTA A$2.92 (company figure; my own from the balance sheet is A$2.90 excluding all intangibles, A$2.94 keeping the A$26.9m of carbon credits) after A$425.5m (A$0.71 a share) of deferred tax, of which A$369.7m is on the land and A$91.7m on the herd's trading-stock values, less A$47.6m of tax assets [A1 note F4, A3]. Break-up value: net assets A$1,775.3m, less intangibles other than carbon credits (A$0.8m), less 2.5% selling costs on the land (A$43.5m), less A$25m of closing costs, keeping the deferred tax as the tax on sale: A$1,705.9m, A$2.83 a share. Python: (1775.3 - 27.7 + 26.9 - 43.5 - 25) / 602.77 = 2.83. Depends on: land at book (supported above) and cattle prices.

2. Cash earnings at the yield listed farmland owners trade on. Owner earnings of A$11.7m a year (five-year average) at a 6% cash yield is A$195m, A$0.32 a share; FY26's A$13.1m gives A$0.36. A cross-check on the land alone: at the 3.9% passing yield RFF achieved on its 2026 sales, AACo's land would rent for A$67.9m a year, which is about what the whole operation earns before depreciation and interest (A$71.6m). The cattle and beef business on its own earns roughly a market rent on its land and little more. Depends on: owner earnings, which were A$1.0m to A$26.5m in the last five years.

4. What a minority holder's share is worth as a wait for a sale. A minority holder receives nothing until someone buys the company or the board returns the assets. If that happens with a chance h each year, at a value V growing at 3% a year, discounted at 9%: at V = A$2.05 (a bid at about 0.70 times NTA, see the players section) the value is A$0.95 at h = 5% and A$1.30 at h = 10%; at V = A$2.83 (break-up) it is A$1.29 at h = 5% and A$1.77 at h = 10%. Today's A$1.325 implies about a 10.7% chance a year of a bid at A$2.05, or 5.2% a year of full break-up value (arithmetic in python3 -I, scratchpad aac/). This method uses the price only to read back the implied chance.

Range. To a buyer of the whole: A$2.80 to A$2.95. To a minority holder with nothing happening: A$1.00 to A$1.35, from methods 3 and 4; method 2 (A$0.32 to A$0.36) shows how little of the price is supported by cash earnings. The gap between the two ranges is the value of a control transaction, and only the two large holders can make one.

The board. It decides dividends and buy-backs and has the debt room (A$221.6m undrawn) but not the cash (free cash flow minus A$36.0m in FY26). An on-market buy-back of up to 10% in 12 months needs no vote, and the holders' resulting increase is exempt from the 20% rule (s611 item 19) [S8]: a 10% buy-back (60.3m shares, about A$81m) would lift NTA per share to about A$3.09 and the AA Trust to 59.0%, Tattarang to 24.9%. But at about 36,000 shares a day, an on-market buy-back could buy only a few million shares a year without being most of the market; a large one would have to be an equal-access tender.

What the rules let each do:
- Creep: either can add 3 percentage points every six months (s611 item 9: at least 19% throughout the previous six months) [S8]. The AA Trust could reach 90% only by about 2033 at that rate, and only if Tattarang sold.
- A bid for all: the AA Trust can bid at any time. Compulsory acquisition after a bid needs 90% of the shares and 75% of those bid for (s661A, from my reading of the Act; the AustLII page refused a typed address). Without Tattarang the AA Trust can reach at most 77.6%. With Tattarang, it needs 14.5 points of the 24.5-point free float, so holders of 10.0 points (41% of the float) can block.
- A scheme: the AA Trust would vote in a separate class or be excluded, leaving Tattarang with 47.7% of the votes that count; a scheme needs 75% of votes cast, so Tattarang alone can block it.
- A sale of the AA Trust's stake: a buyer going from under 20% (or from Tattarang's 22.4%) to 75% must do it through a takeover bid for everyone, at no less than the highest price it paid in the previous four months (s621(3), from my reading of the Act). Minority holders would get the same price as the AA Trust.
- General compulsory acquisition (s664A) needs 90% held; holders of 10% of the rest can object and send it to court, which looks at fair value. Not available to anyone today.

Comparable cases. Webster (2019): PSP Investments, holding 19.1%, agreed a scheme for the rest at A$2.00, a 57% premium to the price and 47% above book net assets of A$1.36 [P11]; Webster delisted in February 2020 (Norgate). Vitalharvest Freehold Trust (2020 to 2021), a listed farmland trust: undisturbed at 96.5c, a contest between Macquarie and Roc was reported won by Macquarie at A$1.24 in April 2021, 28% higher (Motley Fool, IREI) [S7]; the trust delisted in July 2021 (Norgate). Inside AACo itself: Tattarang paid Heytesbury A$2.13 in June 2022 (0.94 times the NTA of A$2.27 then), and the AA Trust paid A$1.81 to A$1.91 in August and September 2022; their latest buying was at A$1.35 to A$1.38 (0.53 to 0.54 times NTA). A bid for control at 0.70 times NTA, about A$2.05, a 55% premium, fits those prices. A bid at break-up value (A$2.83) has no precedent here.

Nothing happens. With no bid, buy-back or return, the price is set by the same market that has let the price-to-NTA ratio fall from 0.72 (March 2022) to 0.45 while NTA rose: A$1.25 is 0.43 times NTA of about A$2.90, near the 52-week low of A$1.275 (Norgate). If cattle prices fall and NTA drops back to the FY25 level (A$2.55), the same ratio gives about A$1.10.

## Why the market is wrong
For the most part it isn't. The land values are sound, but a minority holder can only get them through a sale or a return, and neither has happened in ten years: no dividend, no buy-back, no return of capital, no land sale. The business earns about a market rent on its land and little more, so there is no cash to return without borrowing. The market prices listed farmland on cash yield; AACo, with almost none, trades where a farmland owner with no cash yield trades. The two holders could unlock the value, but each blocks the other, the controller stopped buying in May 2024, and the price already implies roughly a one-in-ten chance a year of a bid at about A$2.05, which is above the rate I can justify from 13 years of control without one.

## What would make this wrong
The two things most likely to make "no case" wrong:
1. A control transaction comes early. Mr Lewis is 89 and his US case is closed by the pardon; a family decision to sell the stake would force a bid for everyone, and Tattarang, 23.5% under water at A$1.73 a share, is the natural buyer. At A$2.05 the shares would rise 55%. Watch for any AA Trust or Tattarang notice, a change in the AA Trust's trustees or protectors, or a Tavistock director leaving the board.
2. The board starts returning capital at scale. A land sale at or near book with the cash paid out, an equal-access tender buy-back, or a dividend policy would show that minority holders can reach the asset value, and the market would likely re-price towards the peers' 0.57 to 0.61 times NTA (A$1.65 to A$1.80) only if the return looked lasting.

Exit rule: no position. Reopen this note at once on any of: a substantial holder notice from the AA Trust, Tattarang or a new holder; a control proposal from anyone; the board announcing a buy-back of 5% or more of the shares, a capital return, a dividend policy, or the sale of a property with the proceeds returned; a strategic review. A binding bid would go to the deal sleeve. A buy-back or dividend announced at the 1H27 result on its own would reopen it only if it is large enough (5% of the shares, or a payout of A$40m or more) to change the middling case; a token amount would not.

What would make a buyer wrong, for the record: cattle prices fall (the herd mark cost A$149m in FY24), another Gulf flood (about 7,000 head lost in 2026), and the price ignoring NTA rises as it has since 2022.

# Step One Clothing (ASX:STP): deep dive. Not a value pick: the bad case (A$0.10) is 26% below the price

Status: finished. Verdict: wait for a named event. The event is the 1H FY27 result (about 18 February 2027), with the AGM addresses on 11 November 2026 as an early read. The exact reopen conditions are under "Dated events". This is a simulation and not financial advice.

Written 2026-11-08 by the research desk. Fact sheet: research/facts/STP.md, grade A as a value pick. The question it set: has the reset stopped the cash going out, or will revenue keep falling and slowly use up the A$25.8m of cash that is about the whole market value?

Value pick (rulebook section 5a): no. The bad case, A$0.10, is 26% below the A$0.135 price; the rule allows 10% (A$0.1215). The middling case, A$0.20, is 48% above the price and would clear the 25% test on its own. On these outcomes the bad leg passes only at a price of about A$0.11 or lower. As an ordinary research pick, the event inside six months (the 1H FY27 result) gives no edge: the expected price by 31 March 2027 is about A$0.135, a loss of about 9% after costs.

Order of work. The brief and the fact sheet both state the A$0.135 price, so it was known from the start. The Norgate price series was also opened early, for the liquidity check, before the valuation was written. No input to "What the filings show" or "Valuation" comes from Step One's own share price; the peer multiples use the peers' prices. Step One's price history is used only in "Why the market is wrong", "Outcomes and chances" and the checks, where the shares' own record shows how the market prices this cash.

In short. The fact sheet's two anchors don't hold up as written. Its "if it goes our way" value of A$0.334 needs EBITDA of A$6m, more than three times FY26's adjusted A$1.7m and four times the second half's run-rate, while revenue was falling faster, not slower: down 24.5% in the first half of FY26 and 29.5% in the second, with sales on the company's own website down 37%. That is the good case, not the middling one. Its "against us" value of A$0.124 is a fair asset value (my own bad case leaves about A$21m to A$26m of cash in June 2028, because running down surplus stock offsets most of the losses), but it is not a floor for the price. A minority holder can't reach that cash: the founder controls 57.9%, the board says the cash is there to fund the reset, dividends wait until about A$6m of profit has been earned back, and the company has never bought back a share. The shares already closed at A$0.115 on 3 September 2026, 0.83 times cash, and in the bad case they would more likely trade at about A$0.10. A take-private by the founder is possible (he has the money, from a A$28.3m sell-down at A$1.70 in September 2024), but nothing in the filings points to one; if it came, holders would probably be offered about A$0.17 to A$0.22. The expected price by 31 August 2028 is about A$0.17, roughly 14% after costs over 22 months, but the bad case breaks the value pick rule. Wait for the 1H FY27 result.

## The company in plain words
Step One designs and sells its own underwear, mainly men's trunks in bamboo-based fabric with an anti-chafe panel, through its own website, and increasingly through Amazon, TikTok Shop, John Lewis, The Iconic, Decathlon and eBay [S1, S3]. Manufacturing and warehousing are outsourced; the head office in Australia had 26 staff at 30 June 2026 (33 a year earlier) [S1]. Revenue in FY26 was A$63.7m: Australia A$35.0m, the UK A$26.2m and the US A$2.5m; 82% came through its own website and 18% through other retailers' channels [S1]. Gross margins are high (75.9% in FY26 before the stock write-down) and the main cost is advertising (A$21.1m, 33% of sales) [S1].

Who runs it. Founder Greg Taylor is CEO, on A$500,000 a year including super with no bonus or equity awards [S1, remuneration report]. Michael Reddie is an executive director and Chief Legal Officer; his firm, Reddie Lawyers, was also paid A$288,000 in FY26 [S1, note 25]. The chair is David Gallop. Rick Dennis, the audit committee chair, resigned on 16 October 2026, leaving a board of four: two independents (Gallop and Catherine Thompson) and two executives (Taylor and Reddie). A new independent director is being sought [S16]. The company secretary changed on 20 October 2026 (both MUFG practitioners) [S17].

Who owns it [S1, shareholder information at 4 August 2026]. Dallard Road Pty Ltd and Gregory Taylor hold 107,327,144 shares (57.91%), the only substantial holder. Then Faraday Capital and Michael Reddie 2.42%, Citicorp Nominees 2.14%, Pacific Custodians (the employee share trust) 1.57%, BNP Paribas (NZ) 1.09%, Hancroft 1.07%. The top 20 hold 72.39%. ## Why big investors may be overlooking it
- Size: a market value of about A$25m.
- Index: not in the S&P/ASX 300; removed from the All Ordinaries on 22 September 2025 [S19].
- Register: 57.9% held by the founder and CEO, so the free float is about A$10m and he decides every ordinary resolution.
- Liquidity: a median of about A$14,000 a day in October 2026, and about A$7,400 to A$9,500 over 40 and 60 sessions (see Checks).
- Sector: online consumer retail, out of favour; peers below trade at low multiples.
- History: FY26 guidance of A$10m to A$12m of EBITDA was given at the AGM on 12 November 2025 and withdrawn three weeks later [S8, S7].

But it is also cheap for a plain reason: revenue is falling about 30% a year and the cash belongs, in practice, to whoever controls the board.

What the trend says:
- Pricing is better. The second-half gross margin of 79.5% is the reset working: sale days fell from 109 to 27 and sale events made up 26% of direct revenue against 63% [S1]. The company notes 2H FY25 had a mid-year sale and 2H FY26 didn't, so the comparison flatters a little [S1].
- Demand is not stabilising. Revenue fell faster in the second half than the first, and website sales fell 37%. Conversion dropped to 3.4% and returning customers' share of orders fell from 65% to 53%: the existing base is buying less often. The CEO's letter says some customers "returned less frequently as promotional activity was reduced" [S1, CEO letter].
- Advertising is buying less. A$9.8m of advertising in 2H FY26 was slightly more than in 2H FY25 (A$9.5m) for 29% less revenue. Revenue per advertising dollar fell from 4.1 to 2.8.
- New customers are holding up better than revenue (101,000 against 107,000 a year earlier). The "customer database" of 2.12 million counts everyone who ever bought, not active customers [S1].
- Other retailers' channels grew, but the growth slowed from +76% to +47%.
- Orders on its own systems fell 31% in FY26, from 771,000 to 529,000 [S1].

There is no trading update since 19 August 2026. The next read is the AGM on 11 November 2026.

### The bad case, tested harder (brief point 1)
Stock. At 30 June 2026 stock on hand was A$25.871m at cost: A$16.746m saleable and A$9.125m written down to nil [S1, inventory table]. A general provision of A$1.229m (7.3%) sits against the saleable stock, and A$0.332m was in transit, giving the A$15.849m book value.
- The written-down stock turns into almost no cash. In December 2025 clearance events found that "a portion of stock on hand still could not be sold at or above cost" [S4]. The annual report says "the return for effort is expected to diminish over time and increasingly incur additional costs" and the rest will be disposed of in 1H FY27 [S1]. Some goes out free with orders. I assume nil, with any sale proceeds as a small upside. The tax deduction when it goes only helps once there are profits.
- The saleable stock turns into cash at well above book while the business trades: at a 76% to 80% gross margin a full-price sale brings in four to five times cost. The cash benefit comes from not buying it again. In a wind-down it would fetch roughly 40% to 80% of book through clearance (at 75% off a full price of four times cost, a unit still returns about its cost).

Working capital the business needs. Step One "targets approximately 12 months' worth of inventory" against supply interruptions [S10, inventory section]. At FY26's cost of goods (A$15.3m) that is about what it holds. At the bad case's FY28 sales (about A$41m, cost of goods about A$9m to A$10m), about A$4m to A$6m of stock would not need replacing. It pays factories quickly (A$1.4m owed), customers pay through card gateways within a month (A$0.9m due), and production orders were down to A$1.3m. A business of this size needs perhaps A$5m of operating cash on top of its stock.

Lease and other obligations. One office lease: A$0.794m, of which A$0.285m falls due within a year, A$0.286m in one to two years and A$0.223m in two to five, at 5.95% [S1, note 20]. Production orders A$1.255m. Nothing else: no debt, guarantees or capital commitments [S1].

Cash burn if revenue falls another 25%. The cost lines move like this (share of revenue): gross margin, delivery and payment fees together leave 51.2% in FY26 and 55.7% in 2H FY26 (57.6% in FY25); advertising is chosen by management (33% in FY26, 36% in 2H FY26); overheads are about A$9.7m a year and mostly fixed [S3 slide 5]. ### Range

## The players and what the rules allow (the take-private angle)

What the rules let him do (Corporations Act 2001 [R2]; ASX Guidance Note 33 [R1]). I couldn't re-open the Act's section text: AustLII returned a bot check, and I did not open the legislation.gov.au compilation. The provisions below are stated from the Act as it stands; the red team should check them.
- Creep (section 611 item 9): having held more than 19% for six months, he can buy up to 3% every six months, about 5.56m shares (about A$0.75m at A$0.135). At the median of 102,000 shares a day that is about 54 days of the whole market's volume. He hasn't used it.
- Company buybacks (sections 257B to 257D): an on-market buyback of up to 10% of shares in 12 months needs no vote; 18.5m shares would lift him to 64.3%, and an increase from a buyback is exempt from the 20% rule (section 611 item 19). An equal-access buyback or an equal capital reduction (section 256C, ordinary resolution) can return cash; his votes carry an ordinary resolution. A selective buyback needs a special resolution with no votes in favour from those selling, and holders can only be offered it, not forced.
- Takeover bid: compulsory acquisition needs 90% (section 661A), so he would need about 59.5m of the 78.0m outside shares (76%). With 1,293 holders below a marketable parcel and many inactive holders, 90% is hard.
- Scheme of arrangement (section 411): 75% of votes cast and a majority by number of holders voting, with his shares voting separately or excluded; the court can set the headcount test aside. With low minority turnout, a scheme at a price an expert calls fair or reasonable would very likely pass.
- Delisting (Guidance Note 33): ASX usually requires a special resolution and at least a month to sell after it [R1, section 2.7]. ASX "may impose a voting exclusion on any party whom ASX considers will have a material informational advantage", which "will generally arise in the context of a controlling security holder or directors and senior managers"; Taylor is both. ASX's stated concern is removal used "to deny minority security holders a market for their securities in order to coerce them into accepting a current or planned offer from a controlling security holder to buy their securities at an undervalue" [R1, section 2.1]. A forced delisting at a low price is unlikely to be allowed.

Is it plausible? For: the shares trade at about cash, he has the money, listing costs (directors' fees of about A$0.37m, audit of A$0.18m, registry, secretary and investor relations) are large against A$1.7m of adjusted EBITDA [S1], and as CEO he knows the business best. Against: he sold at A$1.70 rather than bought, he hasn't bought at A$0.13, his public statements are about staying a long-term listed holder, and the board is recruiting a new independent director to chair the audit committee [S16], which a company planning to delist wouldn't usually bother with. I put the chance of a proposal within 24 months at about 10% to 15%, and within six months under 5%.

What minorities would likely be offered. An independent expert would value 100% of the company on a control basis, starting near net tangible assets (19.8c without the tax asset, 21.9c with it), less the cost of ongoing losses. A controller wanting a scheme to pass needs at least "reasonable". That points to about A$0.17 to A$0.22, centre A$0.19, a premium of 26% to 63% to A$0.135. I found no comparable ASX consumer company taken private by its founder to check this against, so it rests on the expert's likely starting point, not on precedent. A proposal is folded into the middling outcome below.

## Why the market is wrong
Mostly it isn't, yet. At A$0.135 the market values the business at about nothing and the cash at about face value. For a business shrinking about 30% a year whose cash is controlled by one holder, that is a reasonable price, and the shares have already traded below it (A$0.115, 0.83 times cash, after the FY26 result).

It would be wrong in two ways, neither yet visible:
1. If the reset holds. The second half's 79.5% gross margin is real, the business made a small cash profit before tax at that margin, and on Cettire's multiple a business that stops shrinking at about A$55m of sales is worth about A$0.20 or more. The 1H FY27 result (Black Friday and Christmas) is the first proper test, because 1H FY26 was full of clearance sales.
2. If the controller moves. A buyback, a capital return or a take-private would turn the cash into value for minorities at or above about A$0.17.

Neither is signalled, and the trend in the latest half is the wrong way.

## What would make this wrong

## What the price assumes
FY25, to December 2025 [S1, S2]:
- revenue €138.4m, up 3.9% (6.3% in constant currency);
- subscription revenue €75.6m;
- EBITDA €30.4m (21.9%);
- profit after tax €1.0m;
- free cash flow €6.4m.

1H26, to June 2026 [S3]:
- revenue €72.5m, up 7.9%;
- subscription revenue €41.9m, up 15%;
- annual recurring revenue (ARR) €87.8m, up 14.9%;
- EBITDA €17.4m (24.0%);
- profit after tax €1.9m;
- free cash flow €10.9m.

FY26 revenue guidance is €147m to €152m, reaffirmed on 26 October [S4].

The business is valued at about:
- 2.8 times FY26 revenue, or 2.6 times a likely FY27;
- 4.8 times ARR;
- about 11.6 times FY26 EBITDA (about €36m at 24%), or 10.3 times a likely FY27 at the company's 25% target.

The catch is development spending. Fineos capitalises most of it: €15.0m of €24.5m of R&D in 1H26, against €13.2m of amortisation [S3]. EBITDA leaves that spending out, so it flatters the business. EBITDA less capitalised development was only about €2m in the half. On cash earnings, the business is valued at more than 40 times.

So the price assumes Fineos keeps growing subscriptions and that margins widen as R&D falls as a share of revenue. The company's own targets [S2]:
- EBITDA of 25% in FY27 and 40% in FY29;
- R&D of 30% of revenue in FY27 and 25% in FY29;
- subscriptions of 65% of revenue in FY27 and 75% in FY29.

It doesn't assume they're all met.

## The one thing the market is missing
Stated as the rulebook asks: the market has marked Fineos down with all listed software on the fear that AI makes software easier to replace. But Fineos runs the regulated core claims and absence systems of US group-benefits insurers. Those are systems customers take years to change, and regulators hold insurers to account for them. And demand is being pushed up by state paid-leave laws.

## The test outside the filings
Done on 9 November 2026, under docs/RESEARCH_PROCESS.md step 2. The questions the filings can't answer:
1. Why have the shares fallen, and is it Fineos or the sector?
2. Who has been selling, and who is buying?
3. Is demand for its absence and claims systems growing?
4. What do buyers pay for insurance software businesses like this?
5. How much should the founder's control count?

What the evidence showed:

1. Mostly the sector, partly the selling.
   - Moves in 2026 to 30 October (Norgate) [S11]:

     | Company | 2 Jan to 27 Feb | 18 Aug to 15 Oct | Year to 30 Oct |
     | --- | --- | --- | --- |
     | Fineos | -14% | -30% | -30% |
     | Xero | -26% | -30% | -45% |
     | WiseTech | -31% | -20% | -41% |
     | Integrated Research | -12% | -15% | -34% |
     | Nuix | +4% | +14% | -3% |

   - Fineos fell in the same waves as the big ASX software names, when markets worried that AI would erode software businesses. Morningstar lowered its moat ratings on four ASX shares because of AI [S14].
   - Fineos's own news in the same months was good:
     - OneAmerica signed a 10-year licence for the full AdminSuite, a "large financial deal" [S5];
     - the 1H26 result showed the first half-year profit;
     - the September quarter reaffirmed guidance [S3, S4].
2. The selling came from an index and a fund. The founder is buying.
   - Fineos left the S&P/ASX All Technology index from 22 June 2026 [S9]. On 19 June, the last day before, 1.49m shares traded, about fourteen times a normal day. Michael Kelly, the founder, bought 300,000 that day at A$2.00 [S7, S11].
   - He had also bought 169,492 at A$2.36 in April [S7]. He now holds about 174.5m, about 50.6% [S7, S12].
   - Regal Partners ceased to be a substantial holder on 3 November [S8]. On 29 October, 3.5m shares traded and the price rose to A$2.04, so someone bought a large block [S11].
   - Selector Funds Management raised its holding to 9.36% in March [S10].
   - Short positions are about 0.5% [S15].
3. Demand is growing, helped by US paid-leave law.
   - Fineos describes itself as the leading provider of core employee benefits systems in North America [S5]. Its absence product manages leave for insurers and employers.
   - State paid family and medical leave schemes keep spreading [S16]:
     - Delaware and Minnesota started paying benefits in January 2026, and Maine in May 2026;
     - Maryland starts in January 2028;
     - Virginia passed a scheme in April 2026 that pays from December 2028.
   - Each new scheme gives insurers and employers more leave to administer and more rules to follow.
   - The 2026 client news [S2, S5]:
     - OneAmerica (10 years, the full suite);
     - the Saskatchewan Teachers' Federation;
     - Guardian moving its old systems across.
   - Morningstar (used as a pointer only) notes that US regulators hold insurers responsible for AI used in their systems, including vendors'. That favours certified, audited platforms [S14].
4. Buyers have paid much more than today's price.
   - Advent agreed in August 2025 to take Sapiens, a larger insurance software company, private for about US$2.5bn, a 64% premium [S17]. That was about 4.6 times its 2024 revenue of US$542m, and about 25 times EBITDA [S17].
   - Duck Creek, a property and casualty peer, went to private equity earlier [S17].
   - Fineos is valued at 2.8 times revenue. It's smaller and has lower margins than Sapiens had, so the discount isn't all mispricing.
5. The founder's control cuts both ways.
   - Kelly is chief executive, and has been executive chairman since January 2026 [S12].
   - No one can bid without him, and Fineos's articles copy Australia's 20% takeover threshold and 3% creep rule [S12]. So a bid is less likely than for a widely held company.
   - Kelly can buy 3% every six months. He has been buying, not selling.

What this changes: it supports the middling case, through demand, the client wins and the founder's buying. But it confirms that the discount is a sector-wide judgement on software, not a forced sale specific to Fineos. That's why the middling case stays at today's multiples rather than recovering.

## The company in plain words
Fineos was founded in Dublin in 1993 by Michael Kelly and listed on the ASX in 2019 [S12]. It sells core software to life, accident and health insurers, mostly for US group benefits (cover employers buy for staff):
- claims;
- absence and leave;
- policy administration;
- billing.

Its clients include Guardian, New York Life, Unum and OneAmerica [S1, S5]. About 71% of revenue is in US dollars, and its costs are mostly in euros [S1].

It is moving from licences and services to cloud subscriptions:
- subscription revenue was 57.8% of revenue in 1H26;
- the target is 65% in FY27 [S2, S3].

Client concentration [S12]:
- two clients each provided more than 10% of FY25 revenue, 14.7% and 10.9%;
- in FY24 there were three.

  - 2.56 times next year's revenue;

On revenue alone, the middling case is A$2.62. On EBITDA alone, it's A$3.16.

## Why the market is wrong
1. It prices Fineos with all software as an AI casualty. Fineos runs regulated core systems that insurers take years to replace, and it's adding AI to them, not being replaced by it.
2. It doesn't credit the demand from US state paid-leave schemes, four of which started or were passed in 2026.
3. It treats the June and November selling as news. It was an index change and a fund leaving, and the founder bought into the first.

It's right that:
- most of the reported EBITDA is development spending that Fineos capitalises, so cash earnings are thin;
- two clients make up a quarter of revenue;
- the founder's control makes a bid unlikely without him;
- revenue growth is still single digits, because services revenue isn't growing.

## Outcomes and chances
Resolve date: 31 August 2028 (661 days away). No dividends are assumed.

| Outcome | What happens | Chance | Price by resolve date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Good | Margins near target and a re-rating, or a bid | 20% | A$3.92 |

These chances were set after the evidence from outside the filings. Client wins, paid-leave demand and the founder's buying put the middling case first. Client concentration and the sector's AI scare keep the worse and bad cases at 35% together.

For the card, the first two combine into one bad case: 35% at A$1.69 (the chance-weighted average of A$1.26 and A$1.87). The card would read 35% at A$1.69, 45% at A$2.89 and 20% at A$3.92.

Expected price: 0.35 × 1.695 + 0.45 × 2.890 + 0.20 × 3.92 = A$2.68, or +22.8% before costs. After about 3.8% of costs it's about +19%. The costs are A$15 brokerage each way on about A$2,250, a spread of about 2% (it has ranged from 1% to 8% at the close), and the 0.5% currency fee. The rulebook minimum over 661 days is 12.9%, so that test passes.

The value test (rulebook section 5a):
- The bad case of A$1.69 is 22.3% below A$2.18, inside the 30% limit.
- Against that fall the middling case needs to be +33.5%, and it's +32.6%.
- So the test fails, by less than one point.

It passes at about A$2.17. There:
- the bad case is 21.9% below;
- the middling case is +33.2% (it needs +32.9%);
- the expected gain after costs is about +19%.

## What would make this wrong
- A top-two client moves off Fineos or cuts its scope.
- AI-native competitors or larger platforms take new US group-benefits deals on price.
- Capitalised development has to be written down, showing that the EBITDA margin was overstated.
- The euro strengthens against the US dollar, cutting reported revenue and margins, as in FY25.
- Kelly uses his control to take Fineos private cheaply, creeping 3% at a time.

Exit rule, if it's ever bought: sell if ARR growth falls below 8%, if a client of more than 10% of revenue leaves, or if FY27 guidance points to an EBITDA margin below 22%. A close 25% below the buy price means a review, not a sale.

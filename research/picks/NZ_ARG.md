# Argosy Property (NZX:ARG): deep dive. Not a value pick: the bad case (NZ$0.89) is 14% below the price and the middling case (NZ$1.10) only 6% above it

Written 2026-11-08 by the research desk. Price at writing: NZ$1.035 (close on 6 November 2026, FinFeedAPI, data/prices/NZX/ARG.csv; the NZX instrument page shows the same close, bid NZ$1.025 and offer NZ$1.035 [S17]). Market value: NZ$904.6m (873,970,395 shares, NZX instrument page [S17]).
Status: finished. This is a simulation and not financial advice.
Fact sheet: research/facts/NZ_ARG.md, grade B. The question it set: are Argosy's book values (average market yield 6.62%, office 7.05%) what the buildings would sell for, when the price implies values about 22% lower?

Value pick (rulebook section 5a, from 8 Nov 2026): it doesn't qualify on either leg. The bad case is 14% below the price (the limit is 10%) and the middling case is 6% above it (it needs 25%). Counting the seven dividends in the window, the middling case is 17.5% above the price, still short of 25%.

Order of work. The brief and the fact sheet both quote the price, so it was in view before the work began. The filings section and the valuation below take no input from Argosy's own share price: they use the filings, other NZX property companies' filings and the prices the market pays for those peers. Argosy's price is used only in "Why the market is wrong", "Outcomes and chances" and the checks. I checked the FinFeed close against NZX: both show NZ$1.035 for 6 November. FinFeed has no rows for 2, 3 and 4 November 2026, which Yahoo Finance shows at NZ$1.045, NZ$1.045 and NZ$1.02 [X1].

Corrections to the fact sheet, for the red team:
1. The 7.05% office figure is a market yield (fully let market rent divided by value), not the valuers' cap rate. The office cap rate is 6.51%, industrial 6.06%, large format retail 6.51% and the portfolio 6.26% [S3, page 9]. The market yields are 6.25%, 7.05%, 6.94% and 6.62% [S1, note 5].
2. "NZ listed property at about 0.6 to 0.7 times NTA across the board" is true of only three companies. At the latest closes Argosy is at 0.65 times, Stride 0.66 and Investore 0.62, but Kiwi Property is at 0.83, Precinct 0.89 (0.83 before its capital return news on 30 October), PFI 0.77 and Vital 0.75 (Valuation, Method 3).
3. The chair's sales were made as a professional trustee of other people's family trusts and a charitable trust (non-beneficial interests), not with his own shares [S15, S1 page 73]. Stuart McLauchlan's purchase was beneficial.
4. FY26 net distributable income (7.05c) and AFFO (6.85c) include a one-off Investment Boost tax deduction worth about NZ$1.6m (0.19c) from 224 Neilson Street [S5]. Underlying AFFO was about 6.66c, and the 6.65c dividend was 100% of it.
5. Of the two FY26 sales, only 4 Henderson Place sold above book (16.6% above the September 2025 value). 143 Lambton Quay sold "in line with expectations for the 31 March 2026 valuation" [S8, S9].
6. The NZ$125m green bond whose maturity the fact sheet didn't read is ARG030, 2.20%, due 27 October 2027 [S1, note 14]. A third bond, ARG010 (NZ$100m), matured on 27 March 2026 and was replaced by bank debt.
7. The non-core list is not fixed: seven properties worth NZ$148m in November 2025 [S5], then, after two sales, "a further six" worth NZ$139m in May 2026 [S2], so some were added. The filings don't name them.

> In short: the buildings would probably sell for close to book. Industrial and convenience retail have sold at or above book in 2024 to 2026 (Argosy's own sales, PFI and Investore), and prime office 3% to 5% below it (Kiwi's ASB North Wharf, Precinct's PwC Tower). Marked to that evidence, NTA is about NZ$1.52 to NZ$1.56, nowhere near the 22% discount the price implies. But the market doesn't price NZ listed property on NTA. It prices cash earnings, and on those Argosy is priced like its diversified peers: an AFFO yield of 6.6% against Kiwi Property's 6.6%, Precinct's 6.4% and Stride's 6.8%. Unlike COF, Argosy's dividend is covered by its own AFFO measure (97% paid out, 100% on the underlying figure); only on the strictest basis (all office and retail spending and capitalised interest taken off) does it fall short, at 118%. The discount to NTA is the sector's, it has a cash and interest-rate reason, and closing it needs rates to fall, a takeover, or capital management like Precinct's 30 October capital return and buyback. As a listed company Argosy is worth about NZ$1.04 to NZ$1.24 a share; NZ$1.40 to NZ$1.56 only if someone turns book value into cash. The expected price by 30 June 2028 is about NZ$1.10, plus 11.5c of dividends: about 17% above today before costs and 14% to 16% after them, roughly 8.5% to 9.7% a year, which is about what the NZ market itself might return (my assumption). No card.

## The company in plain words
Argosy owns 49 commercial buildings worth NZ$2,243.0m at 31 March 2026: 33 industrial (NZ$1,234.2m, 55%), 12 office (NZ$789.7m, 35%) and four large format retail (NZ$219.2m, 10%). By region, 72% is in Auckland, 25% in Wellington and 3% elsewhere [S1 note 5, S3]. It earns rent: NZ$137.5m of rent and NZ$22.3m of recovered outgoings in FY26, NZ$120.8m of net property income (industrial NZ$60.9m, office NZ$46.2m before NZ$2.0m of ground rent at 39 Market Place, large format retail NZ$13.6m) [S1, note 4]. Government administration pays 31% of the rent [S3, page 36].

Who runs it. Argosy is internally managed: the management company, Argosy Property Management Limited, is a wholly owned subsidiary, as is a Cook Islands captive insurer, Argosy Cover Limited [S1, note 24]. Peter Mence has been chief executive since May 2009 and intends to step down by the 2027 annual meeting; the board is searching for a successor. Dave Fraser is CFO. Jeff Morrison chairs the board and is to be succeeded by Martin Stearne after the 2027 annual meeting [S2, S17].

Who owns it, at 31 March 2026 [S1, page 80]: 6,481 holders; the top 20 registered holders hold about 64.5%, almost all nominee and custodian accounts (BNP Paribas Nominees 9.69%, ACC 8.50%, FNZ Custodians 6.42%, Forsyth Barr Custodians 5.40%, HSBC Nominees 5.19%, Apex 3.79%, NZ Depository Nominee 3.62%, Citibank Nominees 3.45%, JPMorgan 2.67%, Investment Custodial Services 2.57%, State Street via HSBC 2.34%, and nine more between 0.84% and 1.99%, including the NZ Superannuation Fund's nominee at 1.15%). ## Why big investors may be overlooking it
They mostly aren't. Argosy is owned by institutions: ACC, FirstCape and Salt hold 21.7% between them, and the custodians in the top 20 hold most of the rest [S1, S14]. It traded a median NZ$373,400 a day over the last 20 sessions (Checks). It isn't one of the 20 largest NZX companies (config/nzx_top20.csv), and I didn't check its index membership beyond that, or broker coverage. What does apply: NZ listed property is out of favour. Over the 12 months to 6 November 2026 Argosy fell 18.2%, Kiwi Property 14.3%, PFI 11.9%, Vital 18.7%, Investore 20.8%, Stride 24.2% and Precinct 18.2% to 30 October (data/prices/NZX). The fall is the sector's, and Argosy's is in the middle of it.

Testing it the way COF was tested:
- Underlying AFFO. The FY26 tax charge benefited from an Investment Boost deduction on 224 Neilson Street worth about NZ$1.6m [S5]. Without it AFFO is 6.66c and the dividend is 100% of it.
- Rent-free periods aren't a hidden add-back here. Argosy capitalises incentives and spreads them over the lease; the net change in lease incentives in FY26 was minus NZ$1.0m [S1, note 5], and the AFFO deducts the NZ$1.4m capitalised. COF's add-back was 38% of its FFO.
- The strictest view. Taking off all capital spending on office and retail buildings (NZ$8.7m, in place of the NZ$3.3m of maintenance) and the NZ$3.4m of interest capitalised to developments leaves 5.65c, and the dividend is 118% of it. Some of that office spending wins leases (the NZ$13m Stout Street project came with MBIE's nine-year renewal), which is why peers also leave it out of AFFO.
- The cash flow statement agrees with the company's AFFO: operating cash NZ$67.4m, less capitalised interest NZ$3.4m, maintenance NZ$3.3m and incentives NZ$1.4m, is NZ$59.3m (6.87c) against NZ$57.4m of dividends declared [S1].

So, unlike COF (254% of cash AFFO), Argosy's dividend is covered on its own measure and on cash flow; it is fully paid out, with no room to spare.

Policy and guidance. In May 2026 the board moved the policy from 85% to 100% of AFFO to 80% to 95% of FFO, saying AFFO "has been significantly more volatile" [S2]. On FY26's 7.40c of FFO that range is 5.9c to 7.0c, so 6.65c fits. FY27 guidance is 6.65c, "retained ... as a result of increased market uncertainty" [S2]. The first-quarter FY27 dividend of 1.6625c was paid on 23 September 2026; the dividend reinvestment plan is suspended [S13]. FY26 guidance of 6.65c was reaffirmed at the interim result and paid [S5, S2]; FY24 and FY25 also paid 6.65c [S1], but I didn't read those years' guidance. Argosy gives no profit guidance.

### Debt
- At 31 March 2026: NZ$857.6m of debt (NZ$632.6m of bank loans, NZ$225m of green bonds), 37.2% of total assets, 36.1% after the April and May sales settled [S1 note 14, S3 page 19]. The board's band is 30% to 40%. The only covenant the accounts describe is that loans must not exceed 50% of property value; "all banking covenants have been met" [S1, note 11]. On 36.1%, values could fall about 28% before gearing reached 50% (python3 -I). The filings I read don't state an interest cover covenant; reported interest cover is 2.7 times (2.5 in FY25) [S3, page 20].
- Rates and hedges: weighted average cost 4.65% (5.11% a year earlier), including margins and line fees; 74% fixed; swaps and fixed bonds average 3.62% with 2.2 years left [S1, S3 page 20]. From the hedging chart [S3, page 34], the hedges in place fall from about NZ$635m at March 2026 to about NZ$540m at March 2027 and NZ$335m at March 2028, at about 3.6% rising to about 4.0%.
- Maturities [S1 note 14, S3 page 34]: ARG020 (NZ$100m, 2.90%) matured on 29 October 2026, to be repaid from bank debt (announced; I found no later notice); ARG030 (NZ$125m, 2.20%) matures on 27 October 2027; bank tranches of NZ$200m (October 2028), NZ$325m (October 2029), NZ$100m (October 2030) and NZ$100m (July 2032), at margins and line fees of 1.07% to 1.45%. Argosy said on 1 October 2026 it is refinancing the syndicated facility, which rises by NZ$25m to NZ$750m, and plans a green loan framework [S11]. NZX shows ARG030's last quote as 4.67, which I read as its yield, since NZX debt securities are quoted on yield [S17].
- Green bonds: ARG010 (NZ$100m) was repaid on 27 March 2026 with a new bank tranche; ARG020 and ARG030 are secured on the properties [S1].

### Is Argosy cheaper than its peers, and is the whole sector mispriced?
- Against NTA, yes: 0.65 times against a peer median of 0.76. On cash, no: its 6.62% AFFO yield is the same as Kiwi Property's and between Precinct's and Stride's. Its dividend yield, 6.43%, is in the middle. Its lower multiple of NTA comes from earning less cash on its book (4.3% of NTA), partly because of the NZ$288m of value-add property.
- The sector: buildings sell at about book to private and offshore buyers, while every listed company trades below NTA. At today's yields, Argosy would trade at NTA only if investors accepted an AFFO yield of about 4.3%, which is below the 4.42% seven-year base rate and the 5.62% Kiwi Property just paid on secured bonds [P4]. Listed investors are asking about 6% to 7% in cash plus whatever the rent grows. That's a premium over secured property bonds, not an obvious mistake. The gap between the private and listed prices is real, but it has lasted: Argosy traded between 0.64 and 0.80 times NTA at the four balance dates I checked since March 2024, and at 0.81 at its best, in November 2025.
- What would close it within 24 months: lower long-term rates (they have been rising: the base rate for secured property bonds went from 4.05% on PFI's six-and-a-half-year bond in April to 4.42% on Kiwi Property's seven-year bond in October 2026 [P10, P4]); a buyer for the whole company; or the company selling buildings at book and buying back shares, as Precinct has just started to do. An illustration of the last: selling the NZ$139m of non-core property at book, buying back NZ$100m of shares at NZ$1.10 and repaying NZ$39m of debt would lift NTA to about NZ$1.66 and AFFO to about 6.96c a share (assuming a 6% yield on what is sold), small gains in themselves. Precinct's 7.4% on the day shows the market reacts more than the arithmetic does.

### Range
As a listed company Argosy is worth about NZ$1.04 to NZ$1.24 a share, central about NZ$1.12: its cash earnings at what the market pays peers' cash earnings, cross-checked by peers' and its own multiples of NTA. The low end depends on AFFO holding near 6.8c and the market keeping diversified NZ property at about a 6.6% cash yield. The high end, NZ$1.40 to NZ$1.56, is the buildings marked to sales evidence, and it depends on a sale of buildings with money returned, or a bid; nothing in the filings says either is planned.

## Why the market is wrong
On the buildings, it is. At NZ$1.035 the shares imply a property value of about NZ$1,750m, 22% below book: a cap rate of about 8.0% against 6.26%, and a contract yield of 7.3% against 5.72% (market value NZ$904.6m, plus about NZ$812m of debt after the April and May sales, plus NZ$45m of other liabilities, less NZ$12m of other assets; python3 -I). Recent sales of industrial and retail property have gone at 5.4% to 5.9% initial yields and at or above book, and office at 0% to 5% below book.

But the market isn't pricing the buildings, and on what it is pricing, Argosy isn't cheap. It pays out all of its AFFO, has paid the same 6.65c for four years, carries a fifth of its income expiring in the year to March 2028, takes 31% of its rent from government, and has cheap bonds to replace at higher rates. On that cash it yields what Kiwi Property and Precinct yield. The gap to NTA is the sector's, it has a cash and interest-rate reason, and the dated results in the window are more likely to confirm the present NTA than to move the price to it. What would close it is not on any calendar: falling rates, a buyer, or capital management. I can't say within 24 months why the market should reprice Argosy against its peers, so as a value pick there is no trade.

## What would make this wrong

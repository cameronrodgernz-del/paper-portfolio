## The company in plain words
Austco designs and sells nurse call and clinical communication systems for hospitals and aged care: the call points at the bedside, the over-door lights and displays, the Tacera software that routes a patient's call to the right nurse's phone (Pulse Mobile), real-time location tags and enterprise reporting [A3]. It doesn't make the hardware itself: products are built by contract manufacturers, which it moved out of China in 2025 [A18]. It sells through its own subsidiaries in Australia, New Zealand, the US, Canada, Singapore and the UK and through resellers in more than 60 countries, and says it supports more than 5,000 facilities [A3]. It has 271 employees [A13].

Where the money comes from (FY26, A$94.2m) [A1, note 2]: equipment A$51.9m (55%), installation A$31.0m (33%), software licences and maintenance agreements A$11.4m (12%). By region: Australia and New Zealand A$52.8m (of which Australia A$32.0m), North America A$30.4m, Asia A$6.7m, Europe A$4.4m [A1, notes 2 and 5]. A system is specified when a hospital is designed, tendered with the build, installed over months and then maintained for years; the company itself describes the cycle as lengthening in FY26 [A3, slide 10]. Its largest customer is a US hospital operator with about 180 hospitals; the five-year agreement renewed in May 2025 brought in more than A$49m over the previous five years (about A$10m a year, roughly a tenth of revenue) and has no minimum purchase commitment [A18].

Since late 2023 Austco has bought four of its own resellers at 3.5 times EBITDA plus an earn-out: Teknocorp (November 2023), Amentco (May 2024, Queensland), G&S Technologies (May 2025, New Zealand) and Medical Communications Systems (August 2026, South Australia) [A3, slides 20 and 24; A11]. In October 2026 it agreed its first purchases of product and software businesses, RTM and CareTech, both of Brisbane, completing about 1 December 2026 [A9; A10].

Who runs it: Clayton Astles, chief executive; Brendan Maher, chief financial officer and company secretary; Graeme Billings, chair [A4]. Auditor: Grant Thornton [A1].

Who owns it [A4, shareholder information at 26 August 2026]: substantial holders Asia Pac Holding (54,777,798 shares, 14.62%; the register shows Asia Pac Holdings and Asia Pac Technology entities, one of them for the John Bennetts super fund, together 55.3m) and Australian Ethical Investment (51,201,161, 14.06%). HSBC Custody Nominees holds 21.04% on the register. The rest of the top 20 are mostly private investment companies and small funds (Moat Investments 3.37%, Dixson Trust 2.94%, Gliocas 2.78%, DMX Capital 0.79%). The top 20 hold 62.2%; there are 1,669 holders. No substantial holder notice has been lodged in the past 12 months [A22]. Directors' on-market trades in the past year: the chief executive sold 400,000 shares from 4 to 10 March 2026 at an average A$0.345 [D1]; director Brett Burns made a small net purchase on 2 March 2026 (sold 68,319 held directly at A$0.35 and bought 97,222 indirectly at A$0.358) [D2].

What this shows:
- Acquisitions carry the growth. Australia's external revenue was A$31.6m in FY25 and A$32.0m in FY26 (+1.3%); New Zealand (Australia and NZ less Australia) went from A$6.6m to A$20.8m, the effect of buying G&S on 30 May 2025 [A1, note 5; A14, note 5; A17]. Teknocorp and Amentco were owned for all of both years, so Australia's 1.3% is close to organic. The group excluding New Zealand fell from A$74.8m to A$73.5m (-1.8%). The company says consolidation now removes about A$3m a year of Austco's former sales to the resellers it bought [A2]. For G&S those sales ran through Austco's New Zealand company (G&S's order book was added "less orders they had open with Austco NZ" [A14]), so they sit mostly in the New Zealand figures left out here, and the Australian resellers were already owned in FY25.
- North America is the one growing region. In US dollars (average rates for each half from the RBA series [M3]) it was US$17.7m in FY25 and US$20.6m in FY26 (+16.7%), but the second half was only 2.5% up (US$10.2m to US$10.5m) after a 36% first half.
- Asia's fall is the end of a project rather than a delay: the Jurong hospitals in Singapore were installed in FY24 and FY25 (a A$5.5m contract after the phase 2 win of September 2024 [A23]) and have now moved to a ten-year maintenance contract worth A$4.2m in all from October 2026 [A3, slide 12; A20].
- Software and maintenance grew 19% to A$11.4m, but North America's software and maintenance revenue fell from A$4.5m to A$1.6m while Australia and NZ's rose from A$2.9m to A$7.3m (G&S's maintenance book and, the company says, faster growth in the second half) [A1, note 2; A3, slide 17]. Software licences are booked when sold, so a large FY25 US licence sale not repeated would explain the US fall; the filings don't say.
- Customers' prepayments fell: contract liabilities (money received for work not yet done) dropped from A$6.36m to A$3.80m [A1, note 2(c)], consistent with fewer new project deposits.

- Q1 FY26 revenue was A$23.2m with EBITDA A$4.2m [A8]; Q2 therefore A$25.0m and A$4.06m (H1 FY26 A$48.2m and A$8.26m [A5]).

The tax note shows a charge of A$3.58m before two one-off credits in FY26 (A$0.88m from accelerating R&D deductions and A$0.12m for the prior year), and A$3.12m before a A$0.99m credit for newly recognised tax losses in FY25 [A1, note 6]. Taking out the earn-out revaluations (a A$0.92m gain in FY26, a A$2.20m charge in FY25, neither taxed): underlying profit before tax was A$10.66m in FY26 and A$10.26m in FY25, and after the normal tax charge A$7.08m and A$7.14m (python3 -I). The normal rate is about 30% to 34% because some costs aren't deductible. Cash tax will stay lower for a while: A$2.2m of US tax losses and credits are carried as an asset [A1, note 6(b)].

### 3. Cash
- Operating cash flow A$12.5m in FY26 (A$13.4m in FY25). Less equipment (A$0.79m), capitalised development (A$2.00m) and lease payments (A$1.41m): A$8.3m of free cash flow before acquisitions (FY25: A$7.85m) [A1, cash flow statement]. FY26 was helped by receivables falling A$3.5m and held back by inventory up A$2.0m and prepayments falling A$2.6m [A1, balance sheet].

The G&S earn-out can be paid up to 75% in shares at Austco's election [A17, completion notice]; the company says it expects to fund the remaining earn-outs from cash flow and its new facility [A12, slide 10].
- After MCS (A$2.24m upfront in August [A11]) and the A$12.44m upfront for RTM and CareTech (including A$3.0m held in escrow), funded partly by drawing A$7.0m of a new A$12.0m Commonwealth Bank facility, the company expects about A$7.7m of cash at completion [A9]. A$16.28m less A$2.24m less A$12.44m plus A$7.0m is A$8.60m, so the company's own figure implies about A$0.9m less cash from July to completion than at 30 June, before anything else. In the same months last year operating cash flow was strong (A$9.2m in H1 FY26 [A6]). It may reflect completion adjustments, the timing of tax and bonuses, or caution; the half-year cash flow will show which.
- Earn-outs still to pay: G&S about A$2.50m (March 2027), MCS about A$0.64m (after the FY27 accounts), RTM about A$5.82m and CareTech about A$1.04m (after the FY28 accounts) [A12, slide 10]. Pro forma, cash of A$7.7m less A$7.0m of debt less A$10.0m of earn-outs is net debt of about A$9.3m including earn-outs.
- Book value: net assets A$62.1m, of which goodwill A$21.4m and capitalised development A$6.8m; net tangible assets 8.24c a share [A1]. The deals add goodwill. The goodwill test assumes 8% a year sales growth for five years, cost of sales growing 6% and overheads 3%, at a 14.83% pre-tax discount rate [A1, note 13]. That 8% is the company's planning figure, not what it has delivered organically.

The 1 June 2026 update gave revenue of A$90m to A$95m and EBITDA of A$14.0m to A$14.6m [A7]; the result was A$94.2m and A$14.85m, above the EBITDA range [A1]. ### 6. The RTM and CareTech deal
From the announcements [A9; A10; A12, slides 5, 6 and 9]:
- RTM: monitoring software that watches every connected system on a site (nurse call, duress, access control, CCTV, building management) and flags faults, plus installation and maintenance. 27 staff, more than 600 Australian sites, more than 300 system interfaces. Normalised FY26 revenue about A$15.6m and EBITDA about A$2.2m (14.1% margin), unaudited. Upfront A$9.44m, of which A$3.0m goes into escrow if a key customer contract isn't renewed by completion; it returns to Austco if the contract is renewed at a lower value or not at all, with a 24-month longstop. Earn-out: 6.0 times the average of FY27 and FY28 EBITDA, less the upfront payment, uncapped, estimated at A$5.82m. Estimated total A$15.26m: 6.9 times FY26 normalised EBITDA, or 6.0 times the A$2.54m average the earn-out estimate assumes (python3 -I).
- CareTech: designs lower-cost nurse call and wireless products for aged care and smaller hospitals, made by a contract manufacturer, about 200 sites. FY26 revenue A$3.4m, of which A$1.5m was sales to RTM; normalised EBITDA about A$0.9m (unaudited). Upfront A$3.0m; earn-out 3.5 times the FY27 and FY28 average less the upfront, estimated at A$1.04m; total A$4.04m.
- Together: about A$19.3m for A$3.1m of FY26 normalised EBITDA (6.2 times), or A$3.7m on the earn-out forecasts (5.2 times). The company expects A$10.2m of revenue and A$1.8m of EBITDA from them in FY27 (seven months).
- Completion conditions include renewal of the key RTM customer contract, customer consents, verified source code, and a manufacturing agreement with CareTech's contract manufacturer [A9; A10].

What a quick reader would miss:
- RTM's figures don't look like a software company's. A$15.6m of revenue from 27 staff is about A$580,000 a head, and a 14.1% EBITDA margin is close to an installer's (Austco's own group margin after lease payments is 14.3%). RTM is CareTech's largest customer (A$1.5m of CareTech's A$3.4m). The share of RTM's revenue that is recurring licence and support isn't disclosed. Austco pays 3.5 times for installers and 6 times here because of that recurring software [A12, slide 9].
- The uncapped 6 times earn-out means the vendors are paid 6 times any EBITDA growth RTM shows in FY27 and FY28, including growth from Austco's plan to sell RTM's product to its overseas customers if that is booked in RTM. Most of the deal's near-term upside belongs to the vendors; Austco keeps what grows after FY28.
- It is the first borrowing since the 2024 raising repaid the last facility [A19]. Debt of A$7.0m is small against about A$17m of pro forma EBITDA after leases, but with earn-outs the group moves from A$13.6m net cash (June, after the G&S earn-out) to about A$9.3m net debt.
- The H1 FY27 result will carry only December's month of RTM and CareTech. Whether the deal earns its price shows in the FY27 result (August 2027) and the FY28 earn-out.

## Valuation
Done from the filings. All figures in A$m unless stated; arithmetic in python3 -I.

The earnings base. FY26 EBITDA A$14.85m less lease payments A$1.41m is A$13.44m of EBITDA after leases, the measure comparable with peers that report rent as an expense. Pro forma for a full year of the three deals at the company's normalised figures (MCS A$0.82m, RTM A$2.2m, CareTech A$0.9m): A$17.36m. Net debt to match. Pro forma net debt including all earn-outs is A$9.30m at the company's earn-out forecasts and A$6.35m if RTM and CareTech only hold their FY26 EBITDA (the uncapped formulas then pay A$3.76m and A$0.15m) (python3 -I). Each case below pairs its EBITDA with its own earn-outs.

### Method 2: discounted cash flow
Unlevered free cash flow: EBITDA after leases, less capitalised development and equipment (A$3.1m a year: FY26 spent A$2.79m, plus a little for the acquired businesses), less tax at 28% on EBITDA less A$3.0m of depreciation and amortisation (below the 31% book rate while US losses are used), less working capital of 15% of each year's added revenue (receivables plus inventory plus other assets less payables, contract liabilities and provisions were about 15% of FY26 revenue [A1]). Five years, then a terminal growth rate. Discount rate 11% after tax: the company's own goodwill test uses 14.83% before tax, about 10.4% after; a company this small, with a tenth of revenue from one customer without minimum purchases, deserves at least that. Pro forma revenue about A$113m.

### Cross-check: what buyers pay for these businesses
Austco itself pays 3.5 times EBITDA for nurse call installers and resellers (four times since 2023) and 6 times for RTM [A12, slide 9]. ### The fact sheet's anchors
- "Our way" A$0.375. Its method (pre-tax free cash flow of A$14.56m growing 8% a year for five years at the 14.83% pre-tax rate) recomputes to 37.5c; with tax deducted and an 11% after-tax rate it is 40.1c. Either way it rests on 8% growth, which is the company's goodwill-test planning figure and the good case here. The base business grew about nil in FY26, so a buyer would not pay that today, and the market can't see five years of 8% inside six months. It is the good case, not the middling one.
- "Against us" A$0.207 (H2 FY26 as the run rate, no growth). Close to the six-month bad case below.

### The range
The business is worth about A$0.20 to A$0.34 a share in six months, central A$0.27:
- Bad A$0.20: 4.8 times bad-case EBITDA of A$15.67m, plus A$0.4m net cash after the escrow returns. Cross-check (the share price, labelled): between June and mid-October 2026 the market valued the pre-deal business at about 4.6 to 5.5 times EBITDA after leases.

The main thing each depends on: the bad case on the base business slipping and the multiple falling to Ascom's; the middling on organic revenue holding flat at constant currency; the good case on the second-half slowdown proving to be timing, which the order book doesn't yet show.

## What would make this wrong
1. The slowdown was timing after all. If the US rollout, Brazil and Australia's hospital programmes land in H1 FY27 at once, revenue and EBITDA could jump, and on 6 times A$18.5m the shares would be worth about 34c. I give this 20%; a waiting position costs nothing but the chance.

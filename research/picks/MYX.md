The question: is the damages claim against Cosette, over the failed A$7.40 scheme, worth anything to holders after about A$265m of earn-out liabilities and A$41.1m of convertible notes due 31 December 2026? Value pick (rulebook section 5a, from 8 Nov 2026): it doesn't qualify. The bad case, the "nothing happens" value of the business alone, is about A$0.65, 79% below A$3.08; the limit is 10%. It would qualify only at A$0.72 or below. As an ordinary special situation pick it needs a dated event inside six months that shows the answer, and there isn't one: the claim is at the stage where pleadings have closed, no hearing date has been announced, and only a settlement could fix its value before May 2027.

Order of work: the price was in the brief and the fact sheet, so it was in view from the start. > In short. Mayne won the fight over whether Cosette could walk away (first instance 15 October 2025, Court of Appeal 14 October 2026, both with costs). That fight is the one Cosette's 11 November deadline is about: it is the last day to apply to the High Court for special leave to appeal the Court of Appeal's decision. The damages claim is a separate case, filed on 18 February 2026, with defences in by 22 May 2026 and no hearing date. Three things in the deal contract that the fact sheet left out decide what that claim is worth to a holder. First, Cosette's liability under the contract is capped at the reverse break fee of A$6,718,788.50 (8c a share) unless Mayne proves fraud or a "wilful or intentional" breach. Second, Mayne holds the contract's benefit on trust for "Mayne Shareholders from time to time", and nothing yet says whether damages for the lost premium would go to holders when the deal failed in late 2025 or to whoever holds when the money arrives; a law firm's review notes such claims "have never had traction in Australia". Third, the measure is the premium lost at the time, about A$4.21 a share (A$342m) on the post-failure price, not the A$7.40 deal price. Separately, Mayne agreed on 6 November to sell its Australian arm (the Salisbury plant) for A$55.5m, completing January to March 2027, which lifts the business-alone value from about A$0.51 to about A$0.64 a share. The notes due 31 December are paid from cash on hand (A$67.3m at 30 September), leaving a thin A$14m to A$30m until the sale money arrives. The price, A$3.08, needs about a coin-flip chance that today's holders collect close to the whole lost premium. I put that at 15%. No case.

Corrections to the fact sheet and the check, for the record:
1. The liability cap is missing. Clause 14.6 of the scheme implementation deed (SID) caps everything Cosette must pay "in relation to or under this deed (including as a result of any breach)" at the Cosette Break Fee, A$6,718,788.50, unless the breach was fraud or "wilful or intentional" [M8]. The fact sheet's "claim succeeds for the full lost premium" anchor needs that carve-out to be proved first.
2. Who receives shareholder damages is open. Clause 20.4 makes Mayne trustee "for Mayne Shareholders from time to time" [M8]. The fact sheet assumes any recovery reaches current holders; it may instead belong to the holders who would have been paid A$7.40 in late 2025.
3. The "our way" price is too high. Holders would keep the business (about A$0.65) and receive the premium lost at the time (A$7.40 less what the shares were worth without the deal, about A$4.21 on the A$3.19 close of 11 December 2025 [D2]), less costs: about A$4.9 a share, not A$5.92.
4. The 11 November deadline concerns the termination case (material adverse change, misleading conduct, election to affirm the SID), not the damages claim [M6, C1]. The damages claim also pleads misleading or deceptive conduct, tortious interference and knowing involvement; Cosette's defence was filed on 15 April 2026 and Avista's and Mr Burgstahler's on 22 May 2026 [M1, note 29].
5. New since the fact sheet: the A$55.5m sale of Mayne Pharma International (MPI) on 6 November 2026 [M4, M5], and the price is A$3.08, not A$2.54.
6. The last twelve months' underlying EBITDA of A$28.5m holds one very strong month: July to November 2025 earned A$11.5m [M15] and the half to December A$28.6m [M21], so December 2025 alone was about A$17.1m and October to November 2025 about A$0.5m. December was also strong a year earlier (about A$8.6m: A$31.0m for the half [M21] less A$22.4m for five months [M15]). The current run rate is lower than A$28.5m a year.
7. The check's open point on the notes is closed: they mature on 31 December 2026, convert at A$5.356 and pay 2.5% on US$27.95m (annual report note 16 [M1]). The amount is still unclear: note 16 says repayable as a fixed A$41.1m; the directors' report says the book value at maturity will equal the amount repayable, A$41.6m [M1]. I use A$41.6m.

## The company in plain words
Mayne Pharma sells branded prescription medicines in the United States, in two areas [M2, M3]:
- Women's health: NEXTSTELLIS (a contraceptive pill), ANNOVERA (a contraceptive ring), and BIJUVA and IMVEXXY (menopause treatments). The last three are licensed from TherapeuticsMD (TXMD) for royalties of 8% of their sales, plus 10% of ANNOVERA sales to the Population Council and US$40m milestones at US$400m and US$1bn of cumulative ANNOVERA sales [M1, note 17]. FY26 revenue A$174.3m (US$118.2m), direct contribution A$57.9m [M2].
- Dermatology and DistributeRx: older acne and rosacea brands (DORYX, RHOFADE, gACCUTANE, TWYNEO, EPSOLAY) and a cash-pay pharmacy channel through Adelaide Apothecary in Kentucky. FY26 revenue A$138.7m (US$94.1m), direct contribution A$44.5m; a generic RHOFADE arrived in July 2026 and Q1 FY27 contribution fell 61% [M2, M3].
- International (being sold): the Salisbury, South Australia plant, contract manufacturing, and Australian sales of NEXTSTELLIS. FY26 revenue A$70.7m, direct contribution A$4.6m [M2]. Sold to Evaris Pharma for A$55.5m on 6 November 2026, subject to the ACCC, completion expected January to March 2027 [M4].

Group FY26: revenue A$383.7m (FY25 A$408.1m), underlying EBITDA A$34.2m (A$47.0m) [M2]. After the sale, the company will be US only.

People: Chair Professor Bruce Robinson; CEO Aaron Gray; CFO Griffin Buchanan (from 1 August 2026); non-executive directors include Meghan Rivera (from June 2026) and Jacob Ma-Weaver (from 14 August 2026), whose fund Funicular holds 9.90% [M3].

Owners at 21 August 2026: top 20 hold 60.45%; substantial holders Funicular Funds 9.90%, Bruce Mathieson and related entities 6.51%, Rubric Capital 5.36%, Trium Capital 5.02% [M1].

Five years: a 20-for-1 share consolidation and special dividend in January 2023 [M1]. Closes A$6.15 at end 2023, A$4.97 at end 2024, A$3.11 at end 2025, A$3.08 now [D2, D1]; 52-week range A$2.04 to A$6.04 [D1]. The A$7.40 scheme was agreed on 20 February 2025, when the price was A$5.41 [D2, M8].

### What the 11 November deadline is for, and what each answer means
The deadline is 28 days after the 14 October judgment, the period for applying to the High Court for special leave to appeal [M6]. The High Court grants about one civil application in ten, and decides about 80% of applications on the papers without a hearing (Wikipedia's summary of the court's figures, a secondary source; the court's own annual report site refused automated reads [S3]). The chances below are the desk's.

### What the deal contract says about damages
- Cosette's FIRB duties (clause 3.3(a)): apply within three business days, take "all reasonable steps", and "not take any action or omit to take any action that would or would be reasonably likely to prevent or materially hinder the satisfaction of the Regulatory Approvals", each "to the extent it is reasonable to do so" [M8]. Cosette's likely answer: it had to tell the regulator its true intentions.
- The reverse break fee (clause 14.2): A$6,718,788.50, payable if Mayne terminates for Cosette's material breach [M8]. Mayne terminated on that ground [M14]. No filing says the fee has been demanded or paid [M1, M21].
- The cap (clause 14.6(a) to (c)): Cosette's total liability under or in connection with the deed is capped at the break fee, except "in respect of any fraud or wilful or intentional breach", where Mayne may recover damages "whether on its own benefit or on behalf of a person for whom Mayne holds rights on trust" [M8]. Clause 14.6(d) records that the break fee "may not be an adequate remedy for Mayne or Mayne's Shareholders" [M8].
- The trust (clause 20.4): "Mayne receives and holds the benefit of Cosette's obligations under this deed both for itself and as trustee for Mayne Shareholders from time to time, such that Mayne may recover damages against Cosette for any breach of this deed on its own behalf and on behalf of Mayne Shareholders from time to time." A Mayne Shareholder is a registered holder "from time to time" [M8].
- The cap binds Cosette and its related bodies corporate [M8]. Avista (a limited partnership) and Mr Burgstahler are sued in tort and for knowing involvement, outside the deed [M1]. Mallesons also notes a contractual release whose reach to them is unclear, and that the cap may not answer a misleading conduct claim [S1].

## Valuation

### The business alone, two methods
Common inputs (A$m): last twelve months' underlying EBITDA to September 2026 = 34.2 - 11.0 + 5.3 = 28.5 [M2, M3]. International's share = 4.6 - 1.47 + 1.4 = 4.5 (Q1 FY26 from "-5%" [M3]), so 24.0 without it; Q1 FY27 annualised without International = (5.3 - 1.4) x 4 = 15.6. Net financial liabilities after the sale and the notes: earn-outs 264.8 + notes 41.6 + leases 4.1 - cash 67.3 - sale 55.5 = 187.7. Shares 81.89m. Arithmetic in `python3 -I`.

Method 2: cash after royalties. This treats royalties as a running cost rather than a liability: EBITDA without International 24.0, less royalties about 13.0 (A$12.5m in FY26, A$3.5m a quarter now [M2, M3]), leases about 2.5 and upkeep capex about 1.0, leaves about A$7.5m a year (minus A$0.9m on the Q1 FY27 run rate). Add net cash after the notes and the sale (67.3 + 55.5 - 41.6 = 81.2) and take off the fixed amounts that are not royalties: the NEXTSTELLIS deferred payments (about A$85m to A$90m, worked from the A$11.4m of discount unwinding at 10.2% and the A$19.8m cut [M1]) and the PV of the US$40m Population Council milestone (A$10m to A$25m, my estimate; cumulative ANNOVERA sales aren't disclosed). With A$90m to A$110m of fixed amounts: 8x gives A$0.38 to A$0.62, 10x A$0.56 to A$0.81, 12x A$0.74 to A$0.99 a share. On the run rate it is about zero.

Both methods put the business alone at about **A$0.65 a share**, range about A$0.05 to A$1.25, and lower again if Q1 FY27 is the new normal. At A$3.08, the business alone would need about A$44m of EBITDA without International at 10 times, almost double the last twelve months. The main thing it depends on: whether women's health growth outruns the dermatology decline. The second: whether the A$264.8m of earn-outs, which rest on management's sales forecasts, is cut again as it was by A$125.6m in FY26.

A cost the business value doesn't show: running the claim. Cosette litigation cost A$2.4m in Q1 FY27 [M3], so about A$10m a year, paid by Mayne. If the claim fails, Mayne also pays the defendants' costs.

### The claim
The most it could be: the premium lost when the deal failed. On the A$3.19 close of 11 December 2025 that is A$4.21 a share, A$342m on the 81.25m shares the scheme covered; on A$2.40 (nearer the business value then) it is A$5.00, A$406m [D2, M8]. I use A$375m (A$4.58 on today's 81.89m shares), and assume interest to judgment roughly offsets the time until payment, which is generous.

Each step it needs, with the desk's chance:
1. A breach. The Panel found it "at least foreseeable" that the change of intentions put approval at risk, and the Treasurer's letter cited nothing but Salisbury [M9]. Mayne also alleged breaches of Cosette's funding obligations and debt financing warranties [M12]. Against: the duty is "to the extent it is reasonable", and Cosette will say it couldn't mislead the regulator. 70%.
2. "Wilful or intentional", to get past the A$6.7m cap. The FIRB letter was a deliberate act, sent six days after holders approved the scheme by a buyer already trying to leave [M9, C1]; the trial judge found Mr Burgstahler's revised evidence on a key meeting "inconceivable" (as reported by Mallesons [S1]). But "intentional breach" may need Cosette to have known it was breaching. 55%.
3. Causation: FIRB would have approved and Cosette would have paid, despite its debt lapsing on 24 November [M11]. 75%.
4. Recoverable: a court accepts that Mayne, as trustee, can recover holders' lost premium. Untested in Australia [S1]; in Delaware a target could not enforce a lost-premium clause without holders being third-party beneficiaries (Crispo v Musk, 2022, as summarised by Mallesons [S1]). 70%.
5. Collected from Cosette, a private group whose deal debt has lapsed, or from Avista. Cosette did pay A$14.4m of costs within a week of the orders [M18]. 85%.

Together: 0.70 x 0.55 x 0.75 x 0.70 x 0.85 = 17%, about 20% with the uncapped claims against Avista and Mr Burgstahler and under the misleading conduct law. Then, of a win, who gets it: I put 60% on a payment that today's holders share (for example a recovery paid to the company and returned to holders on a current record date) and 40% on it going to the holders who would have received A$7.40 in late 2025. Funicular bought 2.08m of its shares after the deal failed and held about 3.04m before [M23], and its manager is on the board that would decide this, so the question will be argued both ways.

Settlement is the likelier ending. The closest comparable I could check, Huntsman's 2008 claim after Hexion and its owner Apollo walked from their deal, settled in December 2008 for US$1bn (a US$325m break fee, US$425m of cash from Apollo affiliates and US$250m of convertible notes they bought), paid to the company, not its holders [S4]. Suing Avista and its CEO personally, alongside Cosette, adds pressure of a similar kind. A settlement worth the expected trial value (about 20% of A$375m, A$75m) to somewhat more is plausible; I use A$100m paid to the company.

Claim value to someone buying today: about **A$0.9 a share** (expected price A$1.58 below, less the A$0.65 business), range about A$0.3 to A$1.7 across the chance sets tested. Timing: a trial looks unlikely before late 2027, with appeals after that; the claim's resolve-by date is about 30 June 2029 (desk estimate, no court timetable available).

So no Australian holder has yet been paid lost-premium damages after a failed scheme, and there is no record of how long one would wait.

## Why the market is wrong
I think the market is too high, not too low, which is no use for a buy. The price implies the claim is worth A$2.43 a share (A$199m) on top of a A$0.65 business. That is more than half of the whole lost premium, before the cap, the wilful-breach test, the trust question, collection and three years of waiting. The two court wins were about Cosette's right to leave, not about damages, and the price moved little on the second one (A$2.54 to A$2.69 and back to A$2.49 the next day [D2]). The 13.7% jump on 6 November followed the sale, which adds about 13c a share on my figures; part of that jump may be holders reading the sale as making Mayne easier to buy, now that the plant FIRB objected to is going. That is a possibility, not a reason to buy.

## Outcomes and chances
On the claim's final outcome, resolve-by about 30 June 2029 (beyond the six months a non-value pick allows and beyond the 24 months a value pick allows, so this table can't go on a card):

| Outcome | What happens | Chance | Price by resolve date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bad | Nothing reaches today's holders: the claim fails, is held to the A$6.7m cap, can't be collected, or the holders' damages go to those who held in late 2025. The business alone. Unrecovered costs and adverse costs could take a further 10c to 30c off | 55% | A$0.65 (business alone, Method 1 at 10 times) |
| Middling | Settlement of about A$100m paid to the company in 2027 or 2028, less about A$20m of further net costs | 30% | A$1.63 (A$0.65 plus A$80m) |
| Good | Judgment or settlement near the full lost premium (A$375m), reaching today's holders, less about A$25m of net costs | 15% | A$4.92 (A$0.65 plus A$350m) |

Expected price: 0.55 x 0.65 + 0.30 x 1.63 + 0.15 x 4.92 = A$1.58, 49% below A$3.08 before costs.

Checks on the chances. With much kinder chances, 30% bad, 40% middling and 30% good, the expected price is A$2.32, still 25% below. Keeping middling at 30%, the price needs a 50% chance of the good outcome just to break even, before any time value or the minimum gain.

Round-trip costs at our size (2.5% of NZ$100,000, about A$2,016 at NZ$1 = A$0.806 on 6 November [D3]): half the spread each way, about 1.0% in all (Corwin and Schultz estimate over 21 and 60 sessions, 1.0%; the 6 November quote was 1.0% wide [D1, D2]); size cost 0.28% each way (daily volatility 3.5% over 20 sessions, times the square root of A$2,016 over the A$332,570 average day [D2]); brokerage A$15 each way (0.74%) unless the plan's monthly cover applies; 0.5% each way to convert NZ dollars. That is 1.6% to 4.1%; I use 2.5%.

**Expected gain after costs: about -50%**, against a minimum (cash rate 2.13% plus 5%, rulebook section 5a) of 18.8% over the 964 days to 30 June 2029, or 3.5% over six months.

Inside six months (to 7 May 2027): the only thing that would show the claim's value is a settlement. I put a settlement announced by then at about 8% (Cosette has fought every step, including an appeal it pursued after the deal had already ended [C1]), and any money in holders' hands by then at about 2%, because a recovery paid to the company would still have to be paid out by dividend or capital return (a capital return needs a holders' meeting), and a recovery held on trust would need the beneficiaries settled first. Even a settlement could disappoint: a A$100m settlement paid to the company is worth about A$1.63 a share with the business, and the price needs a settlement of about A$220m paid to the company to be worth A$3.08.

## What would make this wrong
The things that would make "no case" wrong:
1. A large settlement soon, paid to the company. Suing Avista and its CEO personally raises the pressure to settle, and the Huntsman case shows such a claim can end in a large payment to the company [S4]. A settlement of more than about A$220m paid to the company would put the value above A$3.08.
2. The business is worth more than I think. If the earn-out liability proves too high (it was cut A$125.6m in FY26 [M1]) and EBITDA without International gets back towards A$44m, the business alone could carry the price.
3. A bid for the US-only Mayne once the Salisbury plant has gone.

Reopen if any of these happens:
- A settlement or judgment is announced, with an amount, a payee (the company, or holders on a stated record date) and a date. Re-run the outcomes on those terms.
- The price falls to A$0.72 or below, where the business-alone bad case is within 10% and the value pick test could be met (a value pick also needs its resolve date within 24 months, which the claim may not meet).
- A trial date for the damages claim is set inside six months of the reopening date.
- The H1 FY27 result (about 23 February 2027) shows underlying EBITDA without International running at A$35m a year or more, which would lift the business alone to about A$2.0 a share at 10 times (Method 1) and bring the expected value near the price.

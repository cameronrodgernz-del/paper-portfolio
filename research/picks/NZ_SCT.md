# Scott Technology (NZX:SCT): deep dive. No case. Not a value pick: the bad case (NZ$1.75) is 26% below the price and the middling case (NZ$2.35) is 1% below it

Written 2026-11-08 by the research desk. Price at writing: NZ$2.37 (close on 6 November 2026, ex the 5c final dividend; FinFeedAPI, data/prices/NZX/SCT.csv, and NZX's own close, bid NZ$2.36 and offer NZ$2.45 [S21]). Market value: NZ$201.2m (84,892,648 shares, annual report note C1 and NZX [S1, S21]).
Status: finished. Verdict: no case. This is a simulation and not financial advice.
Fact sheet: research/facts/NZ_SCT.md, grade A on a single peer multiple (NZ$4.94 at Skellerup's 17.0 times FY27 EBIT). The question it set: can Scott turn its order book and the FY27 range (operating EBITDA NZ$36m to NZ$41m) into profit and cash at a steadier rate, so the market prices it as a growing industrial company rather than a lumpy project shop controlled by its largest customer?

Value pick (rulebook section 5a, from 8 Nov 2026): no. The bad case is 26% below the price (the limit is 10%) and the middling case is 1% below it (it needs to be 25% above). As an ordinary pick the dated event is the H1 FY27 result (about 15 April 2027), and the expected gain to 30 April 2027 is about -3% before costs and -7% to -9% after them, against a minimum of +3.3%.

Order of work. The brief and the fact sheet both quote the price, so it was in view from the start. "What the filings show" and the valuation use Scott's filings and the multiples the market pays named peers; Scott's own price enters only in the multiples quoted at the price, "Why the market is wrong", the outcomes and the checks. Corrections to the fact sheet, for the red team:
1. The fact sheet missed the most important fact on the register. On 15 June 2023, "after discussions with majority shareholder JBS", Scott began a strategic review of its ownership structure with Macquarie Capital. On 13 November 2023 it said it had approached "a group of potentially interested parties" about an offer to all shareholders, and "no offers were received at price ranges which reflected the independent directors' view of value" [S12]. The shares traded between NZ$3.05 and NZ$3.90 during the review (data/prices/NZX/SCT.csv). So a sale has been tried, buyers were sounded out, and none paid enough.
2. The cash earnings multiple of 21.6 times counts leases twice: it divides an enterprise value that includes NZ$28.8m of leases by cash earnings that are already after NZ$7.7m of lease payments and interest. On a consistent basis the multiple is 19.1 times before tax (NZ$208.9m over NZ$10.96m) and 23.4 times after the NZ$2.0m of tax paid.
3. "Met both ranges it gave, each at the top, both given late in the year" understates how late. The FY25 range came on 11 September 2025, after the year had ended on 31 August [S11]. The FY26 range came on 12 August 2026, 19 days before year end [S11]. The FY27 range of 22 October 2026 is the first range Scott has given at the start of a year, so there is no record to lean on.
4. The JBS stake did not creep up evenly. JBS took no shares under the dividend reinvestment plan for the November 2023 and May 2024 dividends, during and just after the review it had prompted; only 147,809 reinvestment shares were issued to anyone in FY24 [S6 note C1, S13]. It started reinvesting again in November 2024. JBS held 53.44% at 31 August 2025 [S1 note E3].
5. JBS's share of sales jumped because of one project. Sales to JBS companies were NZ$8.5m in FY22, NZ$21.9m in FY23, NZ$24.0m in FY24, NZ$36.0m in FY25 and NZ$33.6m in FY26 [S1, S5, S6, S7, S8]. The rise is largely the NZ$56m automated warehouse for JBS Canada at Brooks, Alberta (signed May 2022, the "special project" in the forward work figures), which went ahead without a shareholder vote under an NZ RegCo waiver of the related party rule [S15]. The FY25 annual report put JBS at 14% of FY25 sales; the FY26 report restates it as 13.1% (NZ$36.0m of NZ$275.3m) [S1, S5].
6. Where the term debt falls due: the FY26 annual report contradicts itself. Note C3 puts NZ$9,997k of the term loans in "three to five years"; the liquidity table in section D puts NZ$9,983k in "5+ years" [S1]. Either way nothing falls due in the window. The facility's expiry date and its covenant tests are not disclosed.
7. The fact sheet's anchor EBIT of NZ$26.8m takes FY26 depreciation (NZ$11.7m) off the FY27 midpoint. Depreciation has been NZ$10.7m to NZ$11.7m since FY24 and Scott says it will spend more on innovation and systems; at NZ$12.0m the anchor is NZ$4.88, not NZ$4.94. This matters less than the fact that no buyer has shown it would pay anything like it (Valuation).

> In short: the case falls at the first test. Scott's cash conversion is weak over five years and only peer-like in the last two. From FY22 to FY26 operating EBITDA totalled NZ$152.2m, but after capital spending, development spending and lease payments the business produced NZ$22.6m of cash before tax (15%), and NZ$8.5m for shareholders after tax and interest, while it paid NZ$16.9m of dividends in cash and NZ$15.7m in new shares. Project working capital went from 13.3% of revenue to 25.8%, in both the February and August balance sheets, and the drivers (slower-paying overseas customers, Chinese retentions, more stock for standard products) look mostly structural. The FY27 range needs revenue of 2.1 to 2.3 times the forward work Scott started the year with; in FY25 and FY26 revenue was 1.7 times it, which points to about NZ$250m to NZ$270m, not NZ$310m to NZ$340m. Against six listed food-equipment and automation peers (JBT Marel, Middleby, GEA, Krones, ATS, Interroll; median 16.0 times free cash flow and 15.2 times EBIT), Scott at NZ$2.37 is cheap on EBIT (9.7 times) but not on cash: 14.1 times FY26 free cash flow, 27.8 times its five-year average. Valued on cash it is worth about NZ$2.00 to NZ$2.90, central NZ$2.35, which is the price. The NZ$4.94 anchor needs a buyer to pay about 27 times FY26 free cash flow; in 2023 buyers approached for the whole company would not pay what the independent directors wanted when the shares were at NZ$3.05 to NZ$3.90. JBS (54.06%) can creep 5 points a year, bid, or run a scheme, but nothing says it is moving. No card.

## The company in plain words
Scott designs, builds and services automated production lines and robots. It reports four domains [S1, S3]: materials handling and logistics (palletising, conveyors, automated guided vehicles; NZ$129.9m of FY26 revenue), protein (lamb and beef boning rooms, poultry trussing and the BladeStop safety bandsaw; NZ$67.3m), mining (Rocklabs crushers, pulverisers and automated sample preparation for mining labs; NZ$62.3m) and appliances (production lines for whiteware makers such as GE Appliances and Midea; NZ$34.7m). In FY26 NZ$199m of revenue came from new equipment and NZ$95m (32.3%) from "recurring" work: service, parts, software and upgrades [S3]. It builds in New Zealand, Australia, China, Belgium, the Czech Republic and the United States, and earns most of its revenue offshore.

Who runs it: Mike Christman has been chief executive since mid-2024, after John Kippenberger resigned in March 2024 (the CFO resigned three days later) [S22]. The CFO who joined in July 2023, Mark O'Malley, resigned in May 2026; two interim co-CFOs have run finance since 6 July 2026 [S22]. The board has three independent directors including the chair, Stuart McLauchlan, and three JBS directors: Alan Byers, Brent Eastwood and, since 27 August 2026, Jerson Nascimento Jr., JBS's global chief procurement and innovation officer, representing JBS USA [S1, S14].

Who owns it, at 31 August 2026 [S1, pages 95 and 96]: JBS Australia 45,894,965 shares (54.06%); Oakwood Securities 5,855,175 (6.90%); New Zealand Central Securities Depository 4,796,103 (5.65%, a custodian); Leveraged Equities Finance 3.03%; then custodians and private holders, none above 2%. The top 20 hold about 81.9%. There are 2,463 holders; 29 holders of more than 100,000 shares own 83.2%.

## Why big investors may be overlooking it
- Size and float: NZ$201m market value, of which JBS holds 54%, so the free float is about NZ$92m, and Oakwood holds another 6.9% [S1].
- Liquidity: a median NZ$15,443 a day over 250 sessions and NZ$22,603 over 60 (NZX's data [S21]); NZ$45,645 over the last 20, inflated by selling after the result.
- Not one of the 20 largest NZX companies (config/nzx_top20.csv). I didn't check index membership or broker coverage.
- A messy recent history: a failed ownership review in 2023, the chief executive and CFO leaving in 2024, another CFO leaving in 2026, a fatal accident at the Dunedin site in April 2025 with WorkSafe proceedings to come [S12, S22].
- Lumpy, second-half-weighted project earnings and a controlling holder who is also a top customer.
So it is overlooked in the usual small-cap ways. But the price has also just been tested by heavier trading: it rose to NZ$2.90 on results day (22 October) and fell 18% to NZ$2.37 over the next two weeks on above-normal volume (5c of that was the dividend) [S21].

Is it timing or structural? Some of each, but mostly structural:
- Timing that has already netted out: the JBS Canada warehouse paid deposits up front. Contract liabilities rose NZ$19.1m in FY23 and fell NZ$15.7m in FY24, with creditors falling NZ$9.6m; the company said FY24's operating cash of NZ$6.0m reflected projects that "had received deposits in advance" [S6, S6b]. FY23 and FY24 together still produced only NZ$10.3m of peer-basis free cash flow against NZ$40.5m of EBIT.
- The half-year balance sheets rise too, so it isn't an August quirk. Net working capital was NZ$56.3m at February 2024, NZ$52.6m at February 2025 and NZ$65.4m at February 2026, 20.0%, 20.5% and 23.2% of the previous twelve months' revenue [S4, S10].
- Debtors are slower. Trade debtors were 49 days of revenue in FY21 and 75 days in FY26. Credit terms on large contracts run 30 to 120 days [S1 note B1]. Debtors more than 91 days overdue were NZ$9.1m at August 2026 against NZ$5.0m a year earlier: Americas NZ$4.2m (from NZ$0.7m), China NZ$2.1m (retentions), Australia NZ$1.4m, Rocklabs NZ$1.1m [S1, provision matrix]. In FY25 the company said the August debtor peak "normalised in September 2025" [S5c]; it fell to NZ$45.4m by February 2026 and was back at NZ$60.1m in August 2026 [S4]. JBS's own unpaid balance was NZ$12.7m at August 2025 (NZ$2.2m a year earlier), about half of that year's debtor rise [S5 note E3].
- Unbilled work grew in China and Europe. Contract assets were NZ$14.6m in China (NZ$10.0m a year earlier) and NZ$15.2m in Europe (NZ$12.5m) [S1, provision matrix].
- More stock, by design: the strategy sells more standard products (Rocklabs equipment, BladeStop) and pre-builds equipment; the company said stock was "elevated as at August 2026 due to stock held for orders expected in early FY27" [S3].
- Sundry debtors rose from NZ$5.9m to NZ$15.0m in FY26 (an NZ$8.0m cash outflow) and the accounts don't say what they are [S1].
- Spending that EBITDA leaves out is permanent: equipment, development and lease payments took NZ$14.6m in FY26 (5.0% of revenue) and NZ$12.9m a year on average.

The best two years (FY25 and FY26) converted 77% of EBIT into peer-basis free cash flow, which is what the peers convert (Valuation). The five-year figure is 42%. A growing Scott needs about 25 cents of working capital for each extra dollar of revenue at today's ratio, so the company's own plan (NZ$530m of revenue by FY30) would absorb about NZ$60m of cash on that ratio. My reading: conversion near the FY25 and FY26 level is possible only if the working capital ratio stops rising, and nothing in the filings yet shows that it has.

### JBS: the stake, the deal it came from, the customer and the rules
How JBS got to 54.06%. In 2015 Scott and JBS ran a scheme of arrangement at NZ$1.39 a share (the three-month average price to 1 June 2015): a takeover offer by JBS for all shares, a 1-for-8 rights offer to New Zealand and Australian holders, and a placement of 10,000,000 new shares to JBS for NZ$13.9m, with top-up shares so JBS reached at least 50.1%. The independent adviser, Northington Partners, valued Scott at NZ$1.08 to NZ$1.26 a share and called NZ$1.39 "fully priced". Holders approved it on 26 November 2015, the Panel gave its no-objection statement on 2 December 2015 and the High Court made final orders on 6 April 2016 [S17]. The Overseas Investment Office consented in 2016 [S16]. JBS disclosed 37,415,058 shares (50.100%) on 14 April 2016 [S13, the 2018 notice's "last disclosure"]. Every share since has come from the dividend reinvestment plan, 8,479,907 shares in 14 issues at prices from NZ$3.6416 (November 2017) down to NZ$1.7356 (May 2025), NZ$22.6m in all, an average of NZ$2.67 [S13]. JBS's whole stake cost about NZ$74.6m, NZ$1.63 a share; at NZ$2.37 it is worth NZ$108.8m. JBS has never bought on market. The 2023 review and the pause in reinvestment are in correction 1 and 4 above.

The regulator. In October 2019 the Overseas Investment Office settled its investigation into JBS Australia after the Batista brothers admitted bribery in Brazil. It found they were no longer of good character, required that at least half of Scott's board (excluding the managing director) be independent, and said it "may take stricter measures if Joesley or Wesley Batista assume any control over JBS Australia in the future. This could include asking JBS Australia to sell its shares in Scott Technology" [S16]. The Batistas were proposed for election to the JBS S.A. board at its April 2024 meeting [S20] (reported elected; Food Safety News, secondary). In May 2025 LINZ extended an exemption for JBS's New York listing restructure, listing Scott as an indirectly held New Zealand asset and finding "no meaningful change to the ownership and control" [S19]. No forced-sale step has followed. Scott's board has three independent directors and three JBS directors, which meets the half-independent condition; one JBS director, Alan Byers, is described in the notice of meeting as an adviser to Wesley Batista [S14].

The commercial relationship. Sales to JBS companies: NZ$6.9m (FY21), NZ$8.5m (FY22, mostly BladeStop machines), NZ$21.9m, NZ$24.0m, NZ$36.0m and NZ$33.6m (FY26, 11.4% of revenue) [S1, S5, S6, S7, S8]. Scott bought NZ$1.6m from JBS companies in FY26, and JBS owed Scott NZ$6.6m at August 2026 [S1 note E3]. Terms: "based on price lists in force and terms that would be available to third parties" [S1]. The NZ$56m JBS Canada warehouse was certified by the non-JBS directors as negotiated at arm's length, with the JBS directors excluded from the decision, as conditions of NZ RegCo's waiver [S15]. JBS directors take no fees [S1]. Recent JBS work includes a lamb boning room for JBS in Australia (October 2024) and "the recent JBS US Poultry order" (August 2026) [S6b, S11]. With the Canadian project nearly finished (NZ$3m left in forward work), FY27 JBS sales depend on new orders.

The August 2026 board change. John Berry of JBS Australia (on the board since 2017, first as an alternate) stepped down to be alternate for Jerson Nascimento Jr., JBS's global chief procurement and innovation officer, appointed 27 August 2026 to represent JBS USA [S14]. He stands for election at the annual meeting on 26 November 2026. The announcement ties it to growth ("deep understanding of the global protein industry and the investment priorities of some of the world's largest processors"). It moves the seat from the Australian subsidiary to the group's procurement and innovation function. That fits JBS treating Scott as a supplier of automation; it is not a sign of a bid.

What the Takeovers Code lets a holder above 50% do, from the Takeovers Panel's own guidance [S18]:
- Creep: a holder of more than 50% but less than 90% may increase its holding "so long as they do not buy more than 5% of the company's shares over any 12-month period". The Panel's note measures the 5 points from the lowest holding in the 12 months to the increase, "regardless of how that increase in control percentage came about", so reinvestment shares count toward it. JBS has added about 1 point a year; its lowest holding in the last 12 months was 53.44% (before the November 2025 issue), so it could go to about 58.4% without an offer, about 3.7m shares or NZ$9m at NZ$2.37. (A separate class exemption covers reinvestment allotments only if the extra control is removed within six months, which JBS has not done, so JBS relies on creep.)
- A full offer to all holders, or a partial offer, under the Code, with an independent adviser's report.
- Compulsory acquisition: a holder that reaches 90% "can (or must) buy all of the remaining shares". After a takeover offer the price is usually the offer price; otherwise it must be "a 'fair and reasonable' cash price" certified by a Panel-approved expert, and holders can object.
- A scheme of arrangement, approved by 75% of the votes in each interest class and a simple majority of all votes, then the High Court, with the Panel deciding whether to issue a no-objection statement. This is how JBS got control in 2016.
- An allotment or purchase approved by an ordinary resolution of the other holders.
JBS can afford any of these: the 45.94% it doesn't own is worth NZ$92.4m at NZ$2.37. Nothing suggests it is moving: no purchases, no notice, no review, a seat reshuffle tied to the protein strategy. And the only evidence on its intentions, the 2023 review, showed JBS exploring a sale, not a buyout. So I leave out the players-and-rules section.

### Debt
The main lender is ANZ Bank New Zealand: a facility for borrowings and working capital of NZ$46m (unchanged from 2025; NZ$46.7m in 2024, NZ$31.7m in 2023), of which NZ$26.1m was unused at 31 August 2026. It is secured by general security agreements over Scott and certain subsidiaries and by mortgages over 630 Kaikorai Valley Road, Dunedin, and 10 Maces Road, Christchurch [S1 note C3, S6]. Drawn: term loans of NZ$13.8m (NZ$8.0m in New Zealand dollars, the rest in US dollars, euros and koruna, some through KBC Bank in Belgium) and an overdraft of NZ$8.9m on a NZ$35m line. Interest rates on term loans ran from 0.99% to 5.95%. In FY26 Scott drew NZ$13.1m and repaid NZ$13.7m, and the non-current term debt moved from "one to two years" (NZ$11.9m in 2025) to three years or more, so the term loans were rolled forward during the year [S1 notes C3 and F1]. The expiry date and the covenant tests are not disclosed; the accounts say only that the group "fully complied with, and operated within, the debt facility financial covenants". Net bank debt of NZ$7.7m is 0.2 times operating EBITDA. Leases (NZ$28.8m) are mostly buildings. Payment guarantees and performance bonds of NZ$10.4m and penalty-clause exposure of NZ$7.6m sit off the balance sheet [S1 note F2]. Debt is not a risk to the case inside the window.

## Valuation
Two methods on cash earnings, with an EBIT multiple as a cross-check, because the market prices equipment makers on cash and EBIT and Scott's EBITDA leaves out NZ$14.6m a year of real spending.

### Method 1: discounted cash flow on cash earnings
Cash flow to the whole business after lease payments, so only bank net debt (NZ$7.7m) comes off at the end. Inputs, all from FY26 unless stated [S1]: lease payments and interest 2.63% of revenue (NZ$7.75m); capital and development spending 2.5% (five-year average 2.7%); depreciation of owned assets 1.62% (NZ$4.76m: NZ$11.67m less NZ$6.91m of lease depreciation); tax 25% of EBIT after lease costs (FY26 effective rate 21%); working capital 25% of each year's revenue growth (FY26 ratio 25.8%); five years, then 2.5% growth; discount rate 10.5% (a New Zealand small company with almost no debt).

| Case | FY27 revenue, EBITDA | Growth after | EBITDA margin from FY29 | Working capital per extra dollar | Discount rate | Value a share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bad | NZ$285m, NZ$32.0m | 3% | 11.5% | 30c | 11% | NZ$1.45 |
| Central | NZ$310m, NZ$36.5m | 6% | 12.0% | 25c | 10.5% | NZ$1.99 |
| Good | NZ$325m, NZ$38.5m | 10% | 13.0% | 15c | 10% | NZ$3.14 |

Central arithmetic (NZ$m): FY27 free cash flow = 36.5 - 8.2 leases - 7.8 capital spending - 5.8 tax - 4.0 working capital = 10.8; then 11.3, 12.3, 13.0, 13.8 to FY31; present value 45.2. Terminal cash flow 17.3 / (10.5% - 2.5%) = 216.8, present value 131.6. Enterprise value 176.7, less 7.7 = 169.0, / 84.89m shares = NZ$1.99. Sensitivities (sct/work/dcf.py and rev.py): discount rate 9.5% to 11.5% and working capital 15c to 30c give NZ$1.67 to NZ$2.50; a 21% tax rate gives NZ$2.15; capital spending at the five-year 2.7% gives NZ$1.89. The company's own path (growth to about NZ$530m by FY30 at a 14% margin) gives NZ$3.31. The input that moves the answer most is the margin: each point of EBITDA margin is worth about NZ$0.35 a share.

Example: 16.0 x 17.5 = 280.3, less 36.5 = 243.8, / 84.89 = NZ$2.87. The peers are 10 to more than 100 times Scott's size, widely held and liquid; Scott is controlled, thinly traded and lumpier, which is why I also show 12.5 times.

### Cross-check: EV/EBIT
FY26 operating EBIT NZ$24.4m. At the peer median of 15.2 times: NZ$371m, less 36.5 = NZ$3.94. At 10 to 11.4 times (a quarter to a third off for size, control and liquidity): NZ$2.45 to NZ$2.85. Adjusted for conversion (peer EV/EBIT times Scott's conversion relative to the peers): at FY25 and FY26 conversion (77%, the peer level) NZ$2.96; at five-year conversion (42%) NZ$1.41. EBIT flatters Scott for exactly the reason the brief raised.

### Testing the fact sheet's anchor
NZ$4.94 (NZ$4.88 with FY27 depreciation) is 17.0 times FY27 midpoint EBIT. That is an enterprise value of about NZ$450m: 12.5 times FY26 operating EBITDA, 27 times FY26 free cash flow, 53 times the five-year average. Of the seven peers only JBT Marel, GEA (two of the world's largest food equipment makers) and XRF trade above 22 times free cash flow. Would a buyer pay it? In 2023 Macquarie took Scott to potential buyers for an offer to all holders, and none offered a price the independent directors would accept while the shares traded at NZ$3.05 to NZ$3.90, 12.1 to 15.3 times FY23 EBIT [S12]. And the mechanism isn't there: inside six months the only result is the seasonally weak first half, which carried 36% of FY26 EBITDA. NZ$4.94 is not even the good case.

### The range
- Bad: NZ$1.45 (DCF bad) to NZ$1.75 (where the market priced Scott after its last weak half: NZ$1.75 to NZ$2.02 in the month after the 16 April 2025 result, with FY25 EBITDA then tracking about NZ$30m).
- Central: NZ$2.00 to NZ$2.90. NZ$1.99 (DCF central), NZ$2.15 (12.5 times FY25 and FY26 free cash flow), NZ$2.45 to NZ$2.85 (discounted EBIT multiple); midpoint about NZ$2.35. Which end is right turns on whether FY25 and FY26 conversion lasts.
- Good: NZ$2.90 to NZ$3.15 (peer free cash flow multiple on FY25 and FY26, conversion-adjusted EBIT, DCF good).
- Only a bid would go higher, and the 2023 review says buyers wouldn't.

## Why the market is wrong
It isn't, on this evidence. At NZ$2.37 the price sits at the midpoint of the central range. A reverse DCF (sct/work/rev.py) shows it already assumes improvement: at a 10.5% cost of capital it needs either a 13% EBITDA margin with 6% growth (FY26: 12.3%) or 10% growth with working capital at 15c per extra dollar (FY26 ratio: 25.8c). On EBIT Scott looks cheap against peers (9.7 times against 15.2), which is what produced the fact sheet's gap, but on the cash the business actually produces it is priced like them (14.1 times FY26 free cash flow against 16.0, 27.8 times its five-year average). The 18% fall after the FY26 result looks like the market reading the order book against the FY27 range the same way.

## What would make this wrong
1. FY25 and FY26 conversion is the new normal. Those two years converted 77% of EBIT into free cash flow, the same as the peers. If the first half of FY27 shows working capital falling (sundry debtors and contract assets coming back, the overdue Americas debtors paid), the peer cash multiple (16 times) applies to NZ$17m to NZ$20m of free cash flow, worth NZ$2.77 to NZ$3.34, 17% to 41% above the price.
2. The second half of FY26 is the new run rate. Revenue of NZ$166m and operating EBITDA of NZ$23.1m in six months, if repeated with orders stepping up 20%, would put FY27 EBITDA near NZ$41m and the DCF at NZ$2.41 to NZ$3.28, depending on how much working capital the growth takes (25c down to 15c a dollar).
3. A JBS buyout. JBS could buy the 46% it doesn't own for under NZ$100m. Nothing points to it, and the 2023 review pointed the other way.
Exit rule: not applicable (no position).

Reopen conditions:
- As a value pick: a price at or below NZ$1.60 with the FY27 range intact (then the NZ$1.45 bad case is within 10% and the NZ$2.35 middling case is more than 25% above).

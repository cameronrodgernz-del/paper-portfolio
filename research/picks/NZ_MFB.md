## The company in plain words
My Food Bag sells weekly meal kits and ready-made meals delivered to homes across New Zealand under three brands: My Food Bag (the premium brand, with Nadia Lim), Bargain Box (value) and Fresh Start (health), plus the My Food Bag Shop for one-off gifts and meals and a small corporate channel [S3][S5]. It has one operating segment and one country [S3, note 2]. Customers pay up to a week before delivery and suppliers are paid weekly or monthly, so the business runs with negative working capital and uses its customers' cash [S3, "Working Capital"]. In FY26 (year to 31 March 2026) it made 1.273m deliveries at an average order of NZ$133.70, to 57.1k active customers (customers who took a delivery in the last 13 weeks), with a gross margin on ingredients of 49.0% and marketing at 3.2% of revenue [S4].

Who runs it: Mark Winter, chief executive; Louise Newsome, chief financial officer, appointed in 2024 (her initial disclosure notice is in the 2024 announcement list) [S3][S19]. The board is Tony Carter (chair, retiring on 19 November 2026), Mark Powell (chair from that day), Jen Bunbury, Sarah Hindle and co-founder Cecilia Robinson [S8][S9].

Who owns it (13 April 2026, largest registered holders [S3, p. 85]): Long Term Food Group LP 16.68% (managed by Waterman Capital (Fund 3), the private equity firm that invested in 2016 and took the company to the NZX on 5 March 2021 [S16][S14][S20]); James and Cecilia Robinson with Heimsath Alexander Trustee (the APL Holdings Trust) 7.53%; New Zealand Depository Nominee 7.02%; FNZ Custodians 5.83%; Peter Garvan 4.95%; ACC 4.78%; Custodial Services 2.90%; Theresa Gattung and Philippa Greenwood 2.70%; Forsyth Barr Custodians 2.24%; Masfen Securities 1.98%; Mark Winter 0.66%. The top 20 held 65.9%. Substantial holders: Long Term Food Group LP and Waterman 17.18% (notice of 19 June 2025) and the APL Holdings Trust 7.79% (21 December 2023) [S3][S16]. On today's 254.5m shares those are 16.5% and 7.5%.

## Why big investors may be overlooking it
- Size and liquidity: a market value of NZ$81.5m and about NZ$30,000 traded a day [S1]. It is not one of the 20 largest NZX companies (config/nzx_top20.csv) and, at that size, sits well below the smallest NZX 50 members (my inference; not checked against the index list).
- A concentrated register: the top 20 hold 65.9%, and a private equity fund holds 16.5% [S3].
- A messy history: profit fell from NZ$20.0m to NZ$6.0m in two years, and in FY23 the board paid NZ$17.1m of dividends while free cash flow was negative, borrowed to do it, then suspended the dividend for a year [S13][S12].
- A category out of favour: meal kits have shrunk since the pandemic. A secondary data site puts HelloFresh, the largest listed meal kit company, at about 1 times EBITDA (multiples.vc [S23]; not checked and not relied on).

It isn't unknown, though. The company sets its staff share awards using "external analyst forecasts" and EY's audit tested the impairment model against "broker forecasts" [S3, note 16 and audit report], so brokers cover it. And the six-month sale process put the company in front of buyers in New Zealand and overseas [S7].

## What the filings show

### The level of profit
The FY24 to FY26 profit of NZ$6.0m, NZ$6.35m and NZ$6.69m is the business as it now is:
- Revenue has grown again: flat in FY25, +5.0% in FY26 (second half +6.2%), +5.1% in the first seven weeks of FY27 and +4.5% in the first four months [S5][S9]. Growth came from order frequency (5.5 a year against 5.4) and order value (+2.8%), not from customers, who were 57.1k against 57.0k [S4]. At 30 September 2025 they were 61.3k, against 60.1k a year earlier; numbers peak in winter [S10].
- Margins are steady: gross margin 48.4%, 48.5%, 49.3% and 49.0% in FY23 to FY26, and contribution margin (after assembly and delivery) 20.6%, 21.3% and 21.2% in FY24 to FY26 [S4]. The chair said in August that the first-half gross margin should be in line with last year's 48.5% after price rises to cover ingredients and fuel [S9][S10].
- FY22 was a different business: NZ$194m of revenue, 61.7k customers in its last quarter and an EBITDA margin of 17.5% [S14]. In FY23 the company spoke of "subdued demand, driving diseconomies of scale" and reset its leadership, supply chain and brands [S13].
- The FY26 accounts reclassified NZ$3.2m of production depreciation into cost of sales for FY25, which changes margins between lines but not profit [S3, note 3].

### Cash earnings
- FY26 operating cash flow NZ$13.5m, capital spending NZ$2.9m (NZ$0.46m equipment, NZ$2.41m software) and lease principal NZ$2.1m: free cash flow NZ$8.5m, the company's figure [S3][S4]. Working capital made little difference (-NZ$0.3m net), although year-end timing raised receivables by NZ$3.1m and prepaid revenue by NZ$2.6m [S3, notes 5 and 18].
- Free cash flow is above profit because capital spending is below depreciation. Equipment depreciation was NZ$1.67m against NZ$0.46m bought; software amortisation NZ$2.35m against NZ$2.41m built [S3, notes 9 and 10]. Capital spending averaged NZ$4.1m a year over FY22 to FY26, including NZ$5.2m of automated picking equipment in FY23 [S13]. I use NZ$4.0m a year as the level that keeps the plant going.
- The company's pre-lease EBITDA (EBITDA less the cash paid on leases) was NZ$13.7m in FY26 and NZ$13.2m in FY25 [S4]. That is the cash earnings line a buyer would use.
- Interest: finance costs of NZ$1.10m in FY26 included NZ$0.50m on leases; bank interest after NZ$0.09m of interest income was about NZ$0.51m [S3, notes 1 and 11]. With debt repaid, most of that goes in FY27.

## Valuation
Worked from the filings. I saw the fact sheet's 32c and NZX's traded values while measuring liquidity, as the brief required; no input below comes from the price.

Inputs (python3 -I, NZ$m unless stated):
- FY27 underlying profit after tax: FY26's 6.686 grown 4.5% with margins flat, plus 0.30 of bank interest saved after tax ((0.51 - 0.10) x 0.72) = 7.28. I use a central 7.1 to allow for the fuel and ingredient costs flagged in May, with a range of 6.6 to 7.6. Review costs are left out as a one-off.
- FY27 pre-lease EBITDA: 13.7 x 1.045 = 14.3.
- Owner cash flow FY27: 14.3 less capital spending of 4.0, tax of 0.28 x (14.3 - 4.0 - 0.1) = 2.86 and interest of 0.1 = 7.36.
- Net cash at 31 March 2027: about 1.0 (net debt 1.9 at March 2026, plus the 2.9 final dividend and the interim of about 2.3, less about 8.5 of free cash flow and less review costs).
- Shares: 262.0m diluted (254.5m plus 60% of 12.36m rights); 266.9m in a sale.

### Method 1: price to earnings, the measure the NZX uses for small consumer companies
Trailing price to earnings ratios on NZX's own instrument data, 6 November 2026 [S2]: Briscoe 16.5, Turners 15.9, Hallenstein Glasson 14.4, Green Cross Health 13.4, Foley 13.4, Delegat 11.4, Scales 11.2, PGG Wrightson 10.6, Sanford 8.6, Comvita 8.0, Seeka 5.7. Median 11.4. My Food Bag has stronger cash conversion than most of these and no debt, but four years of flat customers, a shrinking category and a 70% profit fall in its recent past. I use 11 times, with 9.5 to 12.5.
- Central: 11.0 x 7.1 + 1.0 = 79.1 = 30.2c.
- Low: 9.5 x 6.6 + 1.0 = 63.7 = 24.3c. High: 12.5 x 7.6 + 1.0 = 96.0 = 36.6c.
What it depends on: whether FY27 profit holds at about NZ$7m, and the multiple.

### Method 2: discounted owner cash flow
Owner cash flow of 7.36 in FY27, growing 4% a year to FY31 (revenue growth of about 4%, margins flat), then 2% a year; discounted at 11.7%, the board's own post-tax rate in its impairment test [S3, note 10]; plus net cash of 1.0.
- Central: NZ$81.7m, 31.2c (11.4 times FY27 profit, 5.6 times pre-lease EBITDA).
- Discount rate 11% gives 33.6c, 12.5% gives 28.8c; growth of 2% gives 29.3c, 6% gives 33.2c.
- Low (owner cash flow 6.86, 2.5% growth, 1.5% after, 12.5%): 24.9c. High (7.76, 5%, 2.5%, 11%): 38.1c.
What it depends on: the discount rate first, then whether capital spending stays near NZ$4m.

### Method 3: what a buyer with control would pay
Pre-lease EBITDA of 14.3 at 5 to 7 times, plus net cash, over 266.9m shares (rights vesting in a sale): 5 times 27.2c, 6 times 32.6c, 7 times 37.9c. I have no recent New Zealand meal kit deal to anchor the multiple. The failed sale is the evidence (see the players section): with a motivated seller holding 16.5% and an adviser canvassing buyers at home and abroad, nobody offered a price the board would take, so buyers' value is probably about 6 times or less.

### The board's own plan
The impairment test assumes revenue growth of 6.1% a year from FY28 and an EBITDA margin of 12.0% from FY32 (9.6% in FY26), discounted at 11.7% with 2% growth after [S3, note 10]. Following that path (margin rising half a point a year, leases about 1.5% of revenue, capital spending equal to depreciation at 2.4% of revenue), owner cash flow goes 7.3, 8.4, 9.7, 11.0, 12.5 and 13.9 from FY27 to FY32 and the shares are worth about 45c. Profit passes the fact sheet's NZ$9.4m in about FY29. This is the board's forecast for an accounting test, not a value the market will pay inside six months.

### Range
NZ$0.25 to NZ$0.37 a share, central NZ$0.305 (Methods 1 and 2, central 30.2c and 31.2c). A buyer's price of about 32.6c sits just above it. The board's plan gives about 45c.

### The fact sheet's anchors, tested
- "If it goes our way", 40.1c (FY22 to FY26 average profit of NZ$9.39m at 11.1 times, less net debt, on 254.5m shares): it needs FY29-level profit priced today, undiscounted and without dilution. No mechanism gets the price there inside six months. Earnings the market can see won't: the first half is guided to an in-line margin. A capital return won't: it takes value out of the share. A bid hasn't: the sale just failed. It is a good-case value at most.
- "If it goes against us", 25.4c (FY24's NZ$6.0m at 11.1 times): close to my low of 24.3c to 24.9c. That is a fair bad case.

### What a holder can actually expect to receive
Over the 13 months to the end of 2027 (python3 -I):
- FY27 interim, about mid-December 2026: 70% to 90% of first-half profit of about NZ$2.75m to NZ$3.1m (after review costs) is 0.76c to 1.10c; I use 0.9c.
- FY27 final, June 2027: 70% to 90% of second-half profit of about NZ$3.9m to NZ$4.2m is 1.07c to 1.49c; I use 1.26c.
- FY28 interim, December 2027: about 1.0c.
- A capital return: the board has said only that it is "evaluating" one [S7]. Free cash after ordinary dividends adds only NZ$1m to NZ$3m a year, so a return of any size is borrowed. Net debt of 0.5 to 1.0 times pre-lease EBITDA is NZ$7m to NZ$14m, 2.8c to 5.6c a share; the current facility allows about 3.9c. I put a 55% chance on a return being announced in 2027, central NZ$8m to NZ$10m, about 3.5c, most likely with the FY27 result in late May or with the refinancing before October 2027.
- Expected total: 0.9 + 1.26 + 1.0 + 0.55 x 3.5 = 5.1c, 16% of the price.

But a capital return takes the same amount out of the value of each share. Borrowing NZ$10m saves about NZ$0.13m a year of tax at BKBM plus 1.70%, worth about half a cent a share. The rest is a transfer. The ordinary dividend of about 2.2c a year (7.0% at 32c, before imputation credits) is what the business earns. The FY23 record also argues for caution: the board paid NZ$17.1m out of borrowings that year, took net debt to NZ$15.3m and then had to stop the dividend [S13]. The same chair is leaving, and the new chair will decide the size.

### What the failed sale tells us
- The review started when the shares were 24c (close on 24 April 2026; 23.5c to 24c in the week before), with the board saying the price didn't reflect performance [S6]. The price was 28c two days later, 30.5c at the end of May after the FY26 result, 29c at the end of September and 32c at the end of October (data/prices/NZX/MFB.csv). Over the same period the iShares MSCI New Zealand fund rose about 3% in US dollars, 4% with its distribution (data/prices/US/ENZL.csv, 24 April to 5 November 2026).
- On 4 November, when the review ended, the shares closed at 31.5c on 832,768 shares on market plus 486,435 off market at about 32.5c (NZ$426,375 in all, 14 times a median day), and at 32c on 6 November [S1]. The market wasn't paying for a bid.
- A structured process, run by an adviser in New Zealand and overseas, with a largest holder whose fund is past its usual life, and a board that started the review because it thought the price too low, found no buyer it would recommend. A control premium of 30% to 40% on the undisturbed 24c is 31c to 34c, about today's price. So no buyer offered materially more than roughly where the shares now trade, or none offered a price the board and the main holders would accept. Either way, the price already sits at about what an outside owner with control would pay, and holders without control should not expect more.
- Limits: deals fail for reasons other than price (the founders' or Waterman's terms, financing, due diligence). The release gives none. The repo's history test of shares left behind after failed ASX deals (rulebook 9d) found no edge either.

## Why the market is wrong
I can't say it is. At 32c the market pays 11.5 times my FY27 underlying profit (the NZX consumer peer median is 11.4 times trailing), 5.7 times pre-lease EBITDA (about what a buyer would pay), and gets a 7% cash yield from a policy the company already had. The price has risen a third since the review began, much faster than the market, and didn't fall when the sale failed. My "nothing happens" value of 30.5c is below the price. The gap the fact sheet saw rests on the lockdown year and on a capital return that adds cash to holders' pockets but not value to the company.

## What would make this wrong
1. The margin story is real. The board's impairment test has the EBITDA margin rising from 9.6% to 12% [S3]. If the first half shows it starting (gross margin above 49.5%, indirect costs growing slower than revenue), profit could reach NZ$8m to NZ$9m in two years and the shares be worth 35c to 40c. I give that 20%, inside the good case.
2. Yield buyers and a big return. If the board borrows to return NZ$15m to NZ$20m (6c to 8c) and the market prices the remaining shares on yield rather than on earnings, the price could hold above my value for a while. That is the market paying more than the business is worth, which this desk shouldn't bet on.

The other way: a block sale by Waterman at a discount, cost pressure from fuel and ingredients, or a weak Christmas could take the price to 25c or below.
Exit rule: no position now. If one is opened under a reopen condition: sell at the resolve date, at once if first-half or full-year profit before one-offs falls below the prior year, or if the bank facility isn't renewed by 30 September 2027.

## Corrections to the fact sheet
1. The 70% to 90% payout is not a new policy. The company stated it as its "distribution target" in the FY23 annual report and as "MFB's policy" in the FY24 results presentation [S13][S12]. FY26 already paid 72%. What 4 November added was "fully imputed" and a return to the policy after FY24 and FY25 fell short of it.
2. "Surplus capital" is not cash on hand. Cash was NZ$0.6m at March 2026, a NZ$2.9m dividend has been paid since, the business runs on negative working capital, and the bank facility expires on 5 October 2027 [S3]. A 2027 return would be borrowed.
3. The 40.1c anchor is a good case (FY29-level profit on the board's own plan, or a bid), not a middling case, and it ignores 12.36m performance rights.
4. Performance rights: after the 8 July 2026 grant of 4,890,931 to senior staff (not only the chief executive's 2,075,829), 12.36m rights are on issue, 4.9% of the shares [S17]. The sheet gave 7.47m (2.9%).
5. Free cash flow of NZ$8.5m is flattered by capital spending below depreciation: equipment bought NZ$0.46m against NZ$1.67m of depreciation; capital spending averaged NZ$4.1m a year over five years [S3][S13].
6. Customer numbers are flat over four years, not one: 57.5k, 56.8k, 57.0k and 57.1k at March 2023 to 2026, from 61.7k in FY22's last quarter [S13][S12][S11][S4][S14].
7. FY23's dividends of NZ$17.1m were paid while free cash flow was negative, taking net debt from NZ$2.5m of net cash to NZ$15.3m, before the dividend was stopped [S13]. That history bears on any 2027 return.
8. Waterman's fund: Long Term Food Group LP is managed by Waterman Capital (Fund 3), which closed in March 2016 (AVCJ [S20], secondary). The sheet didn't note that the largest holder is a private equity fund that would normally be selling.
9. The price before the review was 24c, so 32c is 33% above it after the sale failed. The sheet didn't give the undisturbed price.

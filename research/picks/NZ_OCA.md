## The company in plain words
Oceania runs 30 retirement villages and care homes across New Zealand, with about 3,900 residents and 2,200 staff [S13]. It earns money in three ways. The first is care fees: rest home, hospital and dementia care, much of it paid by government subsidies (NZ$98.9m in FY26 [S1]). The second is residents' capital: a resident pays a lump sum for an occupation right agreement (ORA), lives in the villa, apartment or care suite, and on leaving is repaid that sum less a deferred management fee (DMF). The unit is then resold at the current price, and Oceania keeps the difference (the resale gain). The third is development: building new units and selling the first ORAs [S1, note 3.3][S2]. In FY26 operating revenue was NZ$267.1m, of which care was NZ$210.0m including NZ$20.0m of care suite DMF [S2, slides 27 and 33]. At listing in 2017 about three quarters of the portfolio was aged care; now 80% of it is under ORAs [S10].

Who runs it: Suzanne Dvorak, chief executive; Kathryn Waugh, chief financial officer [S3]. Liz Coutts chairs the board (a director since November 2014, re-elected on 30 July 2026 with 88.6% of votes cast); Sarah Ottrey joined in February 2026 and Sally Evans retired at the 2026 meeting [S8][S9][S10].

Who owns it (registered holders at 31 March 2026 [S1, p. 83]): Forsyth Barr Custodians 16.23%, BNP Paribas Nominees 12.67%, Lennon Holdings 4.98%, ACC 4.81%, Generate KiwiSaver 4.39%, Tomlinson Group Investments 3.85%, NZ Depository Nominee 3.53%, then custodians; the top 20 hold 69.55%. 6,722 holders; the 399 holders of more than 100,000 shares own 87.05%. Substantial holders: Forsyth Barr Investment Management 12.24% (11 February 2026) and ANZ New Zealand Investments with related ANZ entities 6.64% (27 February 2026, down from 11.93% in November 2025) [S1][S24]. No substantial holder notice has been filed since [S15].

Five years. Oceania listed in May 2017 at NZ$0.79 and raised NZ$100m in March 2021 at NZ$1.30 [S8]. NTA per share rose from NZ$0.74 (FY17) to NZ$1.62 (FY26) and total assets from NZ$918m to NZ$3,076m [S8][S2]. The share price was NZ$0.76 on 3 July 2023, NZ$0.79 on 6 November 2024, NZ$0.805 on 6 November 2025 and NZ$0.66 on 6 November 2026; the low in that span was NZ$0.51 (19 June 2024) and the high NZ$0.93 (12 December 2025) [S17]. No dividend was paid for FY25 or FY26 [S3][S6].

## What the filings show

### Profit is not cash
Underlying profit (NZ$63.7m, 8.8c a share) adds back realised development margin and resale gains and removes value changes [S2, slide 28]. It also counts deferred management fees as they accrue over a resident's stay. DMF revenue was NZ$44.2m on independent units and NZ$20.0m on care suites, NZ$64.1m in all [S1, note 2.2]. The cash actually collected from DMF was NZ$31.6m [S2, slide 32]. Swapping accrued DMF for cash DMF, and leaving out development margin, FY26 underlying EBITDA of NZ$97.4m becomes about NZ$15.9m of cash earnings from the existing business (python: 97.4 - 49.0 - 64.14 + 31.6 = 15.86). That is before NZ$23.3m of maintenance and refurbishment spending and NZ$14.2m of interest on core debt [S2, slides 31 and 32]. The gap is not an error. A resident pays the DMF on leaving, so with many residents who arrived recently (The Helier, Meadowbank, Franklin) accrued DMF builds up: NZ$293m at FY25, NZ$326.2m at FY26 [S4][S6]. It turns into cash only as residents leave.

Of the NZ$26.7m improvement from FY25, NZ$21.4m came from the two working capital lines; the rest of the business improved by about NZ$5.3m (python: (-15.0 - 11.5) - (-41.7 + 9.9) = 5.3). There was no improvement between the halves even though NZ$13.2m of cost savings landed during the year [S3]. Some levers remain for FY27. About NZ$38m of bought-back units was still held at 31 March 2026, down from NZ$52m, and NZ$48.5m of resale proceeds were receivable at year end [S2, slide 11]. Management also targets about NZ$30m of "cost and cash savings" in FY27, from working capital, lower stock and capital spending as well as costs, plus NZ$20.4m of annualised cost savings against NZ$13.2m achieved [S2, slide 10][S3]. So the FY27 target can be met with a one-time release; whether it lasts is a separate question.

Cash from resident turnover (DMF collected plus resale gains) was NZ$67.6m in FY25 and NZ$68.5m in FY26: flat [S2, slide 32]. The stock of embedded value (accrued DMF plus unrealised resale gains on current residents, from CBRE's valuation) was NZ$458.7m (FY23), NZ$516.8m (FY24), NZ$591.0m (FY25) and NZ$600.1m (FY26). That is 9.4% a year over three years and 2% in FY26, after seven sites were sold [S4][S6].

### Balance sheet and debt
At 31 March 2026: total assets NZ$3,076.1m; refundable ORAs owed to residents NZ$1,258.4m; drawn debt NZ$523.3m and cash NZ$16.6m, net debt NZ$506.7m; gearing 30.1% (target 30% to 35%); NZ$218.3m of bank headroom including cash [S2, slide 30]. The debt is made up as follows. Bank: NZ$50.0m drawn of a NZ$185m general facility and NZ$248.3m of a NZ$315m development facility; the sustainability-linked loan was renewed to FY30 [S1, p. 19]. Bonds: OCA010 (NZ$125m, 2.30%, due 19 October 2027) and OCA020 (NZ$100m, 3.30%, due 13 September 2028) [S1][S2]. In June 2026 Oceania issued OCA030, NZ$125m at 5.55% to 1 July 2032, to repay bank debt and for general purposes [S12]. When OCA010 is replaced at about today's rate, interest on that NZ$125m rises by about NZ$4.0m a year (python: 125 x (0.055 - 0.023) = 4.0).

NTA was NZ$1,174.0m, NZ$1.62 a share. Oceania's own "net adjusted value", which uses CBRE's values for care suites, was NZ$1,105.6m, NZ$1.53 a share [S2, slide 30]. Over 90% of assets are property at valuer's figures. Completed villages, net of what residents are owed, were NZ$852.8m, valued by CBRE at discount rates of 14.0% to 20.0%, price growth of 0% to 3.0% for the near term and 3.0% to 3.5% after. Care land and buildings were NZ$835.9m, valued by capitalising earnings at 12.0% to 15.0% [S1, notes 3.1 and 3.2][S2, slide 27]. Unsold new stock was NZ$227m: NZ$157.5m of independent units and NZ$69.3m of care suites, of which 54% are occupied by residents not on ORAs [S2, slide 6].

Evidence on what the property fetches. Seven sites went for NZ$51.1m in FY26 "at or around carrying value", but with NZ$7.2m of losses on sale and associated costs, about 14% of the proceeds [S2, slides 13 and 28]. Development margin is reported at 30% on new sales [S10], a measure that leaves out shared costs such as common facilities (Jarden's estimate of true sector margins is "a loss to about 10% to 15%" in good cases [X5]). The Helier, the flagship Auckland project completed in FY24, cost NZ$149m in full (land, amenities, civil works, design, capitalised wages and interest) and had brought in NZ$145m of sales by May 2026 with 20 apartments left [S2, slide 17]. The filings don't give the prices of the last 20. At NZ$1.5m to NZ$2.5m each the full-cost margin would end at about 17% to 31% (python: (145 + 30)/149 - 1 = 0.174; (145 + 50)/149 - 1 = 0.309), earned over three or more years. So the true margin is somewhere between Jarden's figure and the reported one; I test both in Method 2.

It said its advisers had modelled the options "including the case for share buybacks" and that "notwithstanding current market valuations, the long-term returns from investing in and growing our business ... are expected to outweigh the possible short-term impact of a buyback" [S8]. - Buyback: up to 3.5m shares to 31 July 2027, only to offset staff equity plans; 120,740 were bought in August [S11][S15].
- Dividend policy: 40% to 60% of free cash flow from operations, to resume once it is positive [S3][S7]. Executive short-term pay is tied to the same measure [S10].
- Repayment law. In December 2025 the government proposed repayment within 12 months with interest after six months, and Oceania said it already works that way [S14]. Labour's policy of 31 August 2026 is repayment within three months, with a bill before select committee within its first 100 days [X1]. At the meeting the CEO said three months would mean operators hold "materially more capital ... permanently", but that Oceania "does have the balance sheet to meet this requirement" [S10]. On my rough sums, if units take about six months to resell, three-month repayment would tie up roughly a quarter of a year's NZ$108.6m of resident repayments, about NZ$27m, with nine months' worth about NZ$54m (my estimate; python: 108.6 x 3/12 = 27.2). That is 4c to 7c a share, plus the interest on it.
- Care funding. A Ministerial Advisory Group report released on 18 August 2026 recommends near-term funding increases and a split funding model; the government has not responded [S22]. This could help Oceania, whose care business is 79% of revenue.
- Director dealings: none on market in 2025 and 2026; only staff-plan issues to the CEO and CFO [S15].

## Valuation

### Method 1: price to NTA against the listed peers (the market's quoted measure)
Summerset is at 0.430 times NTA and Ryman at 0.459 times, an average of 0.445 (python: (6.20/14.41 + 1.84/4.005)/2 = 0.4448). On Oceania's NZ$1.62 that gives NZ$0.70 (0.43 times), NZ$0.72 (the average) and NZ$0.745 (0.46 times). Oceania's weaker cash suggests the low end: 0.40 to 0.46 times gives NZ$0.65 to NZ$0.75, central NZ$0.70. This depends entirely on what the market pays for the sector, and the sector multiple moves a lot. Oceania itself was at about 0.58 times in January 2026 (NZ$0.905 against NZ$1.57 at 30 September 2025), 0.50 times on 27 August (NZ$0.81 against NZ$1.62) and 0.36 times at its June 2024 low (NZ$0.51 against NZ$1.41 at 31 March 2024) [S17][S5][S6].

### Method 2: cash flow to shareholders
Script: scratchpad calc/dcf.py, run with python3 -I. NZ$m, years FY27 to FY36, discounted at a 10% cost of equity (my assumption for a geared business in an out-of-favour sector), with a terminal value growing 2.5% a year.
- Free cash flow from operations without the working capital lines: starts at FY26's -NZ$26.5m. FY27 adds the remaining NZ$7.2m of cost savings and loses NZ$2.0m for the sites sold (their NZ$5.1m of FY26 earnings [S2, slide 29] less the interest saved on the NZ$51m). Cash from resident turnover (NZ$68.5m) grows at g a year: 4% (low), 7% (central) or 10% (high). Care earnings add NZ$1.5m a year to FY29. Maintenance spending grows 3% a year. The OCA010 refinance costs NZ$1.8m in FY28 and NZ$4.0m a year from FY29.
- Working capital: NZ$30m in FY27 and NZ$11m in FY28. That is bought-back units falling from about NZ$38m to about NZ$15m (Oceania's target is resale within nine months [S7]) and receivables from about NZ$48.5m to about NZ$30m.
- Development, after construction spending and development interest: NZ$65m (FY27) and NZ$42m (FY28) while the NZ$227m of unsold stock sells down, as FY26's NZ$74.2m did (147.9 - 62.1 - 11.6) [S2, slides 11, 31 and 32]. Net adjusted value NZ$1.53 [S2, slide 30]. Less corporate costs, which site valuations don't carry: about NZ$28m a year after the FY27 savings (FY26 corporate segment -NZ$31.8m [S2, slide 29]), valued at 7.5 times, NZ$210m or 29c. Only a bid or a break-up realises it, and the board has ruled out the review that would start either (above).

### The range
- As a listed share, what the market pays the sector: NZ$0.65 to NZ$0.75, central NZ$0.70 (Method 1). It depends on the sector's multiple.
- What the cash is worth: NZ$0.17 to NZ$1.07, central NZ$0.58 (Method 2). It depends on how fast cash from resident turnover grows.
- What a buyer of the whole would pay: NZ$1.24 to NZ$1.34 (Method 3). It depends on someone bidding.
For the outcomes I use Method 1 for six-month prices, because that is how the market sets prices from month to month. For 24-month middling values I use the midpoint of Methods 1 and 2 (NZ$0.65), because over two years the cash has to justify the multiple.

## Why the market is wrong
At NZ$0.66 I can't say it is. The discount to NTA has three causes. First, cash: the existing business has not yet turned its accrued fees into cash, and on the central cash case it won't cover its own interest and upkeep until about FY31. Second, the board: it has refused the routes that would turn NTA into cash for holders (a review, a sale, buybacks from asset sales), and holders backed it [S8][S9]. Third, politics: Labour's three-month repayment pledge and a close election [X1][X3]. Oceania trades in line with Summerset and Ryman on NTA, and the price implies cash growth of about 7.5% a year, which is demanding but possible. A positive FY27 cash figure is what the market has been told to expect since May [S3]. Achieved with working capital, it is not new information.

What could make the market wrong, and so what the deep dive watches for: (1) cash from resident turnover stepping up at well over 7.5% a year, which would show in the 1H27 result's resales cash reconciliation; (2) a confirmed National-led government taking the three-month rule off the table, after which the sector could return to its pre-31 August multiple (0.50 times for Oceania, NZ$0.81); (3) a bid. The second is a sector bet whose first evidence, the preliminary count, the market will price at Monday's open.

## What would make this wrong
The two things most likely to make the "no case" view wrong:
1. Cash from resident turnover grows faster than the price implies. Many residents arrived in the last three years, and care suites turn over in about three years [S10]. If accrued DMF (NZ$326.2m [S4]) converts faster than I assume, free cash flow from operations could pass NZ$15m within two years without help from working capital. At 10% growth Method 2 gives NZ$1.07. The 19 November reconciliation is the first test.
2. The election and the sector multiple. If the official count confirms a National-led government, the sector could recover the 14% to 28% it has lost since 31 August (Oceania to about NZ$0.81). That is a sector move which this file declines to bet on, and Monday's open will price much of it.
A third, smaller one: a bid. Arvida went at 0.83 times NTA [S23], the chair says sector consolidation is on the table [S10], and private capital already owns NZ village operators. But the board has turned down a review, and there is no sign of an approach.
Against a purchase: a Labour-led government, a weaker housing market cutting NTA, or the FY27 cash target met only with a release that reverses in FY28 could each take the price to NZ$0.50 to NZ$0.57.
Exit rule: no position now. If one is opened under a reopen condition: sell at the resolve date; sell at once if free cash flow from operations is negative at the FY27 result, if a three-month repayment bill is introduced, or if gearing goes above 35%.

## Corrections to the fact sheet
1. The fact sheet missed the 2026 annual meeting. A holder resolution for an independent strategic review to address the NTA discount was opposed by the board, which also rejected buybacks funded by asset sales, and it failed with 86.85% of votes against [S8][S9]. That removes the obvious route from the NTA anchor to the price; NZ$1.62 is a bid value at best (Method 3 puts a buyer nearer NZ$1.24 to NZ$1.34).
2. FY26's -NZ$15.0m included about NZ$11.5m of working capital release (net buybacks +NZ$13.9m, deferred settlements -NZ$2.4m). Underneath, the business ran at -NZ$26.5m against -NZ$31.8m in FY25, and the two halves of FY26 were the same [S2][S5]. The "64% improvement" is mostly working capital.
3. The fact sheet doesn't explain why cash lags profit. DMF is recognised at NZ$64.1m a year but collected at NZ$31.6m [S1][S2]; that gap, not development, is the core of it.
4. The repayment law has moved on since December 2025. The government now proposes nine months with 10% within four weeks (18 September 2026), and Labour pledged three months (31 August 2026) [X2][X1]. Oceania "already works this way" only for the December 2025 proposal. Its chair and CEO said three months would need more capital [S10][S14]. The fact sheet also doesn't mention the 7 November election or the sector's fall since 27 August (Summerset -28%, Ryman -14%, Oceania -19%, FNZ -0.8%) [S17].
5. Peer comparison: the companies themselves now steer to cash from existing operations, and Summerset moved its dividend to that basis on 27 August 2026 [S18]. On it Oceania is the weakest of the three, so its slightly lower NTA multiple looks earned. The definitions differ (Summerset's is before interest).
6. Development margin: the reported 30% leaves out shared costs. The Helier recovered NZ$145m on a NZ$149m cost with 20 apartments left, about a 10% to 15% full-cost margin (my inference) [S2, slide 17].
7. Minor: the seven FY26 sales brought in NZ$51.1m (the cash flow line "proceeds from sale and/or disposal of PP&E and IP" is NZ$52.0m) and cost NZ$7.2m in losses and associated costs, about 14% of proceeds, so "at or around carrying value" is generous [S2, slides 13, 28 and 31]. The NZ$136,775 liquidity figure and the 0.41, 0.43 and 0.46 multiples are correct.

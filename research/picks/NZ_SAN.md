> The desk's view in one line: on its own, Sanford is worth about what it trades for. Underlying profit without stock revaluations rose from NZ$7m (FY21) to NZ$71m (FY25), but FY25 was a high point (mussels had a once-off year, and the first half of FY26 was down 10% underneath a "record" headline), and over the five years the business turned only NZ$37m of operating cash into free cash after capital spending and leases. On mid-cycle cash earnings of about NZ$59m at 9.4 to 10.5 times (listed seafood companies trade on 6 to 14 times earnings, with a median of 9.4), it is worth NZ$5.90 to NZ$6.60 as a listed company; NZ$6.10, the close on 23 October before Umios's purchase was disclosed, sits in that range. The fact sheet's NZ$9.75 is a takeover price, and only a bid would deliver it. A bid is possible but slow: Umios has a minority-first, control-later record, but its own top-up clause makes a bid before November 2027 cost it about NZ$15m more, it has just agreed a C$1.25bn deal for High Liner Foods, any stake of 25% or more needs ministers' consent under the Fisheries Act (in 2000 ministers turned down every overseas bid for half of Sealord), Sanford's constitution lets the board refuse transfers to overseas buyers, and the Tasman group at 19.1% can block a Umios scheme. On 45% bad (NZ$6.10), 45% middling (NZ$7.20) and 10% good (NZ$8.60) by 7 May 2027, with the 5c final dividend, the expected gain is +3.7% before costs and +0.2% to +2.2% after them, against a minimum of 3.5%. Wait for the 17 November result, the notice of meeting and Umios's next filing.

Corrections to the fact sheet (research/facts/NZ_SAN.md), for the red team:
1. Underlying profit. The fact sheet's FY25 NZ$67.6m, H1 FY26 NZ$27.2m and twelve-month NZ$63.2m start from statutory profit (after impairments, restructuring and FY25's 29.2% tax rate) and take out the revaluations after tax. On the company's adjusted EBIT, less revaluations and the fuel gain, less net interest, taxed at 28%, the figures are FY25 NZ$70.9m, H1 FY25 NZ$31.5m, H1 FY26 NZ$28.5m and twelve months to March 2026 NZ$67.9m (workings under "What the filings show"). Both are fair; this file uses the adjusted basis and notes that small impairments recur every year.
2. The "if it goes against us" NZ$2.81 is not a usable bad case. It puts FY24's earnings on Delegat's 7.3 times, which gives less than net tangible assets of NZ$3.14 a share before any value for NZ$377m of fishing quota at 1980s-era cost, and it ignores that Umios paid NZ$7.47 a share on 26 October. Delegat itself is on 5.4 times EBITDA once its NZ$277m of debt is counted, about where Sanford trades. The "nothing happens" value here is NZ$6.10.
3. The "if it goes our way" NZ$9.75 is a control price, not a listed one. It is 7.6 times twelve-month EBITDA before revaluations once debt and leases are added, which is what a bidder might pay (Tassal went for 10.0 times, High Liner for about 9.5 times), but listed seafood companies trade on 6 to 14 times earnings and Sanford traded on 8.4 times its twelve-month underlying profit on 23 October. So NZ$9.75 belongs in the good case, reached only by a bid.
4. The top-up terms, more exactly: a Umios scheme agreement or takeover notice must be made within 12 months of 2 November 2026, but the scheme can be implemented, or the offer go unconditional, after that. The matching payment applies only to shares Umios buys from third parties above 17.532% (2.0 points above its 15.532%) at an average above NZ$7.47, is paid on all 9,638,100 sale shares, and is owed even if Umios later sells down. It does not cover a bid by anyone else [S8].
5. Masfen's last notice (14 March 2022) shows 7,046,201 shares (7.5%); the 7,093,500 (7.58%) is the annual report's register figure, not the notice [S1, S16].
6. The 25% "legislative limit" in Sanford's 2016 constitution note is out of date. Since the 2021 amendments a New Zealand listed company is an overseas person if overseas persons together own 50% or more, or if overseas holders of 10% or more (alone or with associates) control more than 25% of the votes or half the board (Overseas Investment Act s 7(2)(c) and 7(3)) [S20]. For Sanford the live limit is Umios's votes: at more than 25%, Sanford would become an overseas person.
7. Price: NZX shows the 6 November close as NZ$6.65 (the brief's "about NZ$6.63" is a rounding) [S33].

## The company in plain words
Sanford is New Zealand's largest seafood company by quota, holding about 20% of the country's fishing quota [S32]. It has three businesses: wild catch (13 vessels, a Timaru factory, deepwater species such as hoki, scampi, squid, toothfish and orange roughy, and fishing partners who catch its entitlements), salmon farming at Big Glory Bay on Stewart Island with processing in Bluff, and mussel farming in Marlborough and the Coromandel with a factory at Havelock [S1]. It no longer fishes North Island inshore stocks: since October 2023 it rents that catch entitlement (ACE) to Moana New Zealand for about NZ$11m a year rising to NZ$13m, under a contract of at least ten years [S32]. FY25 revenue was NZ$584.1m: salmon NZ$127.5m, mussels NZ$125.5m and wild catch NZ$318.9m before eliminations [S1].

Management: David Mair, managing director since May 2024 (formerly managing director of Skellerup); chair Sir Rob McLeod, who said in May 2026 he would retire "at an appropriate time during calendar year 2026" [S4]; Tony Carter joined as an independent director in February 2026 and stands for election on 16 December [S34]. Besides the managing director, two of the six non-executive directors are not independent: Craig Ellison, former chief executive of Ngāi Tahu Holdings, and John Strowger, "associated with substantial product holder group Tasman Equity Holdings Limited, Arden Capital Limited and Past Limited Partnership" [S1, S28].

Owners now: the Tasman group 19.13%, Umios 15.53%, ANZ New Zealand Investments 7.89%, Masfen Securities about 7.5% [S8, S9, S12, S15, S16]; below 5%, Rural Equities held 4,375,000 shares (4.68%) and ACC 2,033,738 at 1 October 2025 [S1]. The four substantial holders own about 50%.

## Why big investors may be overlooking it
- Size and index: NZ$622m, outside the 20 largest NZX companies (config/nzx_top20.csv, where the smallest is NZ$1,550m). I didn't check NZX 50 membership.
- Liquidity: a median of NZ$68,837 a day on market over the 20 sessions to 6 November [S33].
- Register: four holders own about half, so the free float is about NZ$310m.
- Foreign money is held back by design. The constitution lets the board refuse transfers to overseas persons, suspend their votes and force sales once overseas ownership nears the level that would make Sanford itself an overseas person, and NZX lists the shares with a Non-Standard designation because of it [S1, S18]. An overseas fund can own Sanford, but it can be told to sell.
- Sector: US tariffs on mussels, soft scampi prices in China and management's repeated warning that results "should not be assumed" to repeat [S1, S4].
- History: three chief executives since 2021 (Peter Reidie resigned in August 2023, Craig Ellison acted, David Mair from May 2024), impairments every year, and a 2021 prosecution over the San Waitaki (NZX announcements list).
- Analyst coverage: not checked.

### Is FY25's level new, or a peak?
By division (profit contribution before head office costs, which were about NZ$32m in FY25) [S1, S4]:
- Mussels: NZ$13.9m in FY24, a record NZ$34.8m in FY25 helped by "favourable climatic conditions" that gave much higher processing yields, then NZ$7.7m in H1 FY26 against NZ$18.1m, with revenue down 27%, soft prices and frozen half-shell stock building up. FY25 was a one-off.
- Salmon: NZ$40.9m in FY24 and NZ$50.4m in FY25 (a record 5,623 tonnes harvested). H1 FY26 showed NZ$39.6m, but the group's NZ$17.5m revaluation gain was mostly the salmon biomass; treating all of it as salmon, salmon made about NZ$22m without it, against about NZ$27m a year earlier, on 34% lower sales volumes. The fish held back should be sold in H2.
- Wild catch: NZ$55.7m in FY24, NZ$52.4m in FY25 (scampi revenue down 30.9% on price), NZ$31.7m in H1 FY26 including the NZ$3.6m fuel gain, so about NZ$28m against NZ$24m: better prices on many deepwater species and a strong squid catch.
- Head office costs fell from about NZ$37m (H1 FY25 annualised) to about NZ$28m (H1 FY26 annualised), and net debt has halved since FY24, so some of the gain is lasting.

The desk's reading: FY25 was a high point that leaned on a once-off mussel year, and H1 FY26 was down 10% underneath the "record". My FY26 estimate (H1 NZ$28.5m plus an H2 with salmon selling its built-up fish, mussels clearing stock at low prices and wild catch steady) is NZ$55m to NZ$61m underlying, and a mid-cycle level of about NZ$55m to NZ$60m. Management says the same in its own words: several of the contributors "were factors outside of Sanford's control" and "It should not be assumed that this half year financial result will be repeated in H2" [S4].

### Balance sheet and what the assets may be worth
- Net assets NZ$780.2m at 31 March 2026 (NZ$8.34 a share); net tangible assets NZ$3.14 a share [S3, S33]. Bank debt NZ$120m drawn of NZ$140m, maturing April 2028 to April 2031 [S3]; lease liabilities NZ$41.4m at September 2025 [S1 note 19].
- Fishing quota NZ$377.2m and marine farm licences NZ$102.7m, carried at deemed cost [S1 note 14]. The only market evidence in the filings: in FY22 Sanford sold its lobster quota (CRA2, CRA7, CRA8) for NZ$52.7m against a book value of NZ$8.3m [S1, S32]; and for FY25 "an updated independent valuation of the Group's remaining New Zealand fishing quota ... shows headroom over the $377m carrying value ... which is in excess of the market capitalisation shortfall" of NZ$224m, so the quota is worth more than NZ$601m. A separate independent valuation of Sanford as a whole put the NZ$741m of net assets "within this range" [S1 note 14.1]. Neither figure is published. I found no other independent valuation.
- What a quick reader would miss: the value-in-use test on the wild catch business had only NZ$83.9m of headroom, and it assumed wild catch EBIT grows 12.1% a year for five years; at 7.0% a year the headroom would be gone [S1 note 14.2]. So the case for the quota being worth far more than book rests on what others would pay for quota, not on what Sanford earns from it.
- Managing director David Mair holds 1,800,000 options exercisable from 15 November 2027, at an exercise price the report gives as NZ$4.0063, worked from the 20-day average price before 1 May 2024 with an 8% a year uplift (1.259 times) less dividends paid [S1, S35]. A bid would make them valuable; they are 1.9% of the shares.
- No capital raisings in the last two years; dividends of 10c a year, fully imputed [S1, S4].

### The range
- As a listed company with nothing happening: NZ$5.90 to NZ$6.60, central NZ$6.10 to NZ$6.30 (Method 1 at 9.4 to 10.5 times mid-cycle cash earnings, cross-checked by Method 2 at Sanford's own and Delegat's EV multiple). It depends most on whether mid-cycle cash earnings are nearer NZ$45m or NZ$70m.
- To a bidder: NZ$8.50 to NZ$10.40 (Method 2 at 8 times mid-cycle to twelve-month EBITDA; a 40% to 55% premium to a NZ$6.10 undisturbed price; above net assets of NZ$8.34 and below the asset value with quota of more than NZ$10.74). It depends on someone getting through the approvals below.

### The top-up clause, and what it signals
The clause is protection for a seller who had just been approached: if Umios signs a scheme or gives a takeover notice within 12 months and it succeeds, Ngāi Tahu gets the higher price on its 9,638,100 shares; and if Umios buys from others above 17.532% within 12 months at an average above NZ$7.47, Ngāi Tahu gets the difference on all its shares [S8]. Three readings:
1. Both sides thought a Umios control move was possible. Ngāi Tahu, advised by Bell Gully, priced that in; Umios accepted it.
2. It makes Umios wait. A bid at NZ$9.00 signed before 2 November 2027 would cost Umios an extra NZ$14.7m (NZ$1.53 on 9.64m shares); signed after, nothing. Buying on market between 17.532% and 20% at, say, NZ$8.00 would cost an extra NZ$5.1m. So Umios's cheapest next steps are to buy 1.87m shares to 17.532% at any price, then up to 20% (another 2.3m) at no more than NZ$7.47, and to leave any formal bid until after November 2027.
3. It doesn't stop Umios selling to someone else's bid, and pays Ngāi Tahu nothing if a third party bids. Umios has bought a seat at the table, a block that can stop anyone else taking Sanford to 100%, as much as a first step to control.

### What the rules allow
- Takeovers Code (Takeovers Panel guidance) [S19]: below 20%, anyone may buy by any means, so Umios can go to 20% and the Tasman group can buy only 0.81m more shares. Between 20% and 50% a holder can increase only by a full offer, or a partial offer, conditional on passing 50% (a partial offer for less needs holders' approval), or by an acquisition, allotment or buyback approved by an ordinary resolution of the other holders. The 5% a year "creep" is open only to holders above 50%. Compulsory acquisition comes at 90%, so either blockholder (each above 10%) can stop the other reaching 100% without its acceptance.
- Schemes: 75% of votes cast in each interest class, plus more than 50% of all voting rights; the acquirer's shares vote in a separate class [S19]. In a Umios scheme the Tasman group would hold 22.6% of the class that votes, enough to block unless more than 91% of that class voted; in a Tasman scheme Umios would hold 19.2% of the other class and block unless more than 77% voted.
- Fisheries Act ss 57B to 57H (Part 8): an overseas person needs ministers' consent (the Minister for Oceans and Fisheries and the Minister of Finance, administered by LINZ) before acquiring shares in a quota owner that give it a 25% or more ownership or control interest, increase one, or make the company an overseas person. The tests are the investor test and the benefit to New Zealand (the 2019 reprint calls it the national interest), weighing jobs, technology, exports, competition, investment and onshore processing [S21]. Without consent, quota can be forfeited to the Crown (s 58) [S21].
- Overseas Investment Act s 7: Sanford becomes an overseas person if overseas persons own 50% or more, or overseas holders of 10% or more control more than 25% of the votes [S20]. Umios at 15.5% is a 10% holder, so its votes above 25% would tip Sanford over.
- Sanford's constitution, Fourth Schedule: the board may require any holder or buyer to declare whether it is an overseas person; may refuse to register a transfer that would take overseas ownership past the "Overseas Ownership Threshold" (90% of the legislative limit, which the board can raise, including above 100% where an exemption applies); and may suspend votes and force the sale of "Affected Shares" to a New Zealand buyer within 20 working days, last in, first out. Its decisions are "final and conclusive" and not open to review [S18]. In practice a foreign bidder needs the board's co-operation as well as consent.
- Aggregation limits (Fisheries Act s 59): no one may own more than 45% of the total commercial catch of the Schedule 5 species or 35% of most others [S21]. That limits what Sealord, Talley's or Moana could do with Sanford's quota, and they would also need Commerce Commission clearance.

### Who could block, and what a bid would need
- A Umios bid needs: ministers' consent; the board to accept it, or at least not use its transfer and vote-suspension powers; the Tasman group's support (for a scheme) or enough acceptances from Tasman, Masfen and ANZ (for an offer to pass 50%); the money; and, before November 2027, the top-up.
- A Tasman bid needs about NZ$680m at NZ$9.00 for the 80.9% it doesn't own, and Umios's acceptance to reach 90%; no consent if the group is a New Zealand person.
- The likeliest structure, by precedent, pairs an overseas processor with a New Zealand owner of the quota.
- Who blocks: the Tasman group and Umios each block the other's scheme and compulsory acquisition; ministers block any overseas control; the board can block overseas transfers.

### Comparable cases and what holders received
- Sealord, 2000 to 2001: on 8 May 2000 the Finance and Fisheries Ministers declined every overseas application to buy Brierley's 50% of Sealord, saying "government policy generally points to quota rights being held by New Zealand interests" [S23]. The Government had also refused to let Nissui buy the whole 50% and limited an overseas party to a quarter. The deal that went through was a Treaty of Waitangi Fisheries Commission and Nissui partnership: NZ$207m for the half (NZ$181m in cash plus NZ$26m of debt), with all of Sealord's quota (23% of the national total) kept in Māori hands. A Sanford-led consortium dropped out because the price was "too high" [S23]. Nissui has since gone to half of Sealord: LINZ's 2013 consent for New Zealand Longline, half owned by Sealord, lists Nissui and Te Ohu Kai Moana at 25% each [S22]. So overseas ownership of half of a big quota holder has been allowed, with the other half in New Zealand hands.
- Tassal (ASX), 2022: Cooke paid A$5.23 cash, a 49% premium to the undisturbed price, an enterprise value of A$1.7bn and about 10.0 times FY22 operating EBITDA before biological revaluations [S30].
- High Liner Foods (TSX), October 2026: Umios is paying C$27.00, a 79% premium, C$1.25bn of enterprise value, about 9.5 times rolling adjusted EBITDA of US$94.0m (desk arithmetic: C$1.25bn at US$0.713) [S25].
- Clearwater Seafoods (Canada), 2020: Premium Brands and a Mi'kmaq First Nations coalition paid C$8.25 a share, about C$1bn including debt; the fishing licences went to the Mi'kmaq-owned entity [S31]. Again, licences stayed with local owners.
- NZ King Salmon (NZX), October 2026: Oregon Group (Malaysia) is taking control (50.68% of votes) by buying 9.87% from China Resources at NZ$0.225, below the market, subject to a rule 7(c) vote and Overseas Investment Office consent [S29]. Control changed hands with no premium for other holders.
- Sanford's own lobster quota sale, 2022: NZ$52.7m, 6.3 times book [S1, S32].

### What the share is worth if nothing happens
NZ$6.10: Method 1's mid-cycle cash earnings of about NZ$58m at 9.4 to 10 times. It is also the NZX close on 23 October 2026, the last session before the Umios deal [S33], and the market has moved little since (ENZL US$45.16 on 23 October, US$45.22 on 5 November). It could go lower when the situation ends: if FY26 shows cash earnings nearer NZ$45m, Method 1 gives NZ$4.20 to NZ$4.70. But Umios can buy up to 20% at prices that low without owing Ngāi Tahu anything, and it paid NZ$7.47 a month ago, so there is a buyer below NZ$6.

## Why the market is wrong
On the business alone, it isn't. At NZ$6.65 Sanford trades on 9.2 times twelve-month underlying profit, 10.6 times mid-cycle cash earnings and 6.0 times mid-cycle EBITDA (EV NZ$765.3m including debt and leases), which is about what listed seafood and food companies are paid for earnings this volatile. The price is NZ$0.55 above the NZ$6.10 undisturbed price; that gap is the market's allowance for a bid. A bid at NZ$9.00 with a 10% chance inside six months is worth about NZ$0.29; the rest of the gap is the market's hope that FY26 holds near FY25. The only way the market is clearly wrong is if a bid is much more likely, or much closer, than the rules and Umios's own clause suggest. I can't say it is, so there is no trade today.

## What would make this wrong
1. A bid comes sooner and at a higher price than I allow. Umios's habit is minority first, control later; it paid 22.5% over the market for the block, approached Ngāi Tahu itself, and already has consent for quota and ACE through Maruha NZ. New Zealand has let Nissui own half of Sealord. A Umios-plus-New Zealand-partner structure, or a deal with the Tasman group, could appear within months, and then 10% for the good case is too low and NZ$8.60 may be too low too (net assets with quota exceed NZ$10.74).
2. FY25's level is the new normal. Overheads have come down by about NZ$9m a year, interest has halved, the inshore business is now a steady rent, and salmon and deepwater prices are firm. If FY26 shows NZ$65m or more and cash comes back, the market could pay 11 to 12 times without any bid, NZ$7.70 to NZ$8.30.
3. The other way: the bad case is too kind. A weak FY26 (mussels and scampi down, revaluation reversing, fuel costs up) could take cash earnings towards NZ$45m and the price towards NZ$4.50 to NZ$5.00, with Umios buying only below NZ$7.47.
Exit rule if it is reopened and bought: sell on a close below NZ$5.60, on an equity raise or Umios selling, or when the event that reopened it resolves (a binding offer moves to the deal sleeve's rules; a failed approach is sold the next session).

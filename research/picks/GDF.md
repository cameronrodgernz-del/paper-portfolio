Value pick (rulebook section 5a): it doesn't qualify. Over 24 months the "nothing happens" value is above the price (about +21%) only because it assumes two years of 12.5c distributions from a lending book that keeps performing. The real bad case for a two-year hold of this book is a credit loss: one borrower group has A$95.9m of loan limits plus A$60m of contingent construction funding, about half of what holders will own after the specials, and 71% of loan limits sit on loans above 80% loan to value. A loss there takes the value 15% to 25% below the price, outside the 10% limit. As an ordinary pick with the 1H27 result as its event (resolve by 30 April 2027), the expected gain after costs is about +3.0%, below the minimum of 3.38% for 173 days.

Order of work: the price was in the brief and the fact sheet, so it was in view from the start. "What the filings show" and "Valuation" were worked from Garda's filings and the listed lenders' own NTA and distribution notices; where a method is about what the market pays (the peer ratios, the yields), it says so.

In short. The fact sheet's anchor, a 28% discount to the company's pro forma NTA of A$1.56, overstates the gap. On every quoted security, counting the A$15.0m of loans that the employee plan holders owe Garda, book value is A$1.508 before the 10c of specials and A$1.408 after them. The price less the specials and the accrued December quarter distribution is A$1.006, which is 0.715 times that book. Listed Australian real estate lenders trade at 0.63 to 0.96 times NTA after a bad 2026 for private credit (the two Metrics trusts were suspended for a week in September while KPMG finished their audits; 360 Capital Mortgage REIT's largest borrower went into administration). Garda sits between the stressed ones (MRE 0.63, TCF 0.81, MXT 0.83) and below the diversified ones (QRI 0.91, MA1 0.92, LF1 0.96), and its yield of 12.4% on the ex-special price matches TCF's 12.4%. That is about where its book says it should be: it is the most concentrated and highest-leverage book of the group, its FY26 lending income was almost all added to loan balances rather than paid in cash, and it costs A$7.3m a year to run, about 2.4% of equity. Holders get 20.2c in cash by July 2027 (the 10c of specials and three quarterly distributions of 3.4c); the rest of the A$176.6m of sale cash goes into new loans, which the board calls "the primary destination". The on-market buyback (up to 21m securities to 21 April 2027) has bought back under 1% of the securities in three years and none in FY26. HGT Investments is a Brisbane family company whose director, Andrew Thornton, has sat on Garda's board since 2020; it holds 17.1% of all quoted securities, has not bought since March 2023, backed the strategy and can block any scheme or special resolution, but there is no sign it wants to bid. At A$1.12 the case is a yield trade at the minimum, not a mispricing. The February result is what would show the market is too gloomy, or right.

## The company in plain words
Garda is a Brisbane group that owned industrial property in Southeast Queensland and, since about 2022, lends to property developers there. It is internally managed: Garda Holdings Limited and Garda Diversified Property Fund are stapled, and the fund's responsible entity, Garda Capital Limited, is a Garda subsidiary [5]. Executive Chairman Matthew Madsen (base salary A$695,000, one year's notice) runs it with about a dozen staff; the board is Madsen, Paul Leitch (independent), Andrew Thornton (a director of HGT Investments) and Oliver Talbot [4, pp10-11, 29].

On 21 October 2026 it agreed to sell its last seven properties to a Charter Hall managed fund for A$300.0m, on top of Heathwood (A$17.5m) and Pinkenba (A$33.3m) sold in late September: A$350.8m in all, 1.4% below carrying value, or 5.2% below after A$13.2m of incentives, a rent guarantee and selling costs [1][3]. All A$185.3m of drawn debt is repaid on 20 November 2026. What is left is cash and development loans: the board says lending will be "our primary deployment focus in the medium term", with property ownership kept as an option [1].

FY26 (to 30 June 2026): revenue from operations A$38.3m, funds from operations (FFO, the company's underlying earnings) A$22.4m or 11.1c, statutory profit A$23.8m, distributions 8.5c, NTA A$1.64 [5]. Lending was already 49% of revenue [4, p12].

Holders (register at 27 July 2026, 2,855 holders, top 20 own 59.8% of the 200.66m counted) [4, p83]: HGT Investments 37,340,745; JP Morgan Nominees 6.14%; HSBC Custody Nominees 5.82%; Netwealth 5.38%; Citicorp Nominees 3.00%; Glenelg-Park Nominees 2.50%; Peter Zinn 4.99m plus 3.0m (3.7% of all quoted); Madsen Nominees 4.74m registered (16.91m relevant interest). Directors and executives hold about 9% between them, most of it Madsen's.

Five years: A$1.69 at the end of 2021, A$1.30 (2022), A$1.23 (2023), A$1.21 (2024), A$1.175 (2025), A$1.145 on 30 October 2026; with distributions reinvested, a 9.6% loss over the period (Norgate total return series). NTA peaked at A$2.05 in 2022 [7] and the price has stood 22% to 37% below NTA in each of the last five years [6, p35].

### Earnings against cash
In FY26 the loan book grew by A$90.1m while Garda advanced A$88.1m and was repaid A$16.1m, a net A$72.0m of cash [4, cash flow statement]. The other A$18.0m is interest, fees and fair value gains added to loan balances, about all of the A$16.9m of lending income after the credit loss and fair value adjustments. So in FY26 Garda's lending income was, in substance, not received in cash; FFO counted it, and the FY26 FFO also adds back the credit loss allowance and fair value losses (A$2.4m), so distributions at 100% of FFO leave every loan loss to come out of NTA [4, p15]. Until now rent paid the distributions. From 20 November there is almost no rent, so the 3.4c quarters will be paid from the sale cash until loans repay with their accrued interest. That is fine for paying them; it means the true test of the earnings is the repayments.

FY27 guidance, raised on 21 October: FFO 12.5c (from 10.5c) and distributions 12.5c (from 9.0c), a payout of about 100% because without property there is no tax-deferred income to shelter [1, p3]. In July the lending forecast for FY27 was A$22.7m of revenue with no redeployment assumed [6, p14].

### Method B: the distribution a lender of this kind can pay, at the yield the market asks
- Sustainable distribution: 12.5c in FY27 with much of the cash idle. Fully deployed (A$220m to A$260m of loans at 14% to 16% after fees run off, the cash at 3.5%, costs A$7.3m, swaps A$1.2m), FFO would be 14.7c to 17.0c. Normal losses of 2% of loans a year (A$4m to A$5m, 2.0c to 2.4c a security) would come off NTA because FFO adds them back.
- Yield asked: 12.4% to 12.6% for TCF, 10.8% for MXT, 8.3% to 10.5% for the diversified funds.
- 12.5c at 12.6% is A$0.99; at 10.8% A$1.16. 15c at 12.6% is A$1.19. Range A$0.99 to A$1.19 ex the specials; the middle about A$1.08.

### Method C: run-off value (the ceiling if the board stopped lending)
Collect the loans as they fall due (WALT about 8 months; A$148.6m due within 12 months at June [4]) and pay everything out over about 18 months: A$307.0m of equity, less A$10m of running costs, A$2m of wind-up costs and a 5% loss on A$194m of limits: A$1.31 a security, A$1.22 in today's money at 10% a year, A$1.32 with the specials. Only a wind-down, a full return of capital or a bid gets holders this; it is the good case, not the middling one.

### Loan stress on the book
A 15% loss on group 8's A$95.9m takes 6.6c off book (to A$1.34); 25% takes 11.0c (A$1.30); 25% after the A$60m Burleigh construction facility is drawn takes 17.9c (A$1.23).

### Range
The business is worth about A$1.00 to A$1.20 a security as a listed lender (A$1.10 to A$1.30 with the specials), centred near A$1.07 (A$1.17 with). Method A depends most on which peers the market sees Garda among; Method B on whether 12.5c is sustainable as cash; Method C on the board choosing to stop lending, which it has said it won't.

What it can do:
- Buy up to 19.99% without a bid: about 6.3m more securities (about A$7m). Above 20% it needs a takeover bid, holder approval or the 3% creep, and the creep (item 9 of section 611) is open only to a holder that has had at least 19% for the previous six months, so not yet.
- Ride a buyback: increases from a buyback are exempt (item 19). If all 21m securities were bought back it would hold 18.9%.
- Bid: a takeover bid for everyone at any price. At A$1.20 the 82.9% it doesn't own would cost about A$217m. With its director on the board, the other directors would need an independent committee and an expert. No sign of it.
- Block: a scheme needs 75% of the votes cast; at a turnout like 2025's, HGT's votes are about a third of them. It can block any scheme by someone else and any special resolution, including this year's 10% placement capacity.
- Call a meeting: as a 5% holder it can requisition one for the company (section 249D) and the fund (section 252B). Winding up the fund at members' direction needs an extraordinary resolution, half of all votes that could be cast (section 601NB): HGT and Madsen together have 25%.
- Its stance: its director sits on the board that chose the sale and the lending strategy. There is nothing in the filings to suggest it wants cash back rather than lending.

### Comparable cases
- 360 Capital Mortgage REIT (TCF): a small listed real estate lender with seven loans. Its largest borrower (Bathla) went into administration in the September 2026 quarter; the price fell to a low of A$4.19, receivers are selling the security, and the trust now trades at 0.81 times NAV paying 5.0c a month (12.4%); it has bought back 118,244 units at an average of A$4.99 [26]. Holders got their income; the gap to NAV stayed.
- Metrics Real Estate Multi-Strategy (MRE) and Master Income (MXT): suspended from 28 September to 5 October 2026 while KPMG finished the audits; MXT's NTA was cut 4c (2%), and both are under a review of historical NTA, fees and unit prices. MRE is at 0.63 times NTA and MXT at 0.83 [24][25].
- Australian Unity Office Fund (AOF): holders voted to sell everything and wind up; it paid a 9.0c special in November 2024, had NTA of A$0.42 a unit left at 31 December 2025, and delisted in June 2026 [27]. A wind-up is how a holder gets NTA in cash. Garda's board has chosen the opposite.
- Garda itself: the price has stood 22% to 37% below NTA in each of the last five years [6, p35], and in that time the buyback took less than 1% of the securities [20].

### The "nothing happens" value
Settlements on time, the specials and quarterly distributions paid, no buyback of note, no bid, no loan problem. The price then follows the multiple the market already pays: 0.715 times today, 0.64 times before the sale was announced (A$1.025 on 20 October against a fully diluted pre-sale book of A$1.592). I use 0.70 times: a little below today because the buying since the announcement came with volume nine times normal and is likely to leave with the specials. At 30 April 2027 that is A$0.997 a security, plus 16.8c received in the window: A$1.165. That is the bad case below.

## Why the market is wrong
I can't say that it is, at this price. On the measure the market uses for real estate lenders, Garda is priced among its listed peers: 0.715 times book against 0.63 to 0.83 for the three with problems or doubts, and a 12.4% yield, the same as TCF's, for a book that is more concentrated and more leveraged than TCF's. The gap to the A$1.56 headline is explained by the specials (10c), the employee securities (about 5c), the running costs (A$7.3m a year, worth 0.1 times book or more against an externally managed fund) and the loan risk.

What could show the market is too gloomy is the 1H27 result: the first accounts with no property, under PwC's first review, showing whether the loan marks hold, whether loans are being repaid in cash with their accrued interest, where the A$176.6m went, and whether the buyback was used. A clean result with repayments and a buyback in use would move Garda towards TCF and MXT (0.80 times or so). A markdown would move it towards MRE (0.63). Until then the holder is paid about 12% a year to carry that question.

## What would make this wrong

1. A credit loss in a concentrated, high-leverage book, most likely to show at the 1H27 result under PwC's first review. The places to look: group 8 (A$95.9m, Richlands 42% sold at completion, Burleigh's A$16.9m early-stage loan at 89% loan to value before its development approval, and the A$60m construction facility behind it), group 2 (96% before presales, not started) and group 4's corporate facilities.
2. The discount staying or widening while the cash is lent out: the board says it will close the gap through earnings over a full cycle, not by returning capital; its buyback has never been used at size; and listed private credit is out of favour (Metrics, TCF). A return to the pre-sale 0.64 times would take about 7c off.

What the deciding events must show:
- 20 November settlement: the money in and the debt gone, as planned. On its own that changes nothing, since the price assumes it. - 1H27 result (about 4 February 2027), all of: (a) NTA at 31 December 2026 of at least A$1.45 on the company's count after the 7.3c special, so no markdown under PwC; (b) no loan in default, receivership or stage 3, and the credit loss allowance no more than 2% of the amortised cost loans; (c) at least A$40m of loans repaid in the half, with accrued interest and fees received in cash; (d) either the buyback used (at least 2% of the securities) or group 8 below 40% of loan limits; and (e) a price at or below A$1.065 with the March quarter's 6.1c still to come (A$1.005 after it goes ex).

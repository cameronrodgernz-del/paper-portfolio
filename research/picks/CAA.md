> In short: Capral is a better business than "low return" suggests, but the fact sheet's A$21.54 is a bid-level value, and the market has a settled habit of paying about 0.9 times tangible book for it. Three corrections change the picture. First, the company's NTA of A$13.43 already leaves out the A$29.0m deferred tax asset, so the "harsher" A$11.61 is wrong; tangible book with the tax asset is about A$15.38 a share today. Second, profit after the accounting lease charges overstates cash: actual rent paid runs about A$4m a year above the profit and loss charge, and capital spending is above depreciation, so pre-tax cash earnings after rent and capex are about A$20m to A$25m, not the A$31.7m of underlying pre-tax profit. Third, the low reported return on equity is mostly the cash pile and the tax asset: on tangible operating capital the business earns about 12% to 14% after a notional tax, about what Big River and MaxiPARTS earn. On cash earnings the business is worth about A$15.40 to A$17.20 a share (two methods), against A$13.16. But the board cut its payout range in May, a bank covenant caps distributions below the prior year's profit, the cash is kept for acquisitions and metal-price swings, and nothing dated forces a re-rating. Since 28 September the company has been buying 40% to 50% of the market's volume, and the price rose 15% while it did; that support ends on 18 December. If the market keeps paying 0.9 times a growing NTA, holders make about 18% in 22 months, which clears the cash-plus-5% minimum but not the value-pick test.

## The company in plain words
Capral makes and sells aluminium extrusions (shapes pushed through a die from aluminium billet) and distributes rolled sheet and plate, all in Australia. It has six extrusion plants with eight presses and 65,000 tonnes a year of capacity, nine distribution centres and 15 trade centres, and says it holds about 27% of the Australian market [2][5]. Half of its volume goes to industry (transport, marine, solar, fabrication) and half to building, mostly detached and low-rise housing: in the twelve months to June 2026, industrial 50%, residential 40%, commercial 10%; 52% of volume is sold straight from the mills, 48% through its distribution network [5]. No customer is more than 10% of revenue [4]. Selling prices pass the aluminium price (the LME price plus a regional premium) through to customers, so revenue moves with the metal price and the profit comes from the conversion and distribution margin per tonne.

Who runs it: Tony Dragicevich, managing director since 15 April 2013 (also a non-executive director of Fletcher Building since August 2024); Mark White, chair since 8 May 2024, formerly general manager of CSR's aluminium business; Tertius Campbell, CFO and company secretary [3].

Who owns it (28 February 2026 register and later notices) [3][26][27][28][29]: Allan Gray Australia 3,968,483 shares (25.05% when notified on 14 July 2026; about 25.7% of today's shares); the National Exchange group (David Tweed) about 2,250,000 shares in two names on the register, about 14.6% of today's shares (see "Holders" below); Perpetual 1,262,267 (8.03% on 15 October 2026, falling); the managing director 515,368 (3.3%) [30]. The top 20 held 70.01% and there were 2,162 holders [3].

- Size and trading: A$203m market value; the median session from 5 to 30 October traded A$217,363, but the company itself bought 38.5% of the shares traded from 28 September to 30 October (daily buy-back notices against Norgate volume [9]); without the buy-back the median session traded about A$105,000.

Two "new" holders this year (United Super 5.02% and Hostplus 5.11%) are funds Allan Gray manages money for (both are named in Allan Gray's own notice), so they mostly overlap with its stake [27][29].
- No franking: Capral pays no tax, so its dividends are unfranked and will stay so until about 2030 [6]. Australian income investors, who get a tax credit with franked dividends from taxed peers, have less reason to own it.
- Sector and history: a cyclical metals business that lost money in FY15 and FY19 [19][23] and traded at 0.45 to 0.65 times NTA from 2016 to 2019 (below).
- Analyst coverage: I found none in the company's filings and did not search further.

What the table says:
- The LME revaluation recurs in both directions and nets to about nil: -A$2.2m in total over FY16 to FY25. Leaving it out is fair. It is not cash in itself: it marks stock to the current metal price. A falling metal price brings a revaluation loss but releases cash from stock and debtors.
- The real one-offs are the FY19 restructure (-A$6.7m), FY20's JobKeeper wage subsidy (+A$11.9m) and FY25's insurance settlement (+A$2.8m net, mostly the A$3.0m claim) [1][18][19]. The fact sheet's "FY20 was A$22.9m before tax" includes JobKeeper; underlying it was about A$12.0m.
- Rent paid in cash is above the accounting charge. In FY25 Capral paid A$22.3m in rent (A$18.0m principal, A$4.3m interest) but the profit and loss charge was A$18.3m (A$14.0m depreciation of leased assets plus interest) [1][2]. The gap was A$0.7m to A$2.3m a year from FY19 to FY24 and A$4.0m in FY25. So the company's underlying EBIT and pre-tax profit overstate cash earnings after rent by A$2m to A$4m a year.
- Capital spending has risen above depreciation: A$11.8m in FY25 and A$8.7m in the first half of 2026 alone, with A$7.3m more contracted at 30 June for projects at Canning Vale and Bremer Park; owned-asset depreciation runs about A$10m a year [2][4][5]. FY26 capex looks like A$17m to A$20m.
- Two regimes. FY16 to FY20 earned A$143 to A$320 a tonne after rent and averaged A$9.7m a year of pre-tax cash. FY21 to FY25 earned A$485 to A$602 a tonne and averaged A$28.6m. Since then the business closed and restructured Bremer Park (2019, about A$8m of annual savings claimed [19]), bought the G.James extrusion plant at Smithfield (2021, taking a competitor's NSW capacity [18]), bought five trade centres and distributors since 2022 [2], and got stronger anti-dumping measures (below). The margin per tonne has held at A$519 to A$548 through FY24 to FY26 even as volume fell, which is the best evidence the step-up is structural, but it is only four years old.

A fair mid-cycle figure. Volume has averaged 65.7 thousand tonnes over FY16 to FY25 and 70.4 over FY21 to FY25; I use 68. Margin per tonne after rent has averaged A$563 over FY22 to FY25 and A$548 in the latest twelve months; I use A$545. That gives EBITDA after rent of about A$37.1m and EBIT after rent of about A$26.9m; less capex of about A$12m, pre-tax cash earnings of about A$25m a year, or about A$17.5m after a 30% tax. Range: A$19m (62 thousand tonnes at A$500) to A$29m (70 thousand at A$580). The ten-year average, which includes the weaker pre-2021 regime, is A$19.2m.

Where FY26 sits. Guidance since August is "broadly in line with prior year depending on some recovery of the residential housing market" [5], cut in May from "slightly above" [6]. The twelve months to June 2026 show EBIT after rent of A$26.2m against my mid-cycle A$26.9m, on volume (66.4 thousand tonnes) at the ten-year average. The company calls this the bottom of the cycle [2]; on volume and margin it is close to the middle of the cycle, not a trough. FY26's cash earnings will be below mid-cycle because of the capex step-up, and working capital "will lift in 2H26 as payments for higher cost inventory fall due" [5].

### 2. The tax position
- Losses: A$133.1m of carried-forward tax losses at 31 December 2025, A$63.9m booked as a deferred tax asset (A$19.2m of tax) and A$69.2m not booked (A$20.8m), plus A$21.5m of unbooked deductible timing differences (A$6.5m) [1]. The first half of 2026 used A$5.3m of tax value (about A$17.7m of taxable income) [4], leaving about A$115.4m of losses (A$34.6m of tax) at 30 June 2026.
- How long: Capral's taxable income has run at about A$31m a year (FY25 used A$9.4m of tax value [1]). At that rate the losses last about 3.7 years from June 2026, to about early 2030; the unbooked timing differences add a few months. Calc in calc/value.py.
- What they're worth in cash: A$34.6m of tax not paid over about 3.7 years is worth about A$28m at a 10% discount rate (A$27m to A$30m at 12% to 8%), about A$1.75 a share; with the timing differences, about A$33m.
- Is the deferred tax asset realistic to count? Mostly yes. The booked asset of A$29.0m is a net figure: A$19.2m for losses plus about A$9.8m of ordinary timing differences (lease liabilities, staff provisions, stock) net of deferred tax on plant and leased assets [1]. Capral has used losses every year since 2021, KPMG made recognition a key audit matter and reviewed it with tax specialists [1], and the asset covers "three to four years" of forecast taxable profit [4]. The condition to watch is the company's own footnote that the losses are "subject to same business test" [2]: if the business changed materially (a large acquisition outside aluminium, for example), the losses could be lost.
- Two things the fact sheet missed. (a) The company's NTA already leaves out the deferred tax asset: net assets A$251.5m less intangibles A$9.1m less the A$29.0m asset is the A$213.4m NTA [5]. The "harsher" bad case of A$11.61 takes the asset out twice. (b) Reported profit will start to carry a tax charge before any cash tax is paid. The unbooked loss pool left at June 2026 was about A$15.4m of tax value, which is being booked at about the rate it is used, so in roughly 1.5 to 2 years (about 2028) the company runs out of losses to book and the asset starts to unwind through the profit and loss account. Reported profit would then fall by up to 30% with no change in cash.
- Who the losses are worth something to. Australian holders who can use franking credits get the company tax back from taxed peers; for them Capral's tax-free years mostly bring forward cash and are worth much less than the A$28m above. A foreign holder, or one who can't use credits, gets the full value. The market price is set mainly by Australian holders, so in the market-value methods below I count the losses at half their value; at full value in the high case.

### 3. Imports and anti-dumping
- Extrusions from China: the Minister continued the measures for five years from 29 October 2025 (ADN 2025/096). Effective rates are modest: 0.8% to 5.6% for the largest examined exporters, 18.1% for Press Metal International, 1.7% for residual exporters and 29.4% for uncooperative and all others, some by a floor-price method; two exporters (Jiangsheng and Zhongya) are not covered at all [12]. The company called it "a modest strengthening" [12].
- Malaysia and Vietnam: the company said in February that the Malaysian measures would expire in 2026 with a decision due in June [2], and in August that measures were "continued for a further five years to 2031", with a floor price and modest duties [5]. I did not find a Capral ASX announcement or read the Commission's record for this one.
- Windows and doors (case 691): applied for by Ventora Group and the Australian Glass and Window Association, initiated 25 November 2025, inquiry period 1 July 2024 to 30 June 2025 (Commission case page, the one page of the record I could open [S3]). The rest of the Commission's record is blocked to me (robots exclusion on the industry.gov.au public record), so the following comes from two secondary sources: a preliminary affirmative determination was made, and since 15 October 2026 importers of Chinese aluminium windows and doors must lodge securities of 5.3% to 73.3% of value; the final recommendation is due to the Minister by 16 December 2026, with the Minister's decision within 30 days; Australian product holds about 79% of a market of about A$2.8bn, Chinese imports 19% [S1]. Chinese imports rose about 30% after the case began [S2]. The timetable has slipped once already (from a final report due 25 November) [S1].
- What a win or a loss does to earnings (my estimate, not the company's). Residential building was about 40% of Capral's volume, roughly 26 thousand tonnes [5]. If duties let local fabricators win back a quarter to a half of the imported share, local window demand rises by roughly 6% to 12%; on Capral's window-related volume that is about 1.2 to 2.4 thousand tonnes, worth about A$1m to A$2.4m a year before tax at a marginal A$800 to A$1,000 a tonne, or A$0.45 to A$1.10 a share at 7.5 times. A loss, with imports growing as they have, takes perhaps A$1m to A$1.5m a year off within two years. It matters at the margin; it doesn't change the case. The extrusion measures run to 2030 and 2031, so they are not at stake inside the window; losing them altogether would be a return toward the FY16 to FY20 margins.

### 6. Leases
- Lease liabilities were A$71.3m at 30 June 2026, more than the A$62.1m of net cash: counting leases, Capral had net debt of about A$9m then, about A$14m after the buy-backs since [5]. A$18.2m falls due within a year and the average building lease has 4.2 years left [1].
- Because the leased assets were booked below the lease liabilities when the standard changed in 2019 (the company says this reduces reported net assets by about A$17m, A$1.09 a share), the profit and loss rent charge runs below the cash rent [5][19]. Rent paid (A$22.3m in FY25, A$23.0m in the latest twelve months) is about two-thirds of underlying EBIT (A$35.8m) [1][2].
- Sub-lease income of about A$4.0m a year sits inside EBITDA; the non-cancellable receivable is A$14.9m, running out over the next four years unless extended [1]. That is a small risk to earnings after 2029.
- On cash after rent the value still supports the case: at A$13.16 the business (market value less A$57.0m net cash) is valued at about 4.0 times EBITDA after cash rent, 5.6 times EBIT after cash rent and 5.9 times pre-tax cash earnings (normal capex); including leases, about 3.6 times EBITDA before rent. Those are low against the peers below. The support is weaker than the accounting profit suggests, because cash after rent and capex is A$20m to A$25m, not A$31.8m.

### Method 1: business value on pre-tax cash earnings at peer multiples
Mid-cycle pre-tax cash earnings times a peer multiple, plus surplus cash, plus the tax losses (half value in the central case). Per share on 16.04m shares including performance rights (calc/value.py).
- Low: A$20m x 5.5 (MaxiPARTS) = A$110m, plus A$40m cash, no value for the losses: A$150m, A$9.35 a share.
- Central: A$25m x 7.5 (between MaxiPARTS and Big River's average of 7.7 and the five-peer median of 8.4, a little lower for Capral's metal-price and import risk) = A$187.5m, plus A$45m, plus A$14m (half the losses): A$246.7m, **A$15.38 a share** (A$15.99 on basic shares).
- High: A$28m x 8.4 = A$235m, plus A$57m, plus A$28m: A$320.6m, A$19.99.
Depends most on the multiple: each 1.0 of multiple is A$25m, about A$1.56 a share.

### Method 2: discounted cash flow
Pre-tax cash earnings of A$25m a year growing 2.5%, no cash tax for four years and 30% after, plus A$45m of surplus cash (calc/value.py):
- at 10%: A$302.9m, A$18.89 a share; at 11%: A$275.0m, **A$17.15**; at 12%: A$252.8m, A$15.76.
Depends most on the discount rate and on whether A$25m holds; at A$20m and 11%, about A$14.3.

### Cross-checks
- Return against tangible book (the peers' line above): operating tangible capital of A$151.3m (NTA less net cash at June 2026) at 11.6% after tax is worth 1.36 times, A$205.9m, plus A$45m and A$14m: A$16.53 a share (A$12.76 to A$19.71). This is close to Method 1, since both rest on peer pricing.
- The market's own band: 0.81 to 0.97 times today's NTA of about A$13.50 (June NTA less the buy-backs since) is A$10.94 to A$13.10. That is what the market has paid for this business, year after year, and it is the anchor for the middling outcome below.

### Testing the fact sheet's A$21.54
Would a buyer pay it, and how would the price get there? A$21.54 is about 1.5 times December 2026's likely NTA and about 8.6 times mid-cycle pre-tax cash earnings plus all the cash and the full tax losses. A trade buyer might pay that; the market has not paid above 1.18 times NTA in the last ten years. It mixes bases: an 11.7 times multiple from a company that earns twice Capral's return, applied to accounting profit that overstates cash after rent and capex, plus the full tax losses, which Australian holders using franking credits value at much less. Nothing dated would deliver it: the buy-back is capped at 10% a year, the board cut its payout range, and no holder over 20% is moving. It is the good case, not the middling one.

### The range
Business value: A$15.40 to A$17.20 a share (Methods 1 and 2, central), within A$9.35 to A$20.00. Trading value, on the market's own habit: A$10.90 to A$13.10. The main thing each depends on: Method 1, the multiple the market will pay; Method 2, the discount rate; the trading value, whether the market keeps paying about 0.9 times a growing NTA.

## Why the market is wrong
1. The market prices Capral like a low-return metals business on its reported return on equity, but the low return is the cash pile and the tax asset; on its tangible operating capital it earns about what Big River and MaxiPARTS earn, which trade at 1.6 to 1.75 times NTA against Capral's 0.96.
2. With no franking and a small, illiquid register, Australian income investors have little reason to own it.

Why that might change: earnings that stay at mid-cycle levels through a housing recovery, a windows duty, and buy-backs below value keep lifting NTA per share by about A$1.50 to A$2.00 a year (the business keeps all its pre-tax cash until about 2030). What argues against it changing soon: the market has priced this business the same way for four years, the board is holding the cash, and the company itself has been the marginal buyer since September.

## What would make this wrong
1. The market never pays more than about 0.9 times NTA for this business, and earnings slip back toward the older regime. FY16 to FY20 earned a third of today's margin per tonne, and the step-up is four years old. A metal-price fall, more imported windows and fabricated product, or weaker protection would bring that back; in 2016 to 2019 the shares sat at 0.45 to 0.65 times NTA.
2. The cash isn't returned or is spent badly. The board cut the payout range to 40% to 60%, a covenant caps distributions below the prior year's profit, the stated use of the cash is acquisitions and volatility, and working capital absorbs cash when metal prices are high. 3. Price support ends. The company has bought about a third of all shares traded since March and nearly 40% since late September; the price rose 15% while it did. After 18 December the buyer is gone until at least late February.

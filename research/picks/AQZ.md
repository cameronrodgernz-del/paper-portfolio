# Alliance Aviation Services Limited (ASX:AQZ): deep dive, the case for buying

Written 2026-11-09 by Claude for the one-company-at-a-time process (rulebook section 5a, docs/RESEARCH_PROCESS.md). This is the case for buying. A second model writes the case against without seeing this file, then the two are argued out in research/debate/AQZ.md, which ends with Cam's decision.
Price at writing: A$0.40 (last sale, ASX company data, read 9 November 2026) [S16]. Market value: about A$87.3m (218,352,403 shares on issue, ASIC's daily short position file [S17]). Net debt was A$459.8m at 30 June 2026 [S2]; after the A$37.8m net raised in September it is about A$422m, so the business is valued at about A$509m before leases.
Status: finished. Not a value pick at A$0.40 (see "Where it lands"). This is a simulation and not financial advice.

Order of work: the company came top of the weekly sweep (reports/research_sweep.md), which tags falls from the high, so the price was in view from the start. The values below are worked from cash flow, debt and asset values, not from steps off today's price.

## Where it lands
Wait. On my numbers Alliance is much further from fair value than Bapcor was, and it still fails the rulebook's value test. On my numbers the expected gain is about +51% after costs to 31 August 2028, far above the 12.9% minimum, and the middling case is nearly double the price. But the bad cases are a fresh share raising at a deep discount, or worse, and they sit about 45% below the price, against a 30% limit. That's the shape the value test exists to keep out, and I don't think it should be stretched for a company with a going-concern warning in its last accounts.

The evidence from outside the filings mostly backs the business: the mines Alliance flies to are expanding or were saved, the jets it needs to sell are in demand, and Qantas can't easily do without it before 2028. The weak spot is the Brisbane hangars, which have been on the market for over two years without selling. On these numbers it qualifies at about A$0.31, or close to today's price if the first-half result in late February shows the asset sales done and the ANZ loan refinanced, because then the bad case no longer needs a raising.

## What the price assumes
The business is valued at about A$509m: A$87.3m of shares plus about A$422m of net debt after the raising. Leases are left out (about A$21.5m at June) [S3], which is also how the company measures its own leverage [S1].

FY27 guidance, given on 26 August 2026 [S1]:
- underlying EBITDA of A$175m to A$190m;
- underlying profit before tax of A$55m to A$60m;
- underlying free cash flow of A$10m to A$20m, before a final A$33m payment for aircraft bought from AerCap;
- A$60m to A$75m of asset sales;
- net debt of about 2.1 times EBITDA by 30 June 2027.

On the headline figures the shares look absurdly cheap: 2.8 times EBITDA and about 2.2 times the guided profit after tax. Those figures mislead, because the business spends most of its EBITDA keeping the fleet flying. Maintenance spending on the existing fleet was A$135.6m in FY26, against A$102.0m of depreciation and EBITDA of A$177.5m [S2]. What's left after maintenance and interest is the free cash flow the company guides to, A$10m to A$20m.

So the honest measure is free cash flow. At A$0.40 the shares are about 5.8 times FY27's guided A$15m at the midpoint. A company this indebted, with a going-concern warning and two guidance cuts last year, can trade at 5 to 6 times free cash flow. The price therefore assumes that FY27's cash flow is what the business earns for good: no benefit from the further A$11m of cost cuts due in FY28 (A$38m a year in total, A$27m of it in FY27) [S1], no fall in interest as the debt comes down, and no end to aircraft buying. Or it assumes a real chance of another raising, which would spread the same cash flow over more shares.

Another way to see it: if the four outcomes below keep my values, the price implies about a 27% chance that the turnaround works (the middling or good case). I put that at 60%.

## The one thing the market is missing
Stated as the rulebook asks: the market is pricing Alliance close to what its assets would fetch in a forced sale, as if the going-concern warning will come true, when the condition named in that warning (the raising or the asset sales) was met in September, the mines outside Qantas's control that Alliance flies to are expanding or were saved, and the E190 jets it needs to sell are scarce enough that lessors and Qantas itself are buying them.

The evidence:
- The going-concern note says cash had to come from "either" the raising "or" the asset sales before 31 December 2026, to meet future covenants [S3]. The A$40m raising completed: institutional on 27 August, retail on 16 September [S10, S11].
- Asset cover, as a sense check. At June, net assets were A$373.1m, plus a A$71.4m deferred tax liability that wouldn't be paid in a sale below book [S3]. An independent valuer put the E190 fleet A$67m above its book value at 31 December 2025 [S7]. With the raising, that is about A$549m, or A$2.52 a share. If the aircraft and equipment sold 40% below those values, inventory at half, and A$50m went on winding down, about A$89m (A$0.41 a share) would be left. That's today's price. This is my arithmetic, not a valuation.
- The FIFO customers and the E190 market are covered in "The test outside the filings" below.

What the market is right about: the business turns little of its EBITDA into cash, the founder, his successor as managing director and the finance director have all left within about a year, guidance was cut twice in FY26, and the ANZ loans fall due in September 2027.

## The test outside the filings
Done on 9 November 2026, under docs/RESEARCH_PROCESS.md step 2. The questions the filings can't answer:
1. Will the planned asset sales actually happen, and at what prices?
2. Is the FIFO business, the half not controlled by Qantas, holding up?
3. Does Qantas still need Alliance through the period?
4. Who is selling the shares down, 43% below the price of a raising two months ago?

Some sources couldn't be reached from here: Airfleets' fleet pages refuse automated reading, and the CASA register's own download failed, so the register was read through a public mirror. I didn't contact anyone.

1. Asset sales: the jets look sellable, the hangars don't.
   - The two Brisbane Airport hangars (65-73 and 81 Pandanus Avenue) have been offered three times:
     - Savills offered Hangar 1 by expressions of interest closing 17 July 2024 [S20];
     - Colliers offered both by expressions of interest closing 27 March 2026 [S20];
     - the Colliers listing is still live, updated 1 November 2026, seven months after it closed, with no "under offer" or "sold" flag [S20];
     - a related-listings panel on another page shows the pair under JLL Brisbane, "for sale or lease", which may be a portal error [S20].

     After 28 months on the market, the hangars are unlikely to complete by 31 December 2026, the date in the going-concern note. They're part of the A$47.1m of assets held for sale, which carry A$16.5m of liabilities [S3].
   - The E190 market is tight.
     - Qantas's WA subsidiary, Network Aviation, plans up to 14 mid-life E190s to replace its Fokker 100s. It has secured three, all from Europe: ex-KLM and ex-Eastern Airways aircraft [S21]. The first arrived in Perth on 7 September 2026 and enters service in January 2027 [S21]. That leaves about 11 still to find, and Alliance will have 12 spare E190s [S1].
     - Lessor Azorra booked 20 E190 freighter conversions in July 2026, with rights to more, the largest order the programme has had [S22]. Embraer counts about 380 E190s suitable for conversion [S22], and industry sources say feedstock has been hard to find because airlines are keeping their E-Jets [S22].
     - Valuers report regional jet values rising since 2025, with the E190 singled out [S23].
     - Supply is coming, but slowly. KLM Cityhopper still flies 17 to 19 E190s and plans to phase them out over years, not months [S24]. JetBlue's 13 went to the lessor Azorra in 2025 and 2026, and Breeze retired its last in September 2026 [S24].
   - On the CASA register (read through the aeromarket mirror), 43 E190s and 36 Fokkers are still registered to Alliance [S19]. The one E190 shown as no longer Alliance's, VH-SEF, was re-registered as VH-XFC in 2022, not sold [S19]. So none of the jets for sale has left yet.
   - Sense check: the six AerCap jets still to settle cost US$20m to US$25m in total [S3], about US$3.5m to US$4m each. The airfleets records for several of them show early ex-JetBlue aircraft from 2005 to 2007, bought as they stand and then brought up to standard [S24, S2]. Jets of that vintage have fetched single-digit millions of US dollars [S23], so 12 of them are worth something like A$60m to A$150m, depending on which go and in what condition. That comfortably covers the jet part of the A$60m to A$75m plan.

   Verdict: the asset sales are likely, but later than the company hopes. The jets will carry them, and the hangars may not sell in FY27.
2. The FIFO business is in good shape.
   - Newmont's Tanami mine is Alliance's largest FIFO contract by hours flown, extended for five years in May 2024 [S25]. Newmont is building a second expansion there, with underground works due to be commissioned in the third quarter of 2026. That stretches the mine's life to about 2040 and should add 150,000 to 200,000 ounces a year from 2028 to 2032. The site has more than 1,800 FIFO workers [S25].
   - Phosphate Hill, which Alliance has served for years [S26], was going to close by September 2026 if no buyer was found. Mayfair bought it, taking it over from 1 April 2026 and keeping its 540 workers [S26].
   - South32's Cannington and Groote Eylandt contract runs to 2030 [S27]. BHP's Nickel West, which closed, cost about A$5m of EBITDA in FY26 and is already in the base [S27].
   - Contract flight hours were flat in FY26 (27,328 against 27,376) while contract revenue rose 7% to A$319.2m [S2], so Alliance could put prices up.
   - The competitive change to watch is Network Aviation's E190s, which enter service in January 2027 [S21]. They replace about ten Fokker 100s one for one rather than adding capacity, but their longer range gives Qantas more reach in WA charter [S21].

   Verdict: the FIFO business supports the middling case, and there's no sign of the slippage that hit the wet lease.
3. Qantas needs Alliance until at least 2028, though it's building alternatives.
   - Qantas is building its own E190 fleet in WA [S21], and it moved A220s onto Adelaide–Brisbane from March 2026, replacing E190s [S28].
   - But the 23 jets left on the wet lease are, in my judgement, a large share of QantasLink's east-coast regional flying. Losing them at short notice would cost Qantas flying it can't quickly replace.
   - Qantas owns 19.7% and committed about A$8.1m to take up its full share of the raising [S2].
   - The 2023 extension set seven-year terms, with two two-year options [S28]. Users of a frequent flyer forum read the wet lease as running to 2028 [S28]. The company hasn't published the end date.

   Verdict: Qantas is a backstop until 2028, which covers most of the period. After that it's a real risk, and it's in the bad cases.
4. The selling came from the free half of the register, not from short sellers or the big holders.
   - ASIC's daily short position reports show short positions at 0.1% to 0.3% of the shares through September and October, peaking at 632,780 shares on 15 October [S17]. So short sellers aren't driving the fall.
   - The raising added 57.3m shares at A$0.70, 35.6% more [S2, S17]. About 30.5m shares traded in September and October, 14% of the company [S18].
   - Retail holders took up only 10.6% of their entitlement, leaving 9.0m shares with the sub-underwriters [S11]. Citigroup crossed 5% twice around the raising, through stock lending [S14].
   - The four big holders own about half the company: Qantas 19.7%, Remco Properties 12.5%, Viburnum 10.3% (pre-raise figures) and Kiowa 8.2% [S3, S14]. Qantas and Viburnum committed to their full entitlements [S2]. Kiowa's notice of 8 September shows its share count unchanged at 17,859,559, diluted from 11.09% to 8.67% [S14], so it didn't take up. Remco filed no notice, which a fall of a point or more would have needed, so it probably took up. No big holder has filed since 15 September [S15].
   - Directors bought:
     - Simon Lange, who held senior roles at Airwork until 2021, including running its freighter airline, bought about 555,000 shares for about A$348,500 between 1 and 22 September, at A$0.54 to A$0.68 [S13]. He had sold 60,000 at A$0.675 in March [S31].
     - Chair James Jackson bought 48,500 at about A$0.72 on 31 August, then took up his entitlement [S13]. Bernard Campbell spent about A$36,600 [S13].
   - The meeting on 26 November asks holders to ratify the placement shares and approve an extra 10% placement capacity [S12]. That's standard for a small company, but together they'd let the board issue about 55m more shares without another vote. The market may be reading that as a warning.

   Verdict: the selling looks like holders who took stock in the raising leaving a broken story, not informed selling. It could run further, which is the route to the watch price.

What this changes:
- It backs the middling case and lowers the chance of the worse case. The chances in Outcomes and chances were set with it in hand.
- It doesn't change the bad case's value, which is set by what a fresh raising would cost.
- The edge is real: the evidence disagrees with what the price assumes. But it sits in the middle of the range, while the rulebook's binding test is the bottom of it.

## The company in plain words
Alliance flies people for mining companies, flies jets for other airlines under contract, and runs charters, from bases in Brisbane, Adelaide, Darwin, Townsville, Cairns, Perth and Rockhampton [S3]. It owns its fleet. At 30 June 2026 it had 80 aircraft in service: 45 Embraer E190s (five of them dry-leased to Airnorth) and 35 Fokkers (23 F100s and 12 F70s), a count that includes five F100s in storage [S2, S3].

FY26 revenue was A$712.6m [S2]:
- FIFO and other contract flying: A$319.2m (46%), up 7%;
- wet leasing (aircraft, crew, maintenance and insurance supplied to another airline), mostly E190s for QantasLink and Fokkers for Virgin Australia: A$313.1m (45%), down 1.8%;
- charter A$20.3m, scheduled flights A$11.1m, aviation services A$21.6m (down from A$110.0m as engine and parts trading was wound down), and other A$27.2m.

The Qantas deal was revised on 5 August 2026 [S4]:
- prices rose from 1 July 2026, with a new annual escalation;
- the fleet committed to Qantas falls from 30 to 23 E190s during FY27.

With five jets coming back from Airnorth's dry lease, 12 E190s become free, to be sold, broken up for parts or used to replace Fokkers in FIFO work [S2]. The Fokkers are to be replaced by E190s through 2030 [S30].

The last 15 months:
- November 2025: FY26 guidance was set "materially lower" than analysts expected: EBITDA of A$190m to A$210m and profit before tax of A$46m to A$50m [S9]. The finance director left that month; founder Scott McMillan had already retired as managing director [S3].
- February 2026: the Fokker fleet, engines and parts were written down by A$164.8m, and profit guidance was cut again, to A$35m to A$40m [S7, S8]. Alliance called the Qantas wet lease "commercially unviable and cash flow negative" [S8].
- August 2026: the Qantas deal was revised [S4]. FY26 came in at A$177.5m of EBITDA and A$38.2m of profit before tax, inside the second range but below the first [S1]. A A$40m raising at A$0.70 was announced with the result, and the auditor flagged a going-concern uncertainty [S1, S3].
- October 2026: managing director Stewart Tully left. Steven Greenway, formerly chief executive of flyadeal (Saudia's low-cost airline), president of WestJet's Swoop and chief commercial officer of Scoot, started on 1 October [S6].

Qantas agreed in 2022 to buy the rest of Alliance for about A$614m. The ACCC opposed it, and the two dropped the deal in 2023 [S29].

## Why big investors may be overlooking it
They aren't overlooking it so much as unable or unwilling to hold it:

- Screens read it as a loss-maker: the statutory loss after tax was A$90.9m [S1].

- The raising added 35.6% more shares, and the new holders who wanted out have been selling (question 4 above).

## Valuation
Worked at August 2028, after the FY28 result, about 22 months out. The method is free cash flow, because that's what the business has left after keeping its fleet flying. EBITDA multiples are shown as a cross-check.

The assumptions for FY28:
- Maintenance spending of A$125m to A$135m.
- Interest at about 7.5% on the debt.
- Little cash tax, given the FY26 losses and the deferred tax position.
- No dividends before August 2028 in any case. The company says none in FY27 [S1].

Arithmetic for the middling case:
- EBITDA: FY27's midpoint of A$182.5m plus the further A$11m of cost cuts gives about A$193m, rounded down to A$190m for cost inflation.
- Cash flow: less A$130m of maintenance, A$27m of interest and about A$3m of tax, about A$30m.
- Value: 5.5 times is A$165m, or A$0.76 a share, taken as A$0.75.
- Debt: on the way, net debt falls from about A$422m to about A$373m at June 2027 (A$15m of free cash flow, less A$33m to AerCap, plus about A$67m of asset sales), which is 2.0 times. It reaches about A$343m at June 2028.

Cross-check on EBITDA: in the middling case the business would be valued at about A$165m plus A$343m, or 2.7 times FY28 EBITDA. That's still low for a contract airline that owns its fleet, so the middling value isn't stretched. Its limit is the cash, not the multiple.

The bad case's value comes from what a fresh raising costs, not from the business failing. At A$0.25, a A$35m raising adds about 140m shares. The worse case is a rescue on the lenders' terms. Outright wipe-out is unlikely while the jets are worth well over the debt (the asset sense check above). Rex's collapse in 2024 is the local warning, but Rex leased its jets, while Alliance owns its fleet.

Ranges used:
- worse A$0.05 to A$0.15 (taken as A$0.10);
- bad A$0.22 to A$0.32 (A$0.27);
- middling A$0.65 to A$0.90 (A$0.75);
- good A$1.10 to A$1.50 (A$1.30).

What each depends on most: whether a raising is needed (the bad cases), and FY28 free cash flow (the others). Each A$5m of free cash flow a year is worth about A$0.13 a share at 5.5 times.

## Why the market is wrong, and where it's right
1. It's reading the going-concern warning as a forecast. The condition in it was met in September. The warning may come back in February, because the ANZ loans fall due within 12 months, but that would be a refinancing question, not a solvency one, while the fleet is worth well over the debt.
2. It's treating the raising's new holders leaving as news. The selling came from sub-underwriters and new holders. Short positions are negligible, and the four big holders haven't sold. Two of them committed fresh money, and a third probably did.
3. It's ignoring the parts that work. FIFO revenue grew 7% on flat hours, and the biggest customer's mine is expanding.

Where it's right: cash conversion is poor, guidance has been missed, and a new chief executive often resets expectations at his first result. FY27's guidance came from his predecessor.

## Outcomes and chances
Resolve date: 31 August 2028, after the FY28 result (661 days away). No dividends are assumed in any case.

| Outcome | What happens | Chance | Price by resolve date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Worse | The turnaround fails: EBITDA falls towards A$150m, covenants are breached, ANZ won't refinance on acceptable terms, and a rescue raising goes through at about A$0.10 | 12% | A$0.10 |
| Bad | It stalls: FY27 misses at about A$165m, the asset sales slip, and the banks want equity before refinancing, so about A$35m is raised at about A$0.25 | 28% | A$0.27 |
| Middling | Guidance is roughly met, the asset sales complete in 2027, ANZ is refinanced, and FY28 free cash flow is about A$30m, valued at 5.5 times | 45% | A$0.75 |
| Good | Better than guidance: free cash flow of about A$45m, the jets sell above book, or Qantas or another buyer bids | 15% | A$1.30 |

These chances were set after the evidence from outside the filings. Healthy FIFO customers and a tight E190 market are why the worse case is only 12% for a company with a going-concern warning, and why the middling case is the likeliest. The unsold hangars, the poor retail take-up and the record on guidance are why the bad case stays at 28%.

For the card, which takes three outcomes, the first two combine into one bad case: 40% at A$0.22 (the chance-weighted average of A$0.10 and A$0.27). The card then reads 40% at A$0.22, 45% at A$0.75 and 15% at A$1.30.

Expected price: 0.40 × 0.219 + 0.45 × 0.75 + 0.15 × 1.30 = A$0.620, or +55% before costs.

Costs come to about 3.5%:
- A$15 brokerage each way on an order of about A$2,250 (1.3%);
- half the spread each way (the spread is A$0.395 to A$0.40, 1.25%);
- the 0.5% currency fee each way.

After costs the expected gain is about +51%. The rulebook minimum over 661 days is 12.9% (the 2.13% cash rate plus 5% a year), so that test passes easily.

The value test (rulebook section 5a):
- The bad case of A$0.22 is 45% below A$0.40, over the 30% limit.
- Against that fall the middling case would need to be +68%, and it's +87.5%, so that part passes.
- So the test fails, on the bad case alone.

It passes at about A$0.31. There the bad case is 29% below, and the middling case is +142%, far above the +44% it would need.

It isn't a special situation under section 5a: no approach, review or holder move is in play, so the value test applies.

## What would make this wrong
- The maintenance bill doesn't fall. Many of the ex-JetBlue E190s are 19 to 21 years old, and older jets need heavier checks. If maintenance stays near A$135m while EBITDA is flat, free cash flow stays near A$15m and the middling value falls to about A$0.40.
- Qantas doesn't renew in 2028, or shrinks the fleet further as its A220s and own E190s arrive.
- The new chief executive resets FY27 guidance at the February result.
- The banks want more equity before refinancing ANZ, which is the bad case.
- A FIFO customer is lost, or Network Aviation's E190s win WA contracts.

Exit rule, if it's ever bought: sell if another equity raising is announced below the buy price, if ANZ isn't refinanced by the FY27 result, or if FY27 underlying EBITDA is below A$165m. A close 25% below the buy price means a review, not a sale (rulebook section 5a).

In short: on the filings, about A$41m of the cash is spare once supplier and customer money is set aside (10.8 cents a share), not the A$82m the company's own "net cash" measure gives after the June dividend and the buyback. After the Virgin cut, the surcharge ban, less of FY26's unusual revenue and weaker trading, the business looks to earn about A$19.6m of underlying EBITDA and A$6m of underlying EBIT a year, against A$28.1m and A$15.6m in FY26. That puts it at about A$0.22 a share (range A$0.10 to A$0.50), below the A$0.275 price. The expected price on 3 March 2027 is A$0.273, 0.7% below the price; with an expected interim dividend of about 0.4c the return is about +0.8% before costs and about -2% to -4% after them, against a guide bar of about 9%. No card. What could lift the price inside the window is control (Helloworld at 20.5%, BGH at 13.6%), which makes this a special situation for the watch list, not a research pick.

## The company in plain words
Webjet Group is the consumer travel business demerged from Web Travel Group (then Webjet Limited) on 30 September 2024 and listed on 23 September 2024 [W1, W40]. Its year ends 31 March. It has three businesses [W1, note 4]:
- Webjet OTA: an online travel agency for Australians and New Zealanders, mostly domestic flights, with international flights, hotels, packages and add-ons. FY26 bookings 1.147m, total transaction value (TTV, the gross value of what customers book) A$1,241m, revenue A$115.3m, EBITDA A$38.7m [W2].
- Cars and Motorhomes (GoSee brands such as Motorhomes Republic): an online car and motorhome rental marketplace selling worldwide. TTV A$189m, revenue A$19.5m, EBITDA A$4.3m [W2].
- Webjet Business Travel: Locomote, a business travel booking platform bought for A$15.7m cash on 1 October 2025, plus A$1.5m retention and an earn-out of up to A$6.0m over three years [W1, notes 18 and 29]. Second-half TTV A$27m, revenue A$1.2m, EBITDA loss A$0.6m [W2].

Webjet acts as an agent: it books the trip, keeps a commission, fee or margin, and passes the rest to the airline, hotel or rental company. Revenue was 9.4% of TTV in FY26 (A$136.4m of A$1,457m [W2]). Customers pay Webjet up front and Webjet pays suppliers later (airlines through the IATA settlement cycle, rental companies after the hire), so it holds supplier money for a while: that float is the heart of the fact sheet's cash question.

Who runs it: Nicole Sheffield, Managing Director and CEO from 20 July 2026 (previously Wesfarmers OneDigital) [W25]; Dr Gary Weiss, director since 1 May 2026 and interim Chair since Don Clarke retired on 20 May 2026 [W1, note 31]. The previous CEO, Katrina Barry, resigned on 30 March 2026 and left on 31 May [W23, W24].

### The three named hits to FY27, put in numbers
The company says FY27 will be "materially impacted by lower airline commissions, alongside RBA surcharging regulation changes and lower variable revenue items" [W2], and repeated it at the AGM [W11].
- Virgin Australia: from 1 July 2026 Virgin "will substantially reduce its commission streams and commercial arrangements"; had it applied from the start of FY26 it would have cut FY26 revenue by about A$3.0m [W4]. That is 2.6% of OTA revenue but 10.7% of FY26 underlying EBITDA and 19% of underlying EBIT, since it comes straight off profit. FY27 carries nine months of it (about A$2.25m); a full year is A$3.0m.
- The surcharge ban: the Reserve Bank removed surcharging on eftpos, Mastercard and Visa debit, prepaid and credit cards from 1 October 2026, and cut the caps on what card issuers charge merchants to 0.3% for consumer credit and 8 cents plus 0.16% for debit from the same date [R1, R2]. Webjet says the removal "may limit the Group's ability to directly recover card acceptance costs, which could increase underlying transaction costs and impact margins" [W1, p. 30], and has not quantified it. Every 0.1% of OTA TTV that Webjet now absorbs costs about A$1.2m of EBITDA a year. I use A$3.0m a year (about 0.25% of OTA TTV) in the central case, A$1.5m to A$5.0m either side. None of it falls in the half to 30 September 2026.
- Lower variable revenue: not quantified by the company. I use A$3.0m a year (central), A$1.5m to A$4.5m.

What is spare, after the A$7.8m final dividend paid on 10 June 2026 [W1, note 10] and A$3.75m of buybacks since 31 March (A$3.96m in all [W13], less A$0.21m bought before the year end [W1, note 19]):
- The company's measure (cash at bank only): A$93.9m, now A$82.4m, 21.5 cents a share.
- Supplier and customer money set aside (cash at bank, less trade payables not covered by restricted cash, gift cards, deferred revenue, the retention and the accrued earn-out, plus receivables): A$52.8m, now A$41.3m, 10.8 cents a share. This is my central figure, and the one I think a buyer would use.
- Every current liability paid (net current assets): A$47.7m, now A$36.2m, 9.5 cents a share.

Why the central figure and not the company's: the float is money owed to suppliers and customers. It stays while bookings hold, but it shrinks when they fall (OTA bookings were down 11% to 18 August [W11]) and it reversed in COVID. The board itself keeps cash "to guard against any repeat of the Covid scenario" [W6]. BGH's A$0.80 proposal in May 2025 was "based on a number of key assumptions, including ... assumptions relating to cash levels" [W18], and both 2025 bidders walked away after 12 weeks of due diligence [W9]. Getting the cash out is also limited: the board sized the A$25m buyback to issued capital (now A$26.7m) because buying back more would create franking inefficiencies and possible franking deficit tax [W14, W1 note 19], and the franking account held A$1.1m at 31 March before the final dividend used A$3.4m [W1, note 10].

The half to 30 September 2026 is not yet in these numbers. Capital spending (about A$7m a half), tax and the float shrinking with TTV will have moved cash at bank; the result on 18 November shows by how much. On my central measure the float shrinking does not matter, since payables fall with it.

### What a quick reader would miss
- Underlying EBITDA includes the A$4.5m one-off marketing, but leaves out recurring costs: share-based pay, restructuring and advisory fees and the Locomote earn-out together cost A$8.3m in FY26 and A$7.4m in FY25 (excluding the ACCC settlement) [W2, W1 note 6.3].
- Software spending (A$16.3m) runs well ahead of its amortisation (A$11.0m), so EBIT will keep falling unless spending is cut [W1, note 13].
- The franking account is nearly empty [W1, note 10], so future fully franked dividends depend on tax paid on lower profits.
- The Cars and Motorhomes goodwill (A$16.0m) assumes EBITDA grows 19% a year in years two to five [W1, note 13].
- The ASX's 14.5% trailing yield is FY26's 4c, which was more than FY26's underlying earnings of 3.46c a share; it is not a guide to FY27 [W40, W2].

### Where this differs from the fact sheet
- Index: Webjet left the S&P/ASX 300 on 24 March 2025 [W41]; what it left on 21 September 2026 was the All Ordinaries [W42].
- Guidance: the first FY26 cut (to A$30m to A$32m) came on 13 November 2025 in the preliminary release [W7], not 19 November, and the May 2025 guidance was repeated on 22 August 2025 [W14]. The count is unchanged: set once, cut twice.

### Method 2: earnings including interest, at peer earnings multiples
Underlying profit after tax = (run-rate underlying EBIT + interest of A$2.8m, A$3.3m or A$3.8m) x 0.7.
- Central: (6.05 + 3.3) x 0.7 = A$6.55m. At Helloworld's 7.2 times, A$47.4m, A$0.124 a share; at Flight Centre's 11.8 times, A$77.1m, A$0.202.
- Good: A$12.18m. A$0.231 to A$0.375.
- Bad: a loss, so about nothing on this method.

### Method 3: owner cash, capitalised
Owner cash = run-rate EBITDA less capital spending (A$12m, A$14m or A$15m, cut back from A$16.7m), lease payments (A$1.1m) and tax, plus interest. At 10% with no growth:
- Central: 19.55 - 14.0 - 1.1 - 2.8 + 3.3 = A$4.95m a year, A$49.5m, A$0.129 a share.
- Good: A$9.58m, A$0.251. Bad: about minus A$1.0m, nothing.

Methods 2 and 3 count the cash only through the interest it earns, so they show what a holder gets if the cash is never handed back or used well. Method 1 is what a buyer of the whole company would pay.

## Why the market is wrong
On my numbers it isn't, at least not on the low side. At A$0.275 the market pays about A$23m for the business if all A$82m of cash at bank is counted, or about A$64m on my central cash figure, which is 10.6 times my central run-rate EBIT. My central value (A$0.22) is below the price, and the half-year result on 18 November is more likely to show the drop in earnings (about A$9m of EBITDA against A$14.4m a year earlier, on my estimate) than to show the market is wrong.

The price is too low only if someone pays for all the cash and the business together. That would take a control move by Helloworld or BGH, or a large capital return, which is a different kind of trade (below). As a research pick there is no trade.
